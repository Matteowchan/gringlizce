Grammar: Genre & Text-Type Conventions

At C2, being correct is not enough; you must sound like the right kind of text. This unit hands you the grammatical machinery that tells a report from a review, a news story from an academic paper — and teaches you to hold one genre without slipping out of it.

Genre is convention with a purpose

A genre (or text-type) is not just a topic; it is a bundle of conventions — grammatical, lexical and structural — that a community of readers expects for a given purpose. A report, a review, a news story, an academic article and a formal letter each carry their own markers: their own tense, person, degree of hedging and typical openings. You already command the raw grammar; what is new at C2 is choosing it consciously so that a text reads unmistakably as its type.

Every convention answers to purpose, audience and context. This overlaps with register (Unit 1), but is not the same: register is how formal you are, whereas genre is the whole set of expectations attached to a text-type. The C2 skill is threefold: to recognise genre markers, to sustain one genre without slipping, and to know when a deliberate hybrid serves the writer and when a mixed text merely confuses.

  • The school banned phones at lunch yesterday, students say.News report — freshest fact first, past tense, claim attributed to a source. The markers announce the genre.
  • The new rule appears to have attracted considerable student opposition.Academic article — hedged, impersonal, cautious. Same event, wholly different grammar.
  • The ban is a bold, clumsy, strangely well-meant gamble.Review — vivid present tense and evaluative adjectives; it judges rather than reports.
  • I would be grateful if you could reconsider the decision.Formal letter — indirect request, no contractions; the courtesy is part of the convention.

1. Genre markers: tense, person & stance (the fingerprints of type)

Every genre leaves fingerprints in its grammar. Three of the clearest are tense (which time-frame the genre favours), person (whether the writer appears as "I", "we" or vanishes into an impersonal voice) and stance (how boldly or cautiously a claim is made). Read these three, and you can usually name the text-type before you reach the content.

MarkerWhat it signalsExample
tensepast for news; present for reviews and findingsThe film opens in silence. (review)
personimpersonal for academic/report; "I" allowed in reviewsIt was found that… (report)
stancehedged in academia; assertive in journalismThe data suggest a link. (academic)
C2 subtlety
  • Markers work together: an impersonal voice, the passive and hedging all point the same way — academic. One marker alone can mislead.
  • A single wrong marker can betray the genre: a chatty "I reckon" in a formal report, or a hedged "it may perhaps be that" in a punchy news lead, feels instantly out of place.

2. The academic article & the report (impersonal, hedged, structured)

These two genres prize objectivity. The academic article defines its terms, hedges its claims and hides the author behind an impersonal, often nominalised voice. The report shares that restraint but adds a fixed architecture — headings, findings, and a recommendations section — and leans on the passive to foreground the data rather than the writer.

MarkerAcademic articleReport
voiceimpersonal, nominalised: an analysis of…passive: it was observed that…
stancehedged: the results may suggest…measured: the figures indicate…
shapeclaim → evidence → cautious conclusionheadings → findings → recommendations
C2 subtlety
  • Hedging is required, not weak: "X causes Y" overclaims; "X appears to contribute to Y" is the scholarly default.
  • A report ends in recommendations, an essay in argument: mixing the two — bullet-point advice inside a flowing essay — breaks both genres.

3. The news report & the review (reporting vs judging)

Two public genres, two opposite jobs. The news report tells you what happened: freshest fact first (the "inverted pyramid"), past tense, third person, and every claim attributed to a source. The review tells you what it was like: the narrative present, frank evaluative adjectives, and a licensed first person that weighs strengths against flaws.

MarkerNews reportReview
tensepast: the student council voted…present: the novel follows…
attributionaccording to teachers; students saidthe writer's own judgement is expected
languageneutral, factual, conciseevaluative: gripping, uneven, luminous
C2 subtlety
  • A news report keeps its opinions in quotation marks: unattributed judgement ("the shameful decision") turns reporting into editorialising.
  • A review may say "I", but it must still argue its verdict; "I liked it" is not evaluation until reasons follow.

4. The formal letter & genre consistency (fixed courtesies, held throughout)

The formal letter is the most formulaic genre of all: fixed openings and closings, no contractions, indirect requests, and a clear statement of purpose. Its conventions are so settled that any lapse is instantly felt. This makes it the perfect place to see genre consistency — the discipline of keeping one text-type's markers steady from first line to last.

ElementConventionExample
openingnamed → Dear Mr Kaya; unnamed → Dear Sir or MadamDear Sir or Madam,
requestindirect and softened, never bluntI would be grateful if you could…
closingnamed → Yours sincerely; unnamed → Yours faithfullyYours faithfully,
C2 subtlety
  • Match the pair: "Dear Sir or Madam" closes with "Yours faithfully"; a named "Dear Ms Lee" closes with "Yours sincerely". Crossing them is a classic slip.
  • Consistency is whole-text: one contraction ("don't") or one breezy aside ("anyway") can drop the register and break the reader's trust in the genre.

5. Genre mixing & reader expectations (deliberate hybrid vs accidental slip)

Genres set up expectations, and a writer can either meet them or bend them on purpose. A deliberate hybrid — feature journalism marrying a storyteller's colour to a reporter's sourcing — is a controlled effect. An accidental slip — a report that suddenly exclaims, a formal letter that ends "cheers" — simply breaks the contract and unsettles the reader. The difference is control.

MoveDeliberate hybridAccidental slip
effectfresh, purposeful, still coherentjarring; the reader's trust drops
examplea review that opens like a short storyan academic paper that turns chatty
reader feels"this writer is in command""this writer lost the thread"
C2 subtlety
  • Blending is not slipping: a hybrid knows which conventions it is bending and why; a slip is unaware it has broken anything.
  • Expectations shift with audience and platform: what reads as a bold hybrid in a magazine feature may read as an error in an exam essay.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the genre faults that surface even in fluent C2 writing. Each is a failure of consistency — a marker borrowed from the wrong text-type. Audit your drafts against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
The results were, like, totally surprising to us. (report)The results were unexpected.informal, personal voice imported into an impersonal genre.
Dear Sir or Madam, … Anyway, cheers!Dear Sir or Madam, … Yours faithfully,a chatty sign-off breaks the letter's fixed courtesies.

The film is good. It follows a man. It was released. (review)The film is a quietly devastating portrait of a man.flat, factual news style where a review needs evaluation.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

A junior writer drafts a passage; an editor names the genre it has drifted into and pulls it back. Read it twice and watch how each note fixes a marker — and where the editor says a deliberate blend is fine.

J:For the lab report I wrote: "I was honestly amazed — the temperature just shot up!"The voice has slipped: exclamation and a personal "I" belong to chat, not a report.
E:Make it impersonal: "A sharp rise in temperature was observed." Same fact, correct genre.Passive, no exclamation — the marker now matches the text-type.
J:And the discussion? I put: "This proves the theory is right."Too bold for academic stance — it overclaims.
E:Hedge it: "These findings appear to support the theory." Caution is the genre's default.Hedging is not weakness here; it is the scholarly convention.
J:For the magazine version, can I open with a little scene before the facts?That is genre mixing — is it allowed?
E:Yes — in a feature, a narrative opening before the reporting is a deliberate hybrid. Just don't do it inside the lab report.Blending on purpose is fine; slipping by accident is not.
Test yourself
  • Why did "I was honestly amazed — it just shot up!" fail as a report? (It imports a personal, exclamatory voice into a genre that expects impersonal restraint.)
  • Why is "appears to support" better than "proves" in an academic discussion? (Academic stance is hedged; "proves" overclaims, while "appears to support" keeps the required caution.)
  • When is opening with a scene acceptable, and when is it not? (Acceptable as a deliberate hybrid in a feature; unacceptable as an accidental slip inside a formal report.)

Speaking: The Language of Genre

Learn the vocabulary of text-types, then use it to compare how different genres would handle the very same story.

genre (n)
a category of text defined by shared conventions (tür)
Syn: text-type · Ant: formless writing
"Each genre keeps its own rules of tense and voice."
convention (n)
an accepted, expected way of doing something (kural, teamül)
Syn: norm · Ant: innovation
"Ignoring a genre's conventions confuses the reader."
text-type (n)
a class of text with typical, shared features (metin türü)
Syn: genre · Ant: free-form note
"A report is a very different text-type from a review."
register (n)
the level of formality suited to a situation (dil düzeyi, register)

"The register slipped when he wrote 'gonna' in the essay."

nominalisation (n)

attribution (n)

impersonal (adj)

evaluative (adj)

formulaic (adj)

hybrid (n)

Syn: blend · Ant: pure form

concise (adj)
brief but complete; saying much in few words (özlü, veciz)
Syn: succinct · Ant: long-winded
"News leads must be concise: the key fact first."

"A genre's conventions are shaped by its readership."

Production Task (Üretim Görevi)

Take one small event (a local shop closing, a match result, a new film) and describe, out loud, how three different genres would tell it. Write 8 sentences and use at least 8 of the words above. Make each genre's markers visible and deliberate.

Style requirement — apply this unit's topic
  • Give a news-report version (past tense, freshest fact first, one attribution) and name the markers you used.
  • Give an academic version (impersonal, hedged, one nominalisation) and a review version (present tense, one evaluative adjective).
  • Point out one convention that changes across the three, and why the readership expects it.
  • End on the effect: which genre suited the event best, and what a careless slip between them would do to the reader.

Sentence starters (you can begin with these):

"As a news report, it would run: ... , sources said." "An academic version would hedge: it appears that ..." "A review would judge it in the present: the ... is ..." "The marker that changes most is ... , because ..." "The genre that suited the event best was ... because ..."

Reading: The Unwritten Contract

Read the feature, then work through the five tasks.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Skilled readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think of the last news story, review or formal email you read. What told you, within a line or two, which kind of text it was?
  • The text calls a genre "an unwritten contract". What does that metaphor promise — a set of fixed rules, or a shared understanding between writer and reader?
  • Writers often show a genre's markers while explaining them. Watch for the moment the text puts the same event into three different grammars.
How to read
  • First read: read quickly for the whole argument — what a genre promises its reader, and why the writer calls those promises a "contract". Don't stop for every word.
  • Second read: track the markers as they appear — tense, person, hedging, attribution — and note the difference the text draws between deliberate blending and an accidental slip.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): genre · convention · marker · hedging · attribution · hybrid

Genres form a spectrum of convention: from the impersonal, hedged academic article to the personal, evaluative review.
The Unwritten Contract

Every piece of writing signs a silent contract with its reader. Before a single argument is weighed, the shape of the text — its genre, or text-type — has already made promises. A news report vows to put the freshest fact first and to attribute every claim; an academic article promises caution, defined terms and an impersonal voice; a formal letter offers its fixed courtesies, "Dear Sir or Madam" answered in the end by "Yours faithfully". These conventions are not decoration. They are the grammar of expectation.

Consider how differently each genre handles the same event. Where a news report states, "The school banned phones at lunch yesterday, students say," an academic paper hedges: "The new rule appears to have attracted considerable student opposition." A review, meanwhile, cares less for the fact than for the flavour, judging in the vivid present: "the ban is a bold, clumsy, strangely well-meant gamble." Same event; three grammars; three readerships. Tense, person, and the presence or absence of hedging are not free choices — they are the fingerprints of type.

Genres can, of course, be blended. Feature journalism deliberately marries the storyteller's colour to the reporter's sourcing, and the hybrid is no accident but a considered effect. Yet blending is not the same as slipping. A report that suddenly exclaims, or a formal letter that signs off "anyway, cheers", has not innovated; it has simply broken its promise, and the jarred reader trusts it less.

The skilled writer, then, reads the contract before drafting a line. Admittedly, conventions shift across cultures and decades; nonetheless, at any given moment a genre carries expectations a writer ignores at their peril. To master a text-type is not to memorise a template but to know, precisely, which promise each choice is quietly making.

D · Open-Ended

Write your answer in your own words. These questions are not marked automatically.

1. The writer calls a genre "an unwritten contract" that makes "promises". In your own words, what does that metaphor capture — and why does it matter for a writer?

Sample Answer"To call a genre a contract is to say that, before a word of content is read, the text-type has already promised the reader a certain tense, voice and level of caution. The reader 'signs' by expecting those markers. It matters because a writer who breaks the promise — a chatty report, a hedge-free academic claim — loses the reader's trust, even if every fact is correct. Knowing the contract lets a writer meet or bend it on purpose."

2. The text puts the same event into three grammars (news, academic, review). What is being demonstrated as well as stated?

Sample Answer"The writer is proving the point by showing it: the same phone ban becomes an attributed past-tense report, a hedged academic sentence and a vivid present-tense review. This demonstrates that genre lives in grammar, not content — tense, person and hedging change while the fact stays fixed. The reader sees, rather than is merely told, that markers are 'the fingerprints of type'."

3. The text distinguishes a deliberate "hybrid" from an accidental "slip". Do you agree the difference matters? Give a reason from your own reading.

Sample Answer"I agree. I once read a magazine feature that opened like a short story before turning to hard reporting; it felt fresh and controlled — a deliberate hybrid. But I also read a student email that began 'Dear Professor' and ended 'catch you later', and it read as careless, not creative. The same mixing of registers felt confident in one case and like a mistake in the other, because one was in command of its conventions and the other simply lost track."

Speaking Task 2 (Fix the Genre)

Below is a paragraph that has slipped out of its genre: it is meant to be a formal report for the student council, but it reads like a chatty text message. Your job is to rewrite it into a proper report, correcting every marker — without losing a single fact.

So basically the new homework app is a bit of a disaster lol. Loads of students are complaining and honestly I'm not surprised?! Hardly anyone logged in last month — who'd have thought? We reckon we should maybe scrap the whole thing, or something. Anyway, let me know what you think, cheers.
Style requirement — apply this unit's topic
  • Fix the voice: make it impersonal (use the passive where useful) and remove every contraction and slang.
  • Fix the stance: replace bold or emotional claims with measured, lightly hedged statements.
  • Add the missing markers: a neutral heading and a clear recommendations line, phrased soberly.
  • Write 6–8 sentences. Keep every fact; change only the genre — the result must read unmistakably as a report.

Sentence starters (you can begin with these):

"Findings: A significant number of complaints were received from students ..." "Login figures indicate a marked decline over the past month." "The data appear to suggest that ..." "It is recommended that the application be ..." "Further review of ... would be advisable."

Writing: A Short Persuasive Speech

Write a short, rousing speech that argues one clear case — weaving the devices into a real argument. Your teacher will review it.

The rhetorical toolkit (Devices at a Glance)

At C1 you learned to link and organise ideas; at C2 you learn to persuade with them. Each device below is a pattern of structure. The art is not using them all, but weaving two or three into a real argument, in a register the moment can bear.

DeviceWhat it doesExample to model
tricolongroups three parallel parts, often risingYour voice, your vote, your future.
antithesisbalances two opposites for contrastNot for the few, but for the many.
anaphorarepeats an opening to build momentumWe can act, we can lead, we can win.
rhetorical questionasks what needs no answer, to pull the reader inHow long must we wait for change?
concession-refutationadmits a point, then overturns itAdmittedly it is hard; yet it is right.
inclusive "we"folds the reader into a shared causeTogether, we decide what happens next.
Rule
  • Weave the devices into a real argument — they must serve the case, never replace it.
  • Use at least three different devices, but never a device on every line: restraint is the C2 mark. Empty rhetoric loses the reader.

How to structure the speech (Persuasive Structure)

A persuasive speech has a shape: a hook that grips, a body that argues and concedes, and a close that rouses. The devices belong at the joints — the opening question, the tricolon in the body, the anaphora at the end.

PartWhat to writeDevice to reach for
A. Hookgrab attention; name the stakesrhetorical question, inclusive "we"
B. Bodyargue the case; answer the other sidetricolon, antithesis, concession-refutation
C. Closea rousing call to actionanaphora rising to a climax, then stop

Task (Görev)

Choose one motion you believe in — save the school library, ban single-use plastic in the canteen, keep the late school bus running, make the town centre car-free. Write a short persuasive speech of about 200–240 words that a real audience could hear. Prove you can weave the devices into an argument — and know when to stop.

WHAT TO INCLUDE

  • a hook using a rhetorical question and inclusive "we"
  • at least one tricolon (three parallel parts) and one antithesis (not X, but Y)
  • a genuine concession-refutation (Admittedly… yet / nonetheless…)
  • a rousing close using anaphora that builds to a climax
  • a consistent register throughout — rousing but not hollow; no empty, purple padding
  • at least 4 words from the Speaking section (rhetoric, persuasion, tricolon, antithesis, anaphora, climax, concession, refutation, cadence, eloquent, emphatic, rousing)

EXAMPLE OPENERS

"Ask yourselves: what kind of town do we want to be?" "This is not about ..., but about ..." "Let us not ..., let us not ..., let us not ..."

Sample Answer (~220 words) Friends, neighbours, fellow readers — tonight our library is under threat, and tonight we must decide what kind of town we wish to be.

Ask yourselves this: what is a town without a place to learn? A library is not merely a building of shelves and silence. It is a door for the curious, a refuge for the lonely, and a ladder for the poor. This tricolon is no accident: three plain images, rising, carry more persuasion than any statistic. Admittedly, the budget is tight; nonetheless, some things are too precious to price. Close these doors, and we do not save money — we spend our future.

Some will say the age of print is over. I say the age of ideas has only begun. Where others see old paper, I see young minds; where they see a cost, I see a promise. That antithesis is the whole argument in a single breath.

So let us not falter, let us not flinch, let us not fail. Sign the petition, raise your voice, and stand with your neighbours tonight. This is not a plea for a building, but a call for who we are. Save our library — and, in saving it, save the best of ourselves. The rousing close earns its cadence because the case beneath it is true.
