Ünite 7 · Cleft & Emphatic Structures · Bölüm 1/7

Grammar: Cleft & Emphatic Structures

Stop stating facts flatly. Start steering the reader's eye to the exact word that matters.

Every sentence has a spotlight

A neutral sentence — Sarah called the police — treats every word as equally important. But skilled writers rarely want that. They want to aim a spotlight at one element: who did it, what they did, or when it finally happened. The grammar that does this is the family of cleft and emphatic structures. A cleft literally splits (“cleaves”) one clause into two, so that the part you care about is framed and foregrounded.

This unit treats emphasis as information management. You will learn to front an element with the it-cleft (It was Sarah who called), to reorganise a whole clause with the wh-/pseudo-cleft (What I need is time), to restrict focus with the all-cleft (All I want is peace), to insist on the truth of a verb with emphatic do (I do believe you), and to front a phrase for contrast (That, I can't accept). Used with control, these choices make writing sound assertive, precise and unmistakably C1.

  • It was Sarah who called the police, not her neighbour.It-cleft → the spotlight falls on the person responsible.
  • What the team needs is a clear deadline.Wh-/pseudo-cleft → reorganises the clause to foreground the need.
  • All I want is a quiet weekend.All-cleft → restricts focus: this and nothing else.
  • You doubt me, but I did lock the door.Emphatic did → asserts the truth of the verb against doubt.

1. The it-cleft (It was X who/that...)

The it-cleft takes a plain clause and lifts one element into focus: It + be + (focus) + who/that + rest. The “dummy” it carries no meaning; its only job is to build the frame. You can spotlight almost anything — a subject, an object, a time, a place — and the contrast is often implied: this one, not that one.

FocusFrameExample
a person (subject)It + be + person + whoIt was the caretaker who raised the alarm.
a thing (object)It + be + thing + thatIt was her honesty that won us over.
a time / a delayIt + wasn't + until... + thatIt wasn't until dawn that the truth emerged.
Register & nuance
  • Use who for people, that for things — but that is acceptable for people too: “It was Sarah that called.” ✓
  • The verb be agrees with the frame, not the focus: “It was the students who complained” ✓ — not “It were the students...” ✗

2. The wh-/pseudo-cleft (What I need is...)

The wh-cleft (also called the pseudo-cleft) reorganises a clause around a what-clause and the verb be: What + [clause] + is/was + focus. Here what means “the thing that”. It is a favourite of essays and presentations because it builds suspense — the reader waits across the whole subject to reach the point.

PatternWhat it doesExample
What + S + V + is/was...foregrounds an object or ideaWhat impresses me is her calm under pressure.
What happened was (that)...frames an event or explanationWhat happened was that the system crashed.
What + S + do + is + base Vforegrounds an actionWhat she did was (to) resign on the spot.
C1 subtlety — get the verb right
  • The linking verb agrees with the focus: “What I need is time” (singular); “What I need are answers” (plural).
  • Don't use a continuous form after the what-clause: “What I want is a break” ✓ — not “What I am wanting...” ✗

3. The all-cleft & other clefts (All I want is...)

The all-cleft is a tighter cousin of the wh-cleft. Replacing what with all adds a note of restriction — “this, and nothing more”: All (that) + S + V + is + focus. English also clefts with the (only) thing, the place, the reason and so on, giving you a whole toolkit for aiming the spotlight.

CleftNuanceExample
All (that) I want is...restriction: only this, nothing elseAll I want is a straight answer.
The reason (why)... is that...foregrounds a causeThe reason he left was that nobody listened.
The one thing... is...singles out a decisive itemThe one thing they forgot was the passports.
C1 subtlety
  • The relative that is usually dropped: “All I want...” sounds more natural than “All that I want...”.
  • After the reason, prefer that, not because, in careful writing: “The reason is that...” ✓ — “The reason is because...” ✗

4. Emphatic do / does / did (I do believe you)

When there is no other auxiliary, you can add do, does or did before the base verb to insist on its truth — typically against doubt, denial or contrast. The stress falls on the auxiliary in speech; in writing, the emphasis is understood. The golden rule: do/does/did + BASE verb — never inflect the main verb twice.

FormUseExample
do + basepresent, insisting against doubtI do understand your concern.
does + basepresent 3rd person, contrastShe rarely praises, but she does value effort.
did + basepast, correcting a denialI did send the invoice — check again.
C1 subtlety — the base-verb trap
  • Keep the main verb in its base form: “She does value effort” ✓ — not “does values” ✗ or “did valued” ✗.
  • Emphatic do also energises imperatives: “Do sit down.” — warm, insistent, very natural.

5. Fronting for emphasis (That, I can't accept)

Fronting moves a normally-later element — an object, a complement, an adverbial — to the front of the clause for emphasis or contrast, while the rest of the sentence keeps its usual order. It is a subtler tool than the cleft: no extra words, just a bold reordering that throws weight onto the fronted phrase.

Fronted elementEffectExample
objectstrong contrast with what came beforeThat I simply cannot accept.
complementvivid, literary emphasisBrilliant the plan may be, but it is risky.
adverbialsets a dramatic scene or conditionOnly later did I grasp what she meant.
C1 subtlety — when to invert
  • Plain object-fronting keeps normal order: “That, I can't accept.” ✓ — no inversion.
  • But a fronted negative/restrictive adverbial forces inversion: “Only later did I grasp it” ✓ — not “Only later I grasped it” ✗. (You met this in Unit 4.)

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors that still surface in otherwise strong C1 writing. Each one is a focus slip, not just a form slip. Audit your own emphatic sentences against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
It were the neighbours who complained.It was the neighbours who complained.the cleft be agrees with the frame (it), so it stays singular.
It was the noise who woke me.It was the noise that woke me.use that/which for a thing; who needs a person.
What I am needing is more time.What I need is more time.the wh-clause uses a simple, not a continuous, verb.
She does values her privacy.She does value her privacy.emphatic does takes the base verb, never the -s form.
The reason he stayed is because he was afraid.The reason he stayed is that he was afraid.after the reason...is, use that, not because.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two colleagues argue about who deserves the credit for a rescued project. Read it twice and watch the emphatic structures work as a system: an it-cleft to name the real hero, a wh-cleft to reframe the point, an all-cleft to restrict it, an emphatic did to counter a denial, and a fronted phrase for a final flourish.

A:Everyone's praising the manager, but it was Devrim who actually saved the launch.It-cleft → the spotlight falls on the true agent.
B:Come on — she barely touched the code.A denial, setting up the emphasis to come.
A:She did rewrite the whole payment module overnight; I watched her do it.Emphatic did → asserts the truth against the denial.
B:Fine. But what the client remembers is a smooth demo, not who was awake at 3 a.m.Wh-cleft → reframes the point around the client's view.
A:All I'm asking is that we name her in the report.All-cleft → restricts the request: only this.
B:That, I can agree to.Fronting → the fronted object throws weight onto the concession.
Test yourself
  • Why does A open with “It was Devrim who...” rather than “Devrim saved the launch”? (The cleft sets up a contrast: Devrim, not the manager.)
  • What does “She did rewrite” add that “She rewrote” would not? (It insists on the truth of the verb, answering B's denial.)
  • Rewrite B's last line without fronting. Is it weaker? (“I can agree to that” — correct, but the emphasis on that is lost.)

Reading: The Clerk Who Cracked the Cipher

Read the feature, then work through the five tasks.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Skilled readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think of a famous achievement usually credited to one person or one machine. Might someone quieter have done the real work?
  • The text argues that the official story got the hero wrong. Which structure might a writer use to correct it — a plain sentence, or a cleft like “It was X, not Y, who...”?
  • Writers use emphatic do/did to push back against a doubt. Watch for the moment it happens, and ask what claim it is defending.
How to read
  • First read: read quickly for the whole arc — who really cracked the cipher and why the official story is wrong. Don't stop for every word.
  • Second read: track the emphasis. Notice each cleft (“It was she who...”, “What made her exceptional...”, “All she had was...”) and each emphatic did. Ask what the writer is foregrounding in every case.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): crux · pinpoint · salient · assert · compelling · paramount · foreground · singular

History credited the machine — but it was a clerk with a pencil who spotted what no device could.
The Clerk Who Cracked the Cipher

For decades, the official history had credited a brilliant cipher machine with winning the secret war. What that tidy story overlooked, however, was a quiet clerk named Iris Bellamy. It was she, working alone in a damp basement, who first noticed the flaw that thousands of engineers had somehow missed. The machine has been celebrated ever since; the woman has been all but forgotten.

To understand how such an oversight could occur, one must first picture the institution that employed her. It was a sprawling wartime establishment, hastily assembled and jealous of its own reputation, where prestige flowed almost exclusively towards those who built and tended the celebrated machines. Bellamy had arrived there by an unremarkable route: a provincial grammar school, a spell as a bookkeeper, and a facility with columns of figures that no one thought to value. Assigned to transcription rather than analysis, she occupied the lowest rung of a rigid hierarchy. Yet it was precisely this marginal position, overlooked and undisturbed, that afforded her the one luxury her superiors so conspicuously lacked: uninterrupted time to think, and the freedom to be curious about problems that were not, strictly speaking, hers to solve.

What made Bellamy so singular was not raw brilliance but relentless patience. She did read every intercepted message twice, underlining the salient repetitions that her colleagues dismissed as meaningless noise. All she had was a pencil, a ledger and an almost unreasonable stubbornness. Where others sought a grand mathematical key, she simply watched, and waited, and counted.

Her method, if it deserved so grand a name, was closer to natural history than to mathematics. Day after day she compiled her own private catalogues, tabulating how often particular letter-groups surfaced and where in a message they tended to cluster. She trusted frequencies where her colleagues trusted formulae, and she was untroubled by the tedium that drove sharper minds to distraction. Patterns that appeared to others as random static resolved, under her sustained attention, into something suspiciously regular. She could not yet explain what she was seeing, but she had learned to distinguish genuine coincidence from the faint, recurring signature of human habit. Long before she could prove anything, she was already convinced that the enemy's operators, like all people, were creatures of routine.

That conviction earned her little but polite condescension. The prevailing orthodoxy held that any weakness worth finding would be mathematical in nature, a matter of deducing the machine's internal wiring through sheer analytical force. To suggest instead that the vulnerability lay in the fallible people operating the device struck her supervisors as naive, even faintly impertinent. She was, after all, a transcriber; theory was the preserve of the men upstairs. And so her ledgers accumulated in obscurity, consulted by no one, while the official effort poured its considerable ingenuity into ever more elaborate calculations. What none of them registered was that Bellamy had quietly reframed the entire problem, shifting the question from the impregnable machine to the careless hands that fed it.

The crux came on a wet Tuesday in 1943. It was a single repeated greeting — three trivial words that opened almost every message — that finally pinpointed the machine's weakness. What Bellamy grasped in that moment was paramount: it was the enemy's own lazy habits, not any flaw in the mathematics, that had betrayed them. The realisation was compelling precisely because it was so ordinary.

The greeting itself was almost comically banal — a stock salutation that discipline should long since have banned. Because it appeared, unvarying, at the head of message after message, it furnished exactly the foothold that pure cryptanalysis had been denied: a stretch of known text against which the machine's daily settings could be measured. Where a wholly random opening would have yielded nothing, this small ritual of politeness betrayed the configuration behind it. Bellamy had, in effect, found a door that the enemy themselves had propped open every morning out of sheer force of habit. The mathematics that had resisted the engineers for so long did not need to be defeated; it needed only to be sidestepped, once someone had noticed where the operators were reliably predictable.

Her superiors, predictably, were slow to listen. Indeed, it was months before anyone in authority did take her claim seriously. When they finally tested it, though, the result was undeniable: messages that had resisted the finest minds for years now unravelled in a matter of hours. What had changed was not the machine, but the question being asked of it.

Why, then, did the machine inherit the glory? Partly the answer is bureaucratic. Credit, in large organisations, tends to settle upon whatever is expensive, visible and defensible in a report, and a gleaming apparatus satisfied all three conditions in a way that a clerk's dog-eared ledger never could. Partly, too, it was a matter of secrecy: the details of the breakthrough were buried for a generation, and by the time the files were opened, the convenient version had already hardened into fact. Bellamy herself, characteristically, made no effort to correct the record. She returned after the war to an ordinary life, spoke of her work to almost no one, and seemed to regard the whole episode as a puzzle she had happened to enjoy rather than a triumph she was owed.

There is a broader lesson lurking in this quiet injustice, and it has little to do with wartime secrecy. Institutions, like the histories they eventually authorise, prefer explanations that flatter their own design; they would rather celebrate a clever instrument than concede that everything turned on the overlooked diligence of a single overlooked person. The story of the machine is orderly and reassuring, whereas the story of the clerk is inconvenient, for it suggests that decisive insight can come from precisely the quarter a hierarchy has trained itself to ignore. To remember Bellamy, therefore, is not merely to correct a footnote. It is to unsettle a comfortable assumption about where intelligence resides and whose attention we are prepared to value.

What history has since asserted is a neater tale, one that foregrounds the machine and quietly forgets the clerk. Yet the deeper truth is stranger and more human. It was not the technology that turned the war; it was a woman with a pencil who did what no device ever could — she paid attention, patiently, when everyone else looked away.

Writing: An Argumentative Essay

Write and submit a controlled academic argument; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound C1 (Linking Words)

At B2 you linked ideas with however, therefore, although, whereas. At C1 you gain a sharper, more formal register with connectors such as moreover, consequently, notwithstanding, whereas and nonetheless. Paired with cleft structures, they let you both connect ideas and foreground the one that matters. Used precisely — and sparingly — they signal a writer in full command of tone.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
moreoveradd a stronger, further pointThe plan is costly. Moreover, it is what nobody actually asked for.
consequentlystate a formal result or effectTrust collapsed; consequently, it was the whole board that resigned.
notwithstandingconcede “in spite of” (very formal)Notwithstanding the doubts, it was she who pressed on.
whereasdraw a direct contrast between two ideasThe machine is praised, whereas the clerk is forgotten.
nonethelesssay “even so”, after a concessionFew believed her; nonetheless, she did prove her case.
howevercontrast between two sentencesThe story seemed settled. However, one detail changed everything.

See the difference — the same idea, written two ways:

Flat argument ✗Controlled C1 argument ✓
Effort matters more than talent. Talented people often fail. Hard workers often succeed. This is the important point. What ultimately decides success is not talent but effort. Whereas gifted people frequently drift, it is the patient worker who tends to prevail; moreover, effort is the one factor we can actually control. That, surely, is the crux.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different C1 connectors from the table. Weave your argument; don't list sentences.
  • Moreover, Consequently, However, Nonetheless usually open a sentence and take a comma: “Consequently, the plan was abandoned.”

How to structure an argument (Essay Structure)

A strong C1 argument is not a flat list of opinions. It manages emphasis deliberately: it states a thesis, foregrounds the decisive point, answers an objection, and closes with force. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the shape:

PartWhat to writeStructure to reach for
1. Thesisstate your position and the real pointwh-cleft (What really matters is...)
2. Main argumentforeground the decisive cause or agentit-cleft (It is X, not Y, that...)
3. Counter & replyconcede an objection, then insistconcession + emphatic do/did
4. Conclusionrestrict the claim and land itall-cleft + fronting (All that remains is...; That, we must accept.)

Sample Answer (~205 words) What ultimately decides real success is not raw talent but sustained effort. Talent may open a door, yet it is discipline, not brilliance, that carries a person through it. This, to my mind, is the crux of the whole debate.

History offers a compelling illustration. It is rarely the most gifted who leave the deepest mark; more often, it is the patient, the stubborn and the endlessly curious. Whereas natural ability tends to fade without work, effort compounds quietly over years. Moreover, effort is the one factor we can actually control, which makes it, for most of us, paramount.

Talent does help, of course; I would not assert otherwise. A genuine gift can accelerate progress and make hard work feel lighter. Notwithstanding this, talent left unused achieves nothing, whereas ordinary ability, applied with deliberate persistence, achieves a great deal.

What the evidence consistently underscores is a single, salient truth. All that separates those who succeed from those who merely dream is the willingness to keep going when the excitement fades. Talent, we may admire; effort, we must respect. That, in the end, is the point I would defend.
