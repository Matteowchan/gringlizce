Grammar: Relative Clauses

Study the rules, look at the examples, then do the exercises.

Joining two ideas into one sentence

A relative clause gives more information about a noun (a person, thing or place). Instead of writing two short sentences, we join them with a relative pronoun: who, which, that, whose, where or when. This is one of the most useful skills at B1, because it makes your speaking and writing sound smooth and grown-up.

Look at how two sentences become one:

  • I know a woman. She speaks five languages.Two short sentences.
  • I know a woman who speaks five languages.One smooth sentence → relative clause with "who".
  • This is the park. We first met here.Two short sentences.
  • This is the park where we first met.One sentence → relative clause with "where".

1. Choosing the relative pronoun (who / which / that / whose / where / when)

The pronoun you choose depends on the kind of noun you are describing. Learn this table well — it answers most exam questions.

PronounUse it for...Example
whopeopleThe man who lives next door is a doctor.
whichthings / animalsThis is the phone which takes great photos.
thatpeople or things (informal)The book that won the prize is here.
whosepossession (belonging)She's the artist whose paintings are famous.
whereplacesThat's the town where I was born.
whentimesI'll never forget the day when we met.
Key point
  • that can replace who or which in a defining clause: The book which / that won... Both are correct.
  • Use whose to show that something belongs to the noun: the student whose bag was stolen (= the student's bag).

2. Defining relative clauses (essential information)

A defining relative clause tells you which person or thing we mean. Without it, the sentence does not make full sense. We do NOT use commas.

  • The people who live upstairs are very friendly.Which people? The ones upstairs → essential, no commas.
  • The film that we watched last night was boring.Which film? The one we watched → essential.
Test
  • Cover the relative clause. If the sentence loses its meaning ("The people ... are very friendly" — which people?), it is defining → no commas.

3. Non-defining relative clauses (extra information)

A non-defining relative clause adds extra information that we could remove. The sentence still makes sense without it. We put it between commas, and we do NOT use that.

  • My brother, who lives in Paris, is a chef.We already know who my brother is; the clause is extra → commas, use "who" (not "that").
  • Istanbul, which has millions of people, is a huge city.Extra fact about Istanbul → commas, "which".
Golden rule
  • Non-defining clause = commas + who / which / whose / where. Never use "that" in a non-defining clause. My car, which is red, ... ✓ — My car, that is red, ... ✗
  • Defining clause = no commas, and that is allowed.

4. Defining vs non-defining (the comma changes the meaning)

The two commas are not decoration — they can change what the sentence means. Compare:

SentenceMeaning
My sister who lives in Berlin is a nurse.I have more than one sister; I mean the Berlin one. (defining, no commas)
My sister, who lives in Berlin, is a nurse.I have only one sister; "in Berlin" is just extra news. (non-defining, commas)

5. Leaving out the relative pronoun (the object relative)

In a defining clause, if the relative pronoun is the object of the verb, we can leave it out. This is very common in natural English.

  • The book (that) I read yesterday was great."I read the book" → the pronoun is the object → we can drop it.
  • The woman (who) we met at the party is a writer."We met the woman" → object → can be dropped.
  • The man who lives here is friendly. ✗ (cannot drop)Here "who" is the subject of "lives" → you must keep it.
Quick test
  • Is there another subject right after the pronoun (the book that I read)? Then the pronoun is the object → you can drop it.
  • Is a verb right after the pronoun (the man who lives)? Then it is the subject → you must keep it.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B1 students make most often with relative clauses. Check your own writing against this list.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
My mother, that is a teacher, is kind.My mother, who is a teacher, is kind.non-defining clause → never use "that".
My laptop which is old still works.My laptop, which is old, still works.extra information → needs two commas.
She's the girl who's dog won.She's the girl whose dog won."whose" = possession; "who's" = who is.
This is the house where I grew up in it.This is the house where I grew up."where" already means "in that place"; no extra "in it".
The person which called you is here.The person who called you is here.use "who" (or "that") for people, not "which".

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two friends look at old photos and describe the people and places in them. Read it twice and notice each relative clause — which are defining (no commas) and which are non-defining (commas)?

  • — Who's the man who is standing next to you?defining "who" (which man?).
  • — That's Kaan, whose sister used to be in my class.non-defining "whose" (extra info, commas).
  • — And this is the house where we spent every summer."where" for a place.
  • — Nice! Is that the beach (that) you always talk about?object relative → "that" can be dropped.
  • — Yes! It's the place where I learned to swim, in the summer when I turned ten."where" for place, "when" for time.
  • — Your grandmother, who looks so happy here, must have loved it too.non-defining "who" (commas).
Test yourself
  • Find one clause you could leave the pronoun out of. ("the beach (that) you always talk about".)
  • "Your grandmother, who looks so happy here, ..." — why commas? (non-defining: extra info about a person we already know.)
  • Change "That's Kaan, whose sister ..." into a question with whose. (Whose sister used to be in your class?)

Speaking: Describing People, Places & Things

Learn the words, then use them to describe the people and places in your life.

neighbour (n)
a person who lives near you (komşu)
Syn: local · Ant: stranger
"The neighbour who lives above us is very quiet."
classmate (n)
a person who is in your class at school (sınıf arkadaşı)
Syn: schoolmate · Ant: stranger
"She's a classmate whose notes I always borrow."
relative (n)
a member of your family (akraba)
Syn: family member · Ant: stranger
"I have a relative who lives in Canada."
landmark (n)
a famous building or place that is easy to recognise (simge yapı)
Syn: monument · Ant: —
"The tower is a landmark which you can see for miles."
invention (n)
a new thing that someone has made for the first time (icat)
Syn: creation · Ant: copy
"The phone is an invention that changed the world."
reliable (adj)
that you can trust or depend on (güvenilir)
Syn: dependable · Ant: unreliable
"He's a friend whose word is always reliable."
talented (adj)
having a natural ability to do something well (yetenekli)
Syn: gifted · Ant: talentless
"She's a talented singer who started at ten."
memorable (adj)
easy to remember because it is special (unutulmaz)
Syn: unforgettable · Ant: ordinary
"It was a memorable day when we all met again."
spacious (adj)
large, with a lot of room inside (ferah, geniş)
Syn: roomy · Ant: cramped
"They live in a spacious house which has a big garden."
cosy (adj)
warm, comfortable and pleasant (sıcak, samimi, rahat)
Syn: snug · Ant: cold
"It's a cosy café where I often study."
remote (adj)
far away from towns and people (ıssız, uzak)
Syn: isolated · Ant: central
"We stayed in a remote village where no cars go."
well-known (adj)
that many people know about (tanınmış, meşhur)
Syn: famous · Ant: unknown
"He's a well-known author whose books sell fast."
describe (v)
to say what someone or something is like (tanımlamak, betimlemek)
Syn: portray · Ant: —
"Can you describe the person who helped you?"
belong (v)
to be owned by someone (ait olmak)
Syn: be owned by · Ant: —
"That's the man whose car this belongs to."
Describing people and places: use who, whose and where to add detail.

Reading: The Girl Who Saved the School Library

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think about your own school. Is there a room or corner that you would like to change or bring back to life?
  • The title uses a relative clause ("The Girl Who Saved the School Library"). What extra information does "who saved the school library" give?
  • Do you think it is easy or difficult for a student to start a big project at school? Guess before you read!
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text for the answers. Underline every relative pronoun (who, which, that, whose, where, when).

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): relative · landmark · reliable · talented · remote · spacious · describe · belong

One dusty, forgotten room, brought back to life as a school library.
The Girl Who Saved the School Library

Zeynep is a student who has always loved reading. The school where she studies once had a small library, but by the time she started high school, it was closed and full of dust. Zeynep, who could not imagine a school without books, decided to act, because she believed that every student deserved a quiet place to read.

Even when she was younger, books were a big part of her life. She often stayed up late, reading under a blanket with a small light, because she wanted to know how the story ended. At school, she and two close friends used to swap the books they liked best and talk about them during lunch. For Zeynep, a good story was better than any video game, and she felt sad that the students in the lower years had nowhere to sit and read together. Many of her friends spent every break looking at their phones, and she thought a real library could give them something different.

Two years ago she found a room that nobody used. It was the oldest classroom in the building, and its shelves, which had not been cleaned for years, were empty and broken. Many classmates thought the idea was silly. However, Zeynep had a clear plan. She wanted to create a place where students could read, study and meet after class.

The first job was to clean the room, and it was harder than she had imagined. On the first Saturday, only three people came to help: Zeynep, her best friend Aylin, and her younger brother. They swept the floor, wiped the old windows, and carried out the broken chairs that nobody wanted. Slowly, more teenagers started to join them. Some brought brushes and paint, and together they painted the walls a bright, warm colour. One boy from the art class drew a huge tree on one wall, with a book hanging from every branch, and it quickly became everyone's favourite part of the room.

The project, which took two years, was not easy. She wanted real books, so she asked families whose children had already left the school to donate the ones they no longer needed. There were also weeks when almost no one came to help. But she never gave up.

Finding enough books was one of the biggest problems, so Zeynep and her friends became creative. They made short videos for the school's social media pages, showing the empty shelves and asking for stories, comics and study guides. The posts were shared by hundreds of students, and boxes of books soon began to arrive. To raise a little money for shelves and new lamps, the group organised a weekend book fair in the schoolyard, where students could buy second-hand books and homemade cakes. Teachers who had once called the plan a waste of time started to smile when they saw how serious the young volunteers were.

Today, the library that Zeynep saved is the busiest room in the school. The room where dust once covered every shelf is now full of colour. Students who love stories come in every day, and the teachers, who once doubted her, are proud of what she has done.

The library is now much more than a quiet room full of shelves. Every month the students choose a "book of the month", and anyone who reads it can join a friendly meeting to talk about the ending. Before big exams, the tables fill up with teenagers who study together, share notes, and quietly help each other with difficult subjects. Younger students, who used to feel shy, have made new friends there, and a few of them have even started writing their own short stories. Zeynep still runs a small reading club after class, and she is teaching two ninth-grade students how to look after the books so that the library will stay open long after she leaves.

The library has also brought together students who never used to spend time in the same place. The players from the school football team, who once thought reading was boring, now borrow books about their favourite sports stars and how they became famous. Some students come to draw, others to plan the school magazine, and a group of friends meets there on Fridays to play quiet board games after training. Because everyone follows the same simple rules, the room stays calm, and even the noisiest teenagers speak softly when they walk inside.

Zeynep still remembers the day when she opened the door for the first time. "It was the moment that changed my school life," she says. "This is the place where I grew up as a reader, and now it has a future."

Her advice to other teenagers is simple. "You do not have to be older, richer or famous to change something at your school," she says. "If you really care about an idea, start small, ask your friends for help, and keep going even when people laugh. One empty room and a few boxes of books were enough for us." Next year Zeynep hopes to open a second corner in the library for films and music, so that every student, whatever they enjoy, can find a reason to walk through the door.

Writing: A Person or Place That Matters to Me

Write and submit your description; your teacher will review it.

Relative clauses make descriptions richer (Linking Words)

A good description does two things: it adds detail with relative clauses, and it links ideas with connectors. At A2 you used and, but, so, because. At B1 you add also, when, while, although and however, and you use relative clauses to pack more information into each sentence.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
andadd informationShe's a friend who is kind and always honest.
butshow a small contrastMy room, which is small, is cosy, but it has no window.
soshow a resultHe's reliable, so he's the person who I always call.
becausegive a reasonI love the café because it's a place where I can relax.
alsoadd an extra pointShe's talented. She's also a person whose ideas inspire me.
whensay at what past timeWhen I was a child, the village where we lived was quiet.
whiletwo things at the same timeWhile I was there, I met a boy who became a good friend.
althoughshow a strong contrastAlthough the town is remote, it's a place that I love.
howevercontrast between two sentencesThe house was old. However, it was the home where I grew up.

See the difference — the same facts, written two ways:

Separate sentences ✗Connected, fluent writing ✓
I have a friend. Her name is Aylin. She plays the guitar. She lives in Izmir. Her house is lovely. We spend weekends there. I have a friend called Aylin, who plays the guitar and lives in Izmir. Her house, which is lovely, is the place where we spend every weekend.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 relative clauses and at least 3 different connectors. Do not write sentence by sentence; link your ideas.
  • Remember: a non-defining clause needs commas and never uses that: "Aylin, who plays the guitar, ..."

Description structure (Paragraph Structure)

A description paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same every time — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningIntroduce the person or place with a defining relative clauseThere is a place that means a lot to me.
2. BodyAdd details with more relative clauses. Use non-defining clauses (commas) for extra facts, and link with connectors.The house, which is over a hundred years old, is where my grandmother lived. She was a person whose stories I loved.
3. ClosingA final comment about why it matters nowIt is still the place where I feel most at home.

Task (Görev)

Describe a person, place or thing that is important to you. Use relative clauses to add detail.

WHAT TO INCLUDE

  • an opening sentence that introduces the person or place (with a relative clause)
  • who or what they are, and why they are special to you
  • one non-defining clause with commas (e.g. My uncle, who is a pilot, ...)
  • one sentence with whose (possession) and one with where or when
  • a closing sentence about why this person or place still matters
  • at least 3 relative clauses: who, which, that, whose, where, when
  • at least 3 connectors: and, but, so, because, also, when, while, although, however
  • at least 4 words from the Speaking section (neighbour, classmate, relative, reliable, talented, memorable, spacious, cosy, remote, well-known)

EXAMPLE OPENERS

"There is a person who means a lot to me ..." "The place where I feel happiest is ..." "I want to describe my ..., who ..."

Sample Answer (~165 words) There is a person who means a lot to me: my grandmother, whose small house in a remote village was the centre of my childhood. She was a warm, reliable woman who always had time for everyone. The house, which was old and a little cosy, is the place where I spent every summer holiday.

My grandmother was also a talented cook, and the kitchen was the room where we all gathered. I still remember the mornings when she made fresh bread, because the smell filled the whole street. Although the village was far from any city, it never felt lonely, so I was always happy there.

Sadly, the house now belongs to another family. However, it is still a memorable place that lives in my memory. My grandmother, who taught me so much, is the relative I miss the most, and the village where she lived will always feel like home.
