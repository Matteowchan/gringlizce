Ünite 17 · Ellipsis & Economy · Bölüm 1/6

Grammar: Ellipsis & Economy

At C2, precision includes knowing what to leave out. This unit teaches the grammar of omission — and the discipline of saying a thing once, cleanly, without stripping it down to riddle.

Economy is meaning, minus the waste

Good writing is not measured by how much it says but by how little it wastes. Ellipsis is the grammar of leaving words out: whatever the context has already made clear, you may drop, trusting the reader to supply it. Economy is the wider habit — cutting every word that earns nothing, so the words that remain can breathe. You have met these ideas in Unit 8 (cohesion); what is new at C2 is wielding them deliberately, and knowing precisely where the cutting must stop.

The whole toolkit rests on one principle: recoverability. You may omit only what the reader can reconstruct with certainty. From it grow verb-phrase ellipsis (she can, and he can too), gapping (she took tea; he, coffee), nominal ellipsis (the red one… no, the blue) and clausal ellipsis ("Coming?" "Yes."). Add the pruning of redundancy and tautology, and you can write tight, cohesive prose — provided you never cut so deep that meaning turns ambiguous.

  • She has finished, and he has too.Verb-phrase ellipsis — "finished" is dropped after "has"; the reader recovers it from the first clause.
  • Some students passed the exam; others, [passed] not at all.Gapping — the repeated verb is left out of the parallel second clause.
  • I meant to call you, but I forgot to.Elided infinitive — "to" stands in for the whole phrase "call you".
  • In my personal opinion, I think we should plan ahead in advance.The opposite fault: redundancy. Every bold word doubles a meaning already present — economy would cut them.

1. Verb-phrase ellipsis (dropping the repeated verb group)

After an auxiliary or to, you can drop a verb phrase that has already appeared: the auxiliary alone carries it. This is the workhorse of English ellipsis — frequent, fully grammatical, and welcome even in formal writing.

TypeHow it is builtExample
after an auxiliarykeep the auxiliary, drop the restShe hasn't replied yet, but she will.
after tokeep to, drop the verb phraseI wanted to help, but I wasn't able to.
with so / neitherinvert after the markerHe objected, and so did I.
C2 subtlety
  • The elided phrase must match a form actually present: "She sings, and he can too" jars, because "can [sing]" was never set up — make it "She can sing, and he can too."
  • Ellipsis after to keeps the to: write "I forgot to," not "I forgot." when the infinitive is meant to survive.

2. Gapping & nominal ellipsis (dropping the verb, or the noun)

Gapping omits a repeated verb from a second, parallel clause: the reader carries it over. Nominal ellipsis drops a repeated noun after a modifier. Both depend on a strict parallel: the missing item must line up exactly with one already given.

TypeFull formElided (economical) form
gappingShe ordered tea, and he ordered coffee.She ordered tea; he, coffee.
nominal ellipsisI kept the old letters and burned the new letters.I kept the old letters and burned the new.
after a quantifierSome guests danced; other guests just watched.Some guests danced; others, watched.
C2 subtlety
  • Do not confuse ellipsis with substitution: "the blue one" replaces the noun with one; "the blue" truly omits it. Both are cohesive, but only the second is ellipsis.
  • Gapping needs a clean parallel: "She writes novels; he, of poetry" mismatches — make it "he, poetry."

3. Clausal ellipsis & comparatives (short answers, and "than" clauses)

Clausal ellipsis strips a whole clause down to the word that carries the news — the natural shape of replies and questions. Comparative ellipsis drops the recoverable verb after than or as. Both are everyday economy, but the comparative kind is where careless cutting breeds ambiguity.

TypeFull formElided form
short answer"Will Sophie be joining us?" "Yes, Sophie will be joining us.""Will Sophie be joining us?" "Sophie will."
reduced questionWould you like still water or sparkling water?Still or sparkling?
comparativeShe earns more than he earns.She earns more than he does.
C2 subtlety
  • Comparatives can turn ambiguous when cut too far: "I like her more than you" could mean than you [like her] or than [I like] you. Restore a verb to fix the sense.
  • A short answer must have a clear antecedent question; drop it into a vacuum and the ellipsis fails.

4. Cutting redundancy & tautology (remove what says nothing new)

Economy is not only ellipsis; much waste is simply redundant wording — two words for one idea. Tautology restates a meaning already contained ("free gift", "past history"). The economical writer hunts these and cuts, not to sound curt, but to let the real words carry weight.

WastefulEconomicalWhy
at this present moment in timenowfive words doing one word's work.
a free gift given in advancea gifttautology — gifts are free, and "given" is implied.
in the event that it rainsif it rainsa padded conjunction shrunk to one word.
C2 subtlety
  • Beware hidden tautology: "advance planning", "future prospects", "each and every" all double a meaning already there.
  • Economy trims the redundant, never the necessary: cutting a qualifier that changes the meaning is not economy but error.

5. Ellipsis, register & cohesion (how far to cut, and where)

Ellipsis is a cohesive device (Unit 8): by omitting the repeated, it ties clauses together and keeps the reader's eye moving. But how much you may drop depends on register (Unit 1). Subject-dropping ("Sounds good", "Hope you're well") is fine in a note, wrong in a report; verb-phrase ellipsis and gapping travel into formal prose without a scratch.

RegisterFreely usedBest avoided
note, text, chatsubject ellipsis (Can't come), clausal ellipsisnothing much — brevity is expected
formal report, essayverb-phrase ellipsis, gapping, comparative ellipsisdropped subjects, telegram style
legal / technicalvery restrained ellipsisany cut that could blur reference
C2 subtlety
  • Ellipsis aids cohesion only when the antecedent is close and unmistakable; across a long gap the reader loses the thread.
  • The most economical prose often hides its cuts: the reader feels the flow without noticing what was left unsaid.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the economy faults that surface even in fluent C2 writing. Each is a failure of balance — too much padding, or too deep a cut. Audit your drafts against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
She can sing, and he can dances too.She can sing, and he can too.after "can", drop the verb phrase entirely; adding a mismatched verb breaks the ellipsis.
Due to the fact that it was raining, we cancelled.Because it was raining, we cancelled.a padded conjunction — four words shrunk to one, meaning intact.
Reviewed figures; found errors; corrected all.She reviewed the figures, found errors and corrected them all.over-ellipsis — dropping every subject and object reads as a telegram, not prose.
I trust her more than my colleagues.I trust her more than my colleagues do.ambiguity from a cut comparative — restore the verb so the sense is single.
We must plan ahead in advance for the future.We must plan ahead.tautology — "in advance" and "for the future" merely repeat "ahead".

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

A junior writer drafts a paragraph; a senior editor trims it. Read it twice and watch how each note tightens a bloated line — and where the editor says that cut goes too far.

J:I wrote: "Due to the fact that the results were disappointing, we have made the decision to postpone the launch."Padded: "due to the fact that" and "made the decision to" carry no meaning.
E:Cut the padding: "Because the results disappointed, we postponed the launch."Redundancy gone; the sentence says the same thing in half the words.
J:Then: "Sales fell in March; postponed launch; team informed; awaiting revised figures."Now over-cut: every subject dropped, so it reads as a telegram.
E:That's the opposite error. Restore what the reader needs: "Sales fell in March, so we postponed the launch and told the team."Ellipsis of the repeated subject is fine; dropping all of them is not.
J:Can I keep "The board approved the budget faster than the committee"?She fears it may be ambiguous.
E:No — add the verb: "faster than the committee did." Otherwise it could mean they approved the committee.A cut comparative that breeds ambiguity must be restored.
Test yourself
  • Why is "Because the results disappointed" better than "Due to the fact that the results were disappointing"? (It cuts a five-word padded conjunction and a weak passive to say the same thing — pure economy.)
  • What made the telegram draft ("Sales fell… postponed launch…") fail? (Over-ellipsis: dropping every subject and connector strips the register and the flow; economy became obscurity.)
  • Why must "faster than the committee" become "faster than the committee did"? (The bare comparative is ambiguous — restoring the verb fixes the single intended meaning.)

Speaking: The Language of Economy

Learn the vocabulary of ellipsis and concision, then use it to say more with less.

Production Task (Üretim Görevi)

Speak, out loud, about a change you would make — at work, in your town, in a habit. Write 8 sentences and use at least 8 of the words above. Make your economy visible and deliberate: say each thing once, and cut what earns nothing.

Style requirement — apply this unit's topic
  • Use verb-phrase ellipsis once (…and I will too) and one clear gapping pair (She takes X; he, Y), and name each as you use it.
  • Include one concise sentence that you first give in a verbose form and then trim, showing the cut.
  • Point out one place where you avoided a redundant phrase or tautology.
  • End on the effect: which cut sharpened your point most — and where you deliberately kept a word because cutting it would turn the sense ambiguous.

Sentence starters (you can begin with these):

Reading: The Art of Leaving Out

Read the feature, then work through the five tasks.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Skilled readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think of a text that once impressed you with its plainness. Was it the amount it said, or how little it wasted?
  • The title calls concision "the art of leaving out". What does that phrase promise — a discipline of choice, or mere carelessness?
  • Writers often demonstrate a point while explaining it. Watch for the moment the text drops a word as it discusses dropping words.
How to read
  • First read: read quickly for the whole argument — what ellipsis and economy are, and why the writer treats brevity as courtesy rather than laziness. Don't stop for every word.
  • Second read: track the ideas as they appear — recoverability, redundancy, the risk of ambiguity — and note the warning in the final paragraph about cutting too far.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): ellipsis · economy · concise · redundant · recoverable · ambiguous

Ellipsis at work: the repeated verb is left out, and the reader restores it from the first clause.
The Art of Leaving Out

Great writing is often praised for what it contains — the vivid image, the exact word, the well-turned clause. Yet some of its finest effects come from what it leaves out. To write with economy is to trust the reader: to say a thing once and let it stand, to trim every word that earns nothing, and to let a little silence carry part of the meaning. The plainest sentence, stripped of its padding, can strike harder than a paragraph of ornament.

The grammar of omission has a name: ellipsis. When we say "She can sing, and he can too," we do not repeat "sing" — the reader supplies it. When a waiter asks "Still or sparkling?" no one misses the absent "Would you like still water or sparkling water?" Ellipsis works because language is full of the recoverable: whatever the context has already made clear need not be said again. This is not laziness but courtesy, sparing the reader a second helping of the obvious.

Economy is ellipsis's wider cousin. Where ellipsis omits whole grammatical slots, economy prunes the merely redundant — the "past history," the "advance planning," the "in my personal opinion, I think." Each doubles a meaning already present. The economical writer hunts such phrases and cuts them, not to sound curt, but to let the real words breathe. Brevity, handled well, is a form of respect.

Yet omission has its limits. Leave out too much, and the reader stumbles. "I admire her more than my colleagues" hides its meaning: more than the colleagues admire her, or more than the writer admires the colleagues? Here economy has overreached, and clarity pays the price. The skilled writer therefore weighs each cut. Say less, but never at the cost of sense; leave out the predictable, never the necessary. The art of leaving out is, in the end, the art of knowing exactly what must stay.

1. The writer insists rhetoric is "not trickery… it is the deliberate engineering of language". In your own words, what is the difference — and why does it matter?

Sample Answer"To call rhetoric engineering is to say it is honest, deliberate design — words arranged with skill toward a clear end, the way a bridge is built to carry weight. Trickery would mean hiding a weak argument behind fine phrases. It matters because the same devices can serve truth or deceit; the writer's point is that the craft itself is neutral, and the persuader is responsible for how it is used."

2. Why do you think the writer repeats "we… in threes; we… in threes; we… in threes"? What is being demonstrated as well as stated?

Speaking Task 2 (Re-shape the Appeal)

Below is a limp, flat appeal with no rhetorical shape at all — every fact is there, but nothing persuades. Your job is to re-shape it into a genuinely rousing short appeal by building in real devices — without tipping into empty, overblown rhetoric.

Our community centre is quite useful and it might close. We think it is good for people. It helps children and also older people. It costs some money to keep open. Please consider signing the petition if you want to, and maybe come to the meeting. Thank you for your time and attention.
Style requirement — apply this unit's topic
  • Build in one tricolon and one antithesis (a "not X, but Y" contrast).
  • Use anaphora at least once and one well-placed rhetorical question.
  • Make a real concession then refute it, and use inclusive "we" to unite the reader with you.
  • Write 6–8 sentences. Keep every fact; add the persuasion — but stay emphatic, not hollow.

Writing: A Short Persuasive Speech

Write a short, rousing speech that argues one clear case — weaving the devices into a real argument. Your teacher will review it.

The rhetorical toolkit (Devices at a Glance)

At C1 you learned to link and organise ideas; at C2 you learn to persuade with them. Each device below is a pattern of structure. The art is not using them all, but weaving two or three into a real argument, in a register the moment can bear.

DeviceWhat it doesExample to model
tricolongroups three parallel parts, often risingYour voice, your vote, your future.
antithesisbalances two opposites for contrastNot for the few, but for the many.
anaphorarepeats an opening to build momentumWe can act, we can lead, we can win.
rhetorical questionasks what needs no answer, to pull the reader inHow long must we wait for change?
concession-refutationadmits a point, then overturns itAdmittedly it is hard; yet it is right.
inclusive "we"folds the reader into a shared causeTogether, we decide what happens next.
Rule
  • Weave the devices into a real argument — they must serve the case, never replace it.
  • Use at least three different devices, but never a device on every line: restraint is the C2 mark. Empty rhetoric loses the reader.

How to structure the speech (Persuasive Structure)

A persuasive speech has a shape: a hook that grips, a body that argues and concedes, and a close that rouses. The devices belong at the joints — the opening question, the tricolon in the body, the anaphora at the end.

PartWhat to writeDevice to reach for
A. Hookgrab attention; name the stakesrhetorical question, inclusive "we"
B. Bodyargue the case; answer the other sidetricolon, antithesis, concession-refutation
C. Closea rousing call to actionanaphora rising to a climax, then stop

Task (Görev)

Choose one motion you believe in — save a local library, ban single-use plastic, make a landmark car-free, protect a night bus route. Write a short persuasive speech of about 200–240 words that a real audience could hear. Prove you can weave the devices into an argument — and know when to stop.

WHAT TO INCLUDE

  • a hook using a rhetorical question and inclusive "we"
  • at least one tricolon (three parallel parts) and one antithesis (not X, but Y)
  • a genuine concession-refutation (Admittedly… yet / nonetheless…)
  • a rousing close using anaphora that builds to a climax
  • a consistent register throughout — rousing but not hollow; no empty, purple padding
  • at least 4 words from the Speaking section (rhetoric, persuasion, tricolon, antithesis, anaphora, climax, concession, refutation, cadence, eloquent, emphatic, rousing)

"Ask yourselves: what kind of town do we want to be?" "This is not about ..., but about ..." "Let us not ..., let us not ..., let us not ..."

Sample Answer (~220 words) Friends, neighbours, fellow readers — tonight our library is under threat, and tonight we must decide what kind of town we wish to be.

Ask yourselves this: what is a town without a place to learn? A library is not merely a building of shelves and silence. It is a door for the curious, a refuge for the lonely, and a ladder for the poor. This tricolon is no accident: three plain images, rising, carry more persuasion than any statistic. Admittedly, the budget is tight; nonetheless, some things are too precious to price. Close these doors, and we do not save money — we spend our future.

Some will say the age of print is over. I say the age of ideas has only begun. Where others see old paper, I see young minds; where they see a cost, I see a promise. That antithesis is the whole argument in a single breath.

So let us not falter, let us not flinch, let us not fail. Sign the petition, raise your voice, and stand with your neighbours tonight. This is not a plea for a building, but a call for who we are. Save our library — and, in saving it, save the best of ourselves. The rousing close earns its cadence because the case beneath it is true.
