Present Perfect ContinuousSürekli Yakın Geçmiş

Geçmişte başlayıp süregelen (ve hâlâ etkili) eylemi vurgular; süreye odaklanır.

Emphasises an action that began in the past and is still going on (and still relevant); it focuses on duration.

Present Perfect Continuous (Sürekli Yakın Geçmiş), geçmişte başlayıp konuşma anına kadar süregelen bir eylemi ve özellikle onun süresini vurgular. Kalıbı have/has + been + fiil-ing şeklindedir. Türkçede tam bir karşılığı yoktur; en yakın çeviri "...-dir/-dır ...-yorum" (örneğin "iki saattir çalışıyorum") kalıbıdır. Bu yapı, biten değil devam eden sürece bakar; bu yönüyle sonucu vurgulayan Yakın Geçmiş ile sürekli karşılaştırılır. Durum fiillerinin (bkz. Durum Fiilleri) çoğu bu yapıda kullanılmaz.

Present Perfect Continuous highlights an action that starts in the past and, right up to the moment of speaking, stays ongoing , and it especially emphasises its duration . Its pattern is have/has + been + verb-ing . It has no exact equivalent in Turkish; the closest translation is the "...-dir/-dır ...-yorum" pattern (for example "iki saattir çalışıyorum" — I have been working for two hours). This structure looks not at a finished action but at the ongoing process it involves; in this respect it is constantly compared with the result-focused Present Perfect . Most stative verbs (see Stative Verbs) are not used in this structure.

Yani odak, eylemin bitip bitmediği değil, ne kadar süredir devam ettiğidir. Konuşan kişi bu kalıbı seçtiğinde, dinleyiciye "bu eylem bir süredir sürüyor ve hâlâ önemli" mesajını verir.

İngilizcede "perfect" (geçmişle şimdiyi bağlama) ile "continuous" (süreç, devamlılık) iki fikri tek bir kalıpta birleştirir. Bu yüzden hem geçmişe uzanır hem de "şu an hâlâ oluyor ya da yeni bitti" hissini taşır.

"Başka bir deyişle bu zaman, bir köprü gibidir: bir ucu geçmişte (eylemin başladığı an), diğer ucu şimdide (konuşma anı) durur ve arada geçen bütün süreyi görünür kılar.

Neden bu yapı var?

İngilizce, bir eylemin sonucuna mı yoksa sürecine mi baktığını dilbilgisiyle ayırır. "I have painted the wall" (duvarı boyadım — bitti, sonuç ortada) ile "I have been painting the wall" (duvarı boyuyordum — süreç, belki üstüm başım boya) aynı olayı farklı gözle anlatır. Türkçede bu ayrımı çoğu zaman tonlama ve bağlamla yaparız; İngilizce ise ayrı bir zaman kipiyle yapar. Bu yüzden bu yapıyı öğrenmek, aslında "süreci mi yoksa sonucu mu vurgulamak istiyorum?" diye düşünmeyi öğrenmektir.

Present Perfect Continuous tells us that an action began in the past and, up to the moment of speaking, has continued without interruption . At the heart of this structure lies a single question: "For how long?" In other words, the focus is not on whether the action has finished, but on how long it has been going on. When a speaker chooses this pattern, they give the listener the message "this action has been going on for a while and still matters."

In English, "perfect" (linking the past to the present) and "continuous" (process, continuity) combine two ideas in a single pattern. That is why it both reaches back into the past and carries the feeling of "it is still happening now, or has just finished."

"In other words, this tense is like a bridge: one end rests in the past (the moment the action began), the other in the present (the moment of speaking), and it makes visible all the time that has passed in between.

Why does this structure exist?

English grammatically distinguishes whether it is looking at an action's result or at its process . "I have painted the wall" (I painted the wall — finished, the result is there) and "I have been painting the wall" (I was painting the wall — a process, perhaps I'm covered in paint) describe the same event through different eyes. In Turkish we usually draw this distinction with tone and context; English does it with a separate tense. So learning this structure really means learning to think, "do I want to emphasise the process or the result?"

The closest equivalent in Turkish

The closest pattern to this structure in Turkish is the "...-dir/-dır + present tense" structure: "İki saattir bekliyorum", "Sabahtan beri çalışıyorum". Note: in Turkish the verb is in the present tense (bekliyorum), not the past. This is exactly the source of the trap learners fall into most often — seeing "have been" in English, a Turkish learner assumes it is the past. Yet the sentence's meaning lies not in the past; it lands right in the present .

I have been living here since 2015.
2015'ten beri burada yaşıyorum.
In English, 'have been living', but in Turkish 'yaşıyorum' (present tense). The action is still going on.
They have been arguing about money all evening.
Bütün akşam para yüzünden tartışıyorlar.
It began in the past, continued all evening, and may still be going on; emphasis on duration.
My phone has been acting strangely for a few days.
Telefonum birkaç gündür tuhaf davranıyor.
A situation that started a few days ago and is still going on; 'for + duration'.
Keep in Mind: the BRIDGE rule
Think of this tense as a bridge : one end is in the past (the action began), the other end in the in the present (we are still talking). The distance you walk across the bridge = DURATION. Whenever you hear the question "for how long?", picture this bridge and build have/has + been + -ing .

Why does it feel hard for Turkish learners?

  • Because there is no exact equivalent in Turkish, a "past or present?" confusion arises.
  • "have been + -ing" is a three-part pattern; learners usually forget the word 'been'.
  • The difference from the Present Perfect Simple (result or process) is not intuitive.
  • Stative verbs (know, love, have) are not used in this structure; this exception is not obvious at first.
  • Because the verb in the Turkish sentence is in the present tense, learners tend to build the present continuous (am/is/are + -ing) in English too.

Affirmative sentence

Pattern: subject + have/has + been + verb(-ing). With 'I/you/we/they' you use have, and with 'he/she/it' you use has . 'been' never changes and is never dropped. All three parts (have/has + been + verb-ing) must be present at once; if one is missing, the sentence is wrong.

Brick 1

have / has — chosen according to the subject: I/you/we/they → have, he/she/it → has.

Brick 2

been — never changes, never drops; it is the unchanging backbone of the pattern.

Brick 3

The main verb -ing in the form: working, studying, raining — this is where the continuity comes from.

I have been studying English for two years.
İki yıldır İngilizce çalışıyorum.
have + been + studying.
She has been working here since March.
Mart'tan beri burada çalışıyor.
has + been + working; third person singular.
They've been talking on the phone for an hour.
Bir saattir telefonda konuşuyorlar.
they've = they have; a contraction.
It has been snowing since last night.
Dün geceden beri kar yağıyor.
it + has + been + snowing; weather is a typical use.
Key Tip: the 3-brick wall
Think of the sentence as a wall made of three bricks: [have/has] + [been] + [verb-ING]. 90% of learners forget the middle 'been' brick and the wall collapses. The rule: never pull out the middle brick. As you build each sentence, repeat "HAVE-BEEN-ING" to yourself.

Process or result? Present Perfect Continuous looks at the process (the action itself, the time spent); Present Perfect Simple , on the other hand, looks at the result (completeness, quantity). The same event enters two different patterns depending on what the speaker wants to emphasise. So choosing between the two is not a matter of 'right/wrong' but a matter of emphasis .

I've been reading this book. (I'm not finished.)
Bu kitabı okuyorum. (Bitmedi.)
Process-focused — not how many pages, but the effort is emphasised.
I've read this book. (I finished it.)
Bu kitabı okudum. (Bitirdim.)
Result-focused — completion is emphasised.

A sense of incompleteness. The Continuous often implies that the action is still going on or has not fully finished . That is why "I've been painting the kitchen" gives the feeling "maybe it isn't finished yet," while "I've painted the kitchen" says "it's done, here's the result." English speakers usually feel this nuance unconsciously; you need to turn it into a 'feel' too.

Duration or number/quantity? If duration (how many hours, how many days) is emphasised, use the Continuous; if number/quantity (how many, how many times) is emphasised, the Simple is preferred. "I've been writing emails all morning" (duration) ↔ "I've written ten emails" (number). If you see a countable number in the sentence (three cakes, ten emails, five pages), the Simple is very likely required.

The key to telling these four structures apart is to ask two questions: (1) Is the action linked to the present, or did it stay in the past? and (2) Is the process or the result being emphasised? The Present Perfect Continuous is on the "linked to the present + process" side of both. The Present Continuous looks only at the moment of speaking and does not reach into the past; the Past Continuous stays entirely in the past and cuts its link to the present. The Present Perfect Simple is linked to the present but foregrounds the result rather than the process. If you ask these two questions for every sentence, you will choose among the four structures without confusion.

In academic and formal writing, the Present Perfect Continuous describes ongoing research, debate or trends . It especially stresses that a topic has been studied for a long time and is still current. Even so, because academic writing demands precision, it often prefers the Present Perfect Simple; the Continuous is chosen mainly to emphasise 'ongoing effort/trend'. That is why two tenses usually sit side by side in an academic paragraph: the Simple for an established finding, the Continuous for a debate that is still going on.

Tips & Tricks: use it sparingly in academic writing
In IELTS/academic writing, use the Continuous for trends and ongoing debate : "...have been increasing / have been arguing / have been examining." But when you report a finding as a firm conclusion , switch to the Simple: "Studies have shown..." General rule: process and continuation → Continuous; a proven result → Simple. Switching between the two deliberately makes your style more mature.

Meaning check Answer the question for each sentence.
  1. "I've been painting the fence." — Is the job finished? Is it clear?
    No, it isn't clear; in fact it probably isn't finished. The Continuous looks at the process and does not guarantee completion.
  2. "I've painted the fence." — Is the job finished?
    Yes, it's finished. The Simple emphasises the result/completion.
  3. "It's been raining." — Might it be raining right now, or has it just stopped?
    Either is possible; but the most typical meaning is 'it has just stopped, and its trace (wetness) is there.'
  4. "She's been living in Paris for a year." — Is she still in Paris?
    Yes, she still lives there. The action continues up to now.
  5. "Have you been crying?" — What is the speaker basing the question on?
    On fresh evidence (such as red eyes); they see the trace of a just-finished action.
  6. "I've been trying to reach you all day!" — Is this just information, or is there emotion too?
    There is emotion: it carries a tone of reproach/frustration.
  7. "I've written three reports" versus "I've been writing reports all day" — which one emphasises duration and which one emphasises number?
    The first emphasises number (three = a completed result, Simple), the second emphasises duration (all day = process, Continuous).

Where to start
Enter the topic with a duration question : write "How long have you been learning English?" on the board and collect real answers. Have students start to use the pattern before they fully understand it. Then separate 'for' (duration) and 'since' (a point) with concrete examples; visualise this by drawing a timeline.

Critical concept check: The Turkish learner's biggest trap is thinking 'have been' is the past tense. Ask this question often: "Is the action still happening?" (Is the action still happening?) — The correct answer is usually "yes." This makes the student match the pattern with 'still going on' rather than with the Turkish 'past'.

For the Simple/Continuous distinction, use paired sentences: "I've painted the wall" vs "I've been painting the wall". Ask students "In which one is the job finished? In which one are you covered in paint?" This concrete image fixes the abstract rule.

Suggested class activity
The "guess what they've been doing" game: One student leaves the classroom; when they return, the others look at their "traces" and ask a guessing question: "Your hands are dirty — have you been gardening?" This practises the 'fresh evidence' use in a fun, memorable way. Add a separate elimination round for the stative-verb trap (know, love).

İPUCU

Tek cümlelik hafıza kancası: SINCE = nokta, FOR = çizgi; SAYI → Simple, SÜRE → Continuous. Emin değilsen "How long...?" sorusunu sor; cevabı süreyse Present Perfect Continuous'tasın. How long have you been studying?

  • Pattern: have/has + been + verb-ing. 'been' and '-ing' never drop.
  • Main idea: An action that begins in the past and is still ongoing or has just finished; the focus is on duration.
  • Key question: How long...? — for duration use 'for' (an amount), for the start use 'since' (a point).
  • Keep in mind: SINCE = a point, FOR = a line; see a NUMBER, use the Simple; see DURATION, use the Continuous.
  • Turkish equivalent: "...-dir/-dır + -yor" (iki saattir bekliyorum) — but the Turkish verb is in the present tense, not the past.
  • Difference from the Simple: Continuous = process/duration (may be unfinished); Simple = result/amount (finished).
  • Exception: Stative verbs (know, love, own) take no Continuous; the Simple is used with them (see Stative Verbs).
  • Signal words (for, since, lately) help but are not a guarantee; the meaning decides.
  • Fresh-evidence use: An inference from the trace of a just-finished action — "Your eyes are red. Have you been crying?"
TIP

One-sentence memory hook: SINCE = a point, FOR = a line; NUMBER → Simple, DURATION → Continuous. If you're not sure, ask "How long...?"; if the answer is a duration, you are in the Present Perfect Continuous. How long have you been studying?
