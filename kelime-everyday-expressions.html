Günlük ifadeler (everyday expressions), İngilizcede kelimesi kelimesine çevrilemeyen ama günlük konuşmanın akışını kuran kalıplardır: Never mind, It's up to you, No worries, Fair enough, Sounds good gibi. Bunlar tek tek kelimelerin toplamı değil, bir işlev taşır: reddetme, rahatlatma, onaylama, konuyu kapatma. Bu yönüyle deyimlerle akrabadırlar ama daha şeffaf ve daha sık kullanılırlar; eşdizimlerle de sınır komşusudur. Bu bölümde her kalıbı anlamı, tonu ve resmiyet düzeyiyle öğrenecek; Türkçe düşünen bir öğrencinin düştüğü çeviri tuzaklarını göreceksin. En çok akıcılığı bunlar belirler.

Everyday expressions are patterns in English that cannot be translated word for word but shape the flow of daily conversation: like Never mind, It's up to you, No worries, Fair enough, Sounds good. These are not the sum of individual words but carry a function: refusing, reassuring, approving, closing a topic. In this way they are related to idioms but are more transparent and used more often; they also border on collocations. In this section you will learn each pattern with its meaning, tone and register; you will see the translation traps a learner who thinks in Turkish falls into. These determine fluency most of all.

Speaking a language grammatically correctly and speaking it naturally are different things. A Turkish learner may build flawless sentences yet still sound 'like a textbook' to an English speaker. The difference is often not in individual words but in the small ready-made patterns sprinkled through the conversation: Never mind, No worries, Fair enough, It's up to you. We call these everyday expressions.

These patterns carry a function. That is, we learn them by looking at what they do, not by translating them. Never mind word for word means 'never [mind]'; but its function is 'don't worry, it's not important'. That is why a learner translating word for word from the dictionary always goes wrong. The right question is: In what situation and with what tone is this pattern said?

Why do these exist? Because human speech does not just convey information; it builds relationships, softens, reassures, closes. Saying No worries really means 'you don't need to worry, we're good' — it is a gesture, not information. Turkish has its equivalents too: 'Boşver', 'Sana kalmış', 'Estağfurullah', 'Ne demek', 'Elimde değil'. A foreigner cannot translate these word for word either.

Why Is It Hard?
The difficulty comes from three places: (1) The meaning is not the sum of the words. (2) The wrong register can sound rude or cold — saying No worries to your boss is risky. (3) Because there is no exact Turkish equivalent, the learner says something either too formal or too artificial. This section targets exactly these three traps.

These patterns resemble idioms but are more transparent and far more frequent; they are also related to collocations because the words always come in the same pattern (for example never + mind). But their most important difference: they directly determine the fluency and naturalness of speech.

Rahatlatma ve karşılık

Özür ya da teşekküre yanıt: No worries, Never mind, It's fine — karşı tarafı ferahlatır.

Kararı bırakma

Seçimi nazikçe karşıya verir: It's up to you, Whatever you like — Türkçe “sana kalmış”.

Onay ve kabul

Öneriye yeşil ışık: Sounds good (istekli), Fair enough (isteksiz), That works (pratik).

Reassuring and responding

A reply to an apology or thanks: No worries, Never mind, It's fine — puts the other person at ease.

Handing over the decision

Gently gives the choice to the other person: It's up to you, Whatever you like — Turkish “sana kalmış”.

Approval and acceptance

A green light to a suggestion: Sounds good (eager), Fair enough (reluctant), That works (practical).

  • Everyday expressions are not translated word for word; they are learned with their function and tone.
  • Never mind = Boşver / it's not important (closes the topic).
  • It's up to you = Sana kalmış / you decide (hands the decision over).
  • No worries = No problem / you're welcome (informal, reassuring).
  • Fair enough = Okay, that's fair / I won't object (reluctant acceptance).
  • Sounds good = That sounds nice / okay (approval of a suggestion).
  • I can't help it = Elimde değil (inability to control).
  • The most critical point is register: informal patterns look wrong in a formal setting.
  • These patterns are the biggest factor in fluency and naturalness — worth memorising.
  • The right question is not 'what does it mean?' but 'when and with what tone is it said?'.

Most everyday expressions are single units: unchangeable blocks memorised as they are. So the 'affirmative/negative/question' conjugation does not work like an ordinary verb. Still, it helps to see how these patterns are positioned within a sentence: some are a stand-alone answer, some are added at the beginning or end of a sentence.

1) As a stand-alone answer

Most patterns can answer a question or situation on their own. No extra verb is needed.

"Sorry I'm late." — "No worries."
"Geç kaldığım için üzgünüm." — "Sorun değil."

Learning everyday expressions by function is the most efficient way. Below we go from the most common functions to the more specific. For each pattern there are examples from several contexts: everyday, work, travel, academic.

CLUE

The real test of this section is register. Patterns that serve the same function suit different settings: with your boss, Of course or You're welcome is safer than No worries.

  1. Identify the function: reassuring, approving, handing over the decision, or a good wish?
  2. Choose the register: is the setting informal or formal? For the same function the pattern changes with register.
  3. Set the tone: eager or reluctant? Sounds good is enthusiastic, Fair enough is measured.

Used to reassure the other person when they apologise or worry. No worries is Australian in origin but is now common across the whole English-speaking world; it is informal. Never mind closes a small setback by saying 'don't worry about it'.

In everyday conversation, English speakers rely heavily on short fixed expressions to manage interaction smoothly. Phrases such as "never mind", "no worries" and "it's up to you" perform social functions rather than simply conveying information: they reassure, soften refusals or hand a decision to the listener. In academic and professional writing, however, these expressions are generally considered too informal. meaning check Think about each situation: what is the pattern's function and tone?
  1. When saying 'Fair enough', is the speaker likely accepting the suggestion with enthusiasm or reluctantly?
    Usually reluctant/measured acceptance: it says 'I won't object, that's fair' but is not enthusiastic. For enthusiasm, use 'Sounds good!'.
  2. When you say 'If you say so' to someone, have you fully believed them?
    Usually no. On the surface it is acceptance but it often carries veiled doubt: 'if you say so, but I'm not so sure'.
  3. What is the difference between 'I can't help it' and 'It can't be helped'?
    'I can't help it' = no personal control (I can't help it). 'It can't be helped' = the situation cannot be changed (there's no help for it). The first focuses on the person, the second on the situation.
  4. Why is it risky to write 'No worries' in a formal email to your boss in reply to their thanks?
    'No worries' is informal register; with someone formal/senior it looks too casual. 'Certainly' or 'My pleasure' is more appropriate.
  5. Do 'Never mind him' and 'Never mind' mean the same?
    No. 'Never mind' = never mind / close the topic. 'Never mind him' = don't mind him / don't worry about him. When the structure changes, the function shifts.

Sık Görülen Zorluk

Where to Start?
Start from function, not from the word. Give the class a real situation ('Someone apologised, what do you say?') and elicit the natural response. Follow the order: first recognition (catching it in listening), then production (using it in dialogue). Instead of listing and drilling patterns, anchor them in context with mini role-plays.

Concept-check questions: Is 'No worries' informal or formal? (informal). Is 'Fair enough' an enthusiastic approval? (no, reluctant/measured). 'I can't help it' + which form follows? (-ing). Such questions test use, not a rule, and show whether the student has really understood.

Activity idea — 'Register Switch': Give students an informal dialogue and ask them to turn the same content into a formal email ('No worries' → 'That is not a problem'). This makes the register difference concrete and bridges to academic writing. Alternative: 'Tone cards' — have the same 'Fair enough' sentence read first in a neutral, then a sarcastic tone, and discuss the difference.

A Common Difficulty
Turkish students try to translate these patterns word for word ('It's not in my hand'). Catch this early and repeat the principle 'patterns are ready-made blocks, not translated but memorised and slotted in'. Also warn against overuse: a student who squeezes 'no worries' into every sentence also sounds artificial.

Tips & Tricks: Sınavda Register
Sınavda (yazma bölümü) bir günlük kalıp yazasın geldiğinde dur: 'Bu resmî bir metin mi?' Evetse kalıbı resmî karşılığıyla değiştir — no worries → that is not a problem, it's up to you → the decision is yours. Konuşma/rol-play bölümündeyse tam tersine bu kalıpları kullanmak sana akıcılık puanı kazandırır.
Tips & Tricks: Dinlemede Yakala
Dinleme sınavında 'Fair enough' veya 'If you say so' duyarsan, konuşanın isteksiz kabul ya da şüphe hissettiğini işaretle — soru çoğu zaman tam bu tutumu sorar. Kalıbın tonu, cevabın anahtarıdır. Benzer şekilde 'No worries' duyunca ortamın samimi olduğunu anlarsın.
Puf Noktası: Çevirme, Eşle
Bir Türkçe kalıbı İngilizceye aktarırken kelimeleri değil işlevi eşle. 'Elimde değil' → kontrol yok → I can't help it. 'Boşver' → konuyu kapat → Never mind. Bu 'işlev eşleme' refleksi, kelime kelime çeviri hatalarının %90'ını önler.
Remember: The MTR Rule
When learning each new everyday expression, note three things: its Meaning (what does it mean?), its Tone (polite/reluctant/relaxed?), and its Register (informal or formal?). Without the MTR check you can't say you know a pattern. Example — No worries: Meaning = no problem; Tone = reassuring; Register = informal.
Remember: The Enthusiasm Ladder
When giving approval, think of the enthusiasm level as a ladder: Fair enough (reluctant) < That works (neutral) < Sounds good (positive) < Sounds great / Perfect! (enthusiastic). You are accepting, but choose how eager you are on this ladder.
Tips & Tricks: Register in the Exam
In the exam (writing section), when you feel like writing an everyday pattern, stop: 'Is this a formal text?' If so, replace the pattern with its formal equivalent — no worries → that is not a problem, it's up to you → the decision is yours. In the speaking/role-play section, on the contrary, using these patterns earns you fluency marks.
Tips & Tricks: Catch It in Listening
In a listening exam, if you hear 'Fair enough' or 'If you say so', mark that the speaker feels reluctant acceptance or doubt — the question often asks exactly about this attitude. The pattern's tone is the key to the answer. Similarly, when you hear 'No worries' you understand the setting is informal.
Key Point: Don't Translate, Match
When transferring a Turkish pattern into English, match the function, not the words. 'Elimde değil' → no control → I can't help it. 'Boşver' → close the topic → Never mind. This 'function-matching' reflex prevents 90% of word-for-word translation mistakes.
