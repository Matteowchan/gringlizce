Ünite 5 · Wish & If Only · Bölüm 1/6

Grammar: Wish & If Only

Talk about regrets and impossible situations — and control the tense that carries the feeling.

Wanting a different reality

Sometimes reality is not what we want. We are too short, we did not study, someone keeps annoying us. In English, the words wish and if only let you step out of the real world and imagine a better one. The tricky part is not the words themselves — it is the tense that follows them, because that tense carries the whole meaning.

The golden idea of this unit: after wish and if only, we "shift the tense one step back into the past". A present wish uses a past tense; a past regret uses the past perfect. Master this shift and you can express regret, frustration and longing like a confident B2 speaker.

  • I wish I had more time.Şu anki durum pişmanlığı (present) → wish + Past Simple.
  • I wish I had joined the team.Geçmiş pişmanlığı (past) → wish + Past Perfect.
  • I wish you would listen to me.Başkasının davranışından rahatsızlık → wish + would.
  • If only I were braver!Güçlü dilek/pişmanlık, vurgulu → if only + were.

1. wish / if only + Past Simple (a present situation)

Use this to complain about, or long for a change in, a situation right now. The situation is real and present, but you use a past tense to show it is imaginary — the opposite of what is true.

Reality nowThe wishMeaning
I don't have much time.I wish I had more time.present situation I want to change
I can't ride a bike.I wish I could ride a bike.a present ability I lack
It's raining.If only it weren't raining!emphatic complaint about now
Key point — the "were" rule
  • After wish and if only, careful English uses were for every subject, not "was": I wish I were taller. ✓ She wishes she were here. ✓
  • Use wish + could for a present ability you do not have: I wish I could speak French.

2. wish / if only + Past Perfect (a past regret)

Use this to look back and regret something that already happened — or did not happen. The event is finished, so you shift one more step back, into the Past Perfect (had + V3).

What really happenedThe regretMeaning
I didn't study.I wish I had studied harder.regret about a past action
I said no to the trip.If only I had said yes!emphatic regret about the past
You didn't tell me.I wish you had told me.regret about another person's past act
Don't mix the two times
  • Present situation → Past Simple: I wish I knew the answer (I don't know it now).
  • Past event → Past Perfect: I wish I had known the answer (in the exam yesterday).

3. wish + would (someone else's behaviour)

Use wish + would when another person (or thing) does something annoying and repeated, and you want them to change. It carries a feeling of irritation and a request for different behaviour.

  • I wish you would stop interrupting me.Tekrarlayan, rahatsız edici davranış → değişsin istiyorsun.
  • I wish it would stop raining.Doğa/durum için de olur.
  • I wish the neighbours would turn the music down.Başkasının davranışına sitem.
Two important limits
  • Do not use wish + would about yourself. For your own situation or ability, use the Past Simple or could: I wish I could concentrate ✓ — not "I wish I would concentrate" ✗
  • Never use wish + will: I wish you would help ✓ — "I wish you will help" ✗

4. if only (the emphatic cousin)

If only works exactly like wish — same tenses — but it is stronger and more emotional. Use it when the feeling is intense, and it often stands as an exclamation on its own.

Tense after "if only"ExampleFeeling
Past SimpleIf only I had a bike!strong present wish
Past PerfectIf only I had listened to her!deep past regret
wouldIf only he would call me back!frustration at behaviour
Tip
  • If only and wish are interchangeable, but if only sounds more dramatic: I wish I had said yes vs. If only I had said yes!

5. Quick decision guide (which tense?)

When you want to build a wish, ask yourself one question: am I talking about now, the past, or someone's behaviour? Then the tense chooses itself.

You mean...UseExample
A situation now you want to changewish/if only + Past SimpleI wish I lived by the sea.
An ability you lack nowwish/if only + couldI wish I could swim.
A regret about the pastwish/if only + Past PerfectI wish I had saved my pocket money.
Annoyance at someone's behaviourwish/if only + wouldI wish he would hurry up.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B2 students still make with wishes. Check every row against your own English.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
I wish I will be taller.I wish I were taller.wish never takes "will"; use "were" for a present state.
I wish you will help me.I wish you would help me.annoyance at behaviour → would, not "will".
I wish I studied harder for yesterday's test.I wish I had studied harder.a past regret needs the Past Perfect.
I wish I would be more organised.I wish I were more organised.don't use "would" about yourself.
If only she was here now.If only she were here now.careful English uses "were" after wish/if only.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two friends talk after a disappointing week. Read it twice and watch each type of wish at work: a present situation, a past regret, and frustration at someone's behaviour.

A:You look down. What's wrong?Genel açılış sorusu.
B:I lost my football match again. I wish I were a calmer player.Şu anki durum → wish + were.
A:Don't be hard on yourself. I wish I could help you practise.Şu an sahip olmadığın imkân → wish + could.
B:Thanks. If only I had trained harder before the match!Geçmiş pişmanlığı → if only + Past Perfect.
A:And your coach? He's always late.Konuyu başka birine getiriyor.
B:Don't remind me. I wish he would turn up on time for once!Başkasının davranışına sitem → wish + would.
Test yourself
  • Which line is a past regret? Why the Past Perfect? ("If only I had trained harder" — a finished, missed chance.)
  • Why is "I wish he would turn up" correct, but "I wish I would be calmer" wrong? (would = other people's behaviour, not your own state.)
  • Rewrite "I wish I were a calmer player" using if only. (If only I were a calmer player!)

Speaking: Regrets & Decisions

Learn the words, then use them to talk about choices you wish you had made differently.

regret (v/n)
to feel sorry about something you did or did not do (pişman olmak; pişmanlık)
Syn: rue · Ant: welcome
"I regret not joining the football team last year."
hindsight (n)
understanding of an event only after it has happened (sonradan anlama, geç akıl)
Syn: retrospect · Ant: foresight
"In hindsight, I wish I had entered the drawing contest."
foresight (n)
the ability to imagine and prepare for the future (öngörü, ileri görüşlülük)
Syn: vision · Ant: hindsight
"With more foresight, I would have started practising earlier."
opportunity (n)
a chance to do something useful or good (fırsat)
Syn: chance · Ant: —
"If only I had taken that opportunity to join the choir."
miss out (phr v)
to lose a chance to do or have something good (kaçırmak)
Syn: forgo · Ant: seize
"I wish I hadn't missed out on the trip."
reconsider (v)
to think about a decision again and maybe change it (yeniden düşünmek)
Syn: rethink · Ant: —
"He reconsidered his plan before the school trip."
impulsive (adj)
acting suddenly, without thinking it through (fevri, düşünmeden hareket eden)
Syn: rash · Ant: cautious
"I wish I weren't so impulsive with my pocket money."
turn down (phr v)
to refuse an offer or invitation (reddetmek)
Syn: reject · Ant: accept
"He turned down a place on the team and now regrets it."
look back (phr v)
to think about the past (geriye bakmak)
Syn: reflect · Ant: —
"When I look back, I have very few regrets."
hasty (adj)
done too quickly, without enough care (aceleci)
Syn: rushed · Ant: careful
"Don't make a hasty decision you might regret."
second-guess (v)
to keep doubting a decision after you have made it (sürekli sorgulamak)
Syn: doubt · Ant: trust
"Stop second-guessing yourself all the time."
settle for (phr v)
to accept something less than you really wanted (yetinmek, razı olmak)
Syn: accept · Ant: hold out
"I wish I hadn't settled for the easy project."
worthwhile (adj)
worth the time, money or effort you give it (değer, zahmete değer)
Syn: rewarding · Ant: pointless
"Every mistake was a worthwhile lesson."
priorities (n)
the things that are most important to you (öncelikler)
Syn: — · Ant: —
"If only I had known my real priorities sooner."
Talking about regrets: use wish + Past Perfect for the past, and wish + could for what you cannot do now.

Reading: The Road Not Taken

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think of a decision you once made. Do you ever wish you had chosen differently?
  • The text is about regret and second chances. Which wish structures do you expect to see?
  • The title is "The Road Not Taken". Do you think the story ends happily or sadly? Guess!
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text. Notice each wish — wish + Past Simple, wish + Past Perfect, if only — and ask why the writer chose it.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): regret · hindsight · opportunity · reconsider · impulsive · hasty · priorities · worthwhile

One decision, two roads: the choices we make — and the ones we wish we had made.
The Road Not Taken: Selin's Second Chance

Everyone who knew Selin thought she would grow up to be a wonderful artist. From the age of nine she carried a sketchbook everywhere, and by eleven she was drawing comics for her friends, painting scenery for the school play and filling notebook after notebook with characters. Her art teacher praised her imagination, and her friends were certain that one day her drawings would hang in a real gallery. Drawing was not simply a hobby for her; it was the language in which she felt most at home. Yet, as she grew a little older, the fear of failing in front of everyone began to weigh on her mind, and the bold dreams of her younger days slowly gave way to more cautious, careful choices.

Selin often says that if only she had made one different decision, her whole life would look different. Last year, she turned down a place on the school art team and joined the quiet writing club instead, because it seemed safer.

The decision did not happen overnight. Some classmates, who had entered the big art competition before and lost, gently reminded her how tough the judges could be. Others told stories of talented friends who had tried and been laughed at. At the time, these warnings felt like good advice rather than fear, and Selin genuinely believed she was making the sensible, grown-up choice. She reasoned that she could always keep drawing at home, while the writing club felt calm and easy. On paper, the logic seemed perfect. In practice, though, something inside her quietly protested every time she watched the art team head off to paint.

For a while she regretted that choice. 'I wish I had followed my heart,' she used to tell her friends. Although the writing club was fun, she never felt truly excited the way she did with a pencil in her hand. However, she kept her sketchbook in the corner of her room and drew in it every single evening.

Her school days followed a familiar rhythm. She went to lessons, wrote short stories for the club, helped edit the class newsletter and packed up when the bell rang. Her friends respected her, and more than once she was asked to lead a writing group. Outwardly, she seemed perfectly happy: good marks, kind friends and a hobby that pleased her parents. Inwardly, though, she felt a quiet emptiness that no gold star could fill. 'You always seem somewhere else,' a friend once said as Selin doodled a superhero in the margin of her notebook. She simply smiled, because she knew exactly where her mind had wandered — back to the art team she had never quite dared to join.

Then, in hindsight, she realised something important. Her time in the writing club had not been wasted; therefore, she could now create something entirely her own. She had learned how to build a story, plan a project and choose the perfect words — skills that most young artists lack. Nevertheless, a quiet voice still whispered, 'What if?'

This realisation grew stronger after she met an old friend who had become a gifted painter. He was extraordinarily talented, yet he confessed that he never knew how to turn his beautiful pictures into a real story, or how to plan a long project without giving up halfway. Listening to him, Selin understood that her so-called 'wrong turn' had actually given her exactly the skills many young artists needed. She began to imagine an opportunity that joined both worlds: the drawing she loved and the storytelling she had spent a whole year learning. For the first time in ages, regret started to feel less like a wall and more like a doorway.

A few months ago, Selin finally reconsidered her priorities. She did not decide impulsively; whereas her younger self might have made a hasty decision, the older Selin planned everything carefully. Moreover, she had saved enough pocket money to buy the paints and paper she would need. Since that day, she has never looked back.

The change was not easy at first. In the beginning she showed her comics to only a few friends and lay awake at night wondering whether she had been silly to try again. She started a little sketch-blog, drew short comic strips to share online and slowly earned a name as a patient, funny storyteller. Word spread from one classmate to another, and before long everyone wanted to read her next strip. She also began drawing clever cartoons for the school magazine, using her writing skills to make each one witty and clear. Every skill she had once thought was a waste now helped her beautifully.

There were setbacks along the way, of course. One time the school magazine lost her best comic strip, and she had to draw the whole thing again from memory. Another pupil started a rival comic that was very popular for a while. Yet each problem taught her to try a new way rather than give up. She learned to keep spare copies of her work, to save her best ideas for later, and to trust that hard weeks rarely last forever. The same patience that had made her a careful writer now made her a determined young artist, and gradually the worried nights grew rarer and the happy days more common.

Today Selin draws comics and paints scenery for the school play. She has less free time than before, yet she has never been happier. 'I used to wish I had chosen differently,' she says. 'Now I know that every road taught me something worthwhile. If only I had understood that sooner.'

When younger children ask her for advice, Selin refuses to tell them that following your heart is always the right answer, or that playing it safe is always a mistake. Instead, she encourages them to see their lives as a long journey made up of many roads, each one teaching them something they will need later. 'The road you did not take is not lost,' she likes to say. 'Sometimes it is simply waiting a little further ahead, and everything you learned on the other path is exactly what you need to walk it well.' It is a lesson she had to live before she could truly believe it — and one she hopes others will grasp a great deal sooner than she did.

Writing: A Decision I Regret

Write and submit your paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound B2 (Linking Words)

At B1 you linked ideas with and, but, so, because. At B2 you sound noticeably more advanced when you use formal, precise connectors. Four of them are essential this unit — and two familiar ones, although and however, become sharper tools.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
moreoveradd a stronger, extra pointI turned it down. Moreover, I never told anyone why.
thereforeshow a logical resultI had no plan; therefore, I settled for the easy project.
whereascontrast two ideas directlyMy friend took the risk, whereas I played it safe.
neverthelesssay "in spite of that"I had many doubts. Nevertheless, I stayed.
althoughconcede a point inside one sentenceAlthough I regret it, I learned a lot.
howevercontrast between two sentencesI was afraid. However, I wish I had tried.

See the difference — the same facts, written two ways:

Basic linking ✗Confident B2 writing ✓
I made a decision. It was fast. I regret it. I learned something. I feel better now. I made a hasty decision, and I still regret it. Although I wish I had thought harder, the mistake taught me a great deal; therefore, I feel wiser now. My friend hesitated, whereas I acted too fast.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different B2 connectors from the table. Do not write sentence by sentence; connect your ideas.
  • Moreover, Therefore, However, Nevertheless usually start a sentence and take a comma: "However, I wish I had tried."

Paragraph structure (Paragraph Structure)

A "regret" paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningA topic sentence that names the regretWhen I look back, one decision still fills me with regret.
2. BodyTell the story. Use the Past Simple for finished events, then wish / if only + Past Perfect for the regret and wish + Past Simple / could for how you feel now. Link with B2 connectors.I turned down the place. If only I had said yes! Now I wish I could try again.
3. ClosingA final comment about now or the futureI have learned my lesson; therefore, I will not hesitate next time.

Sample Answer (~185 words) When I look back, one decision still fills me with regret. Last year, I turned down the opportunity to join the school art team, because I was afraid of failing in front of everyone. In hindsight, that was a hasty and rather impulsive choice.

At the time, my priorities were all wrong. I settled for the quiet writing club, whereas my best friend joined the team and loved every minute. Although I was comfortable, a quiet voice kept asking, 'What if?' I often wished I had been braver.

For a long time I used to second-guess everything. If only I had trusted myself, I told myself, my year would look completely different. Nevertheless, I have slowly stopped blaming my younger self. Every experience, even a disappointing one, has been worthwhile in some way.

These days I think differently. I wish I could draw all day, and I am finally making my own comics. Moreover, I have promised myself one thing: I will never turn down a real opportunity again. I still have regrets; however, I refuse to let them spoil my future.
