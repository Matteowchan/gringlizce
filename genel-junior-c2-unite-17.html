Grammar: Ellipsis & Economy

At C2, precision includes knowing what to leave out. This unit teaches the grammar of omission — and the discipline of saying a thing once, cleanly, without stripping it down to riddle.

Economy is meaning, minus the waste

Good writing is not measured by how much it says but by how little it wastes. Ellipsis is the grammar of leaving words out: whatever the context has already made clear, you may drop, trusting the reader to supply it. Economy is the wider habit — cutting every word that earns nothing, so the words that remain can breathe. You have met these ideas in Unit 8 (cohesion); what is new at C2 is wielding them deliberately, and knowing precisely where the cutting must stop.

The whole toolkit rests on one principle: recoverability. You may omit only what the reader can reconstruct with certainty. From it grow verb-phrase ellipsis (she can, and he can too), gapping (she took tea; he, coffee), nominal ellipsis (the red one… no, the blue) and clausal ellipsis ("Coming?" "Yes."). Add the pruning of redundancy and tautology, and you can write tight, cohesive prose — provided you never cut so deep that meaning turns ambiguous.

  • She has finished, and he has too.Verb-phrase ellipsis — "finished" is dropped after "has"; the reader recovers it from the first clause.
  • Some students passed the exam; others, [passed] not at all.Gapping — the repeated verb is left out of the parallel second clause.
  • I meant to call you, but I forgot to.Elided infinitive — "to" stands in for the whole phrase "call you".
  • In my personal opinion, I think we should plan ahead in advance.The opposite fault: redundancy. Every bold word doubles a meaning already present — economy would cut them.

1. Verb-phrase ellipsis (dropping the repeated verb group)

After an auxiliary or to, you can drop a verb phrase that has already appeared: the auxiliary alone carries it. This is the workhorse of English ellipsis — frequent, fully grammatical, and welcome even in formal writing.

TypeHow it is builtExample
after an auxiliarykeep the auxiliary, drop the restShe hasn't replied yet, but she will.
after tokeep to, drop the verb phraseI wanted to help, but I wasn't able to.
with so / neitherinvert after the markerHe objected, and so did I.
C2 subtlety
  • The elided phrase must match a form actually present: "She sings, and he can too" jars, because "can [sing]" was never set up — make it "She can sing, and he can too."
  • Ellipsis after to keeps the to: write "I forgot to," not "I forgot." when the infinitive is meant to survive.

2. Gapping & nominal ellipsis (dropping the verb, or the noun)

Gapping omits a repeated verb from a second, parallel clause: the reader carries it over. Nominal ellipsis drops a repeated noun after a modifier. Both depend on a strict parallel: the missing item must line up exactly with one already given.

TypeFull formElided (economical) form
gappingShe ordered tea, and he ordered coffee.She ordered tea; he, coffee.
nominal ellipsisI kept the old letters and burned the new letters.I kept the old letters and burned the new.
after a quantifierSome guests danced; other guests just watched.Some guests danced; others, watched.
C2 subtlety
  • Do not confuse ellipsis with substitution: "the blue one" replaces the noun with one; "the blue" truly omits it. Both are cohesive, but only the second is ellipsis.
  • Gapping needs a clean parallel: "She writes novels; he, of poetry" mismatches — make it "he, poetry."

3. Clausal ellipsis & comparatives (short answers, and "than" clauses)

Clausal ellipsis strips a whole clause down to the word that carries the news — the natural shape of replies and questions. Comparative ellipsis drops the recoverable verb after than or as. Both are everyday economy, but the comparative kind is where careless cutting breeds ambiguity.

TypeFull formElided form
short answer"Will Sophie be joining us?" "Yes, Sophie will be joining us.""Will Sophie be joining us?" "Sophie will."
reduced questionWould you like still water or sparkling water?Still or sparkling?
comparativeShe earns more than he earns.She earns more than he does.
C2 subtlety
  • Comparatives can turn ambiguous when cut too far: "I like her more than you" could mean than you [like her] or than [I like] you. Restore a verb to fix the sense.
  • A short answer must have a clear antecedent question; drop it into a vacuum and the ellipsis fails.

4. Cutting redundancy & tautology (remove what says nothing new)

Economy is not only ellipsis; much waste is simply redundant wording — two words for one idea. Tautology restates a meaning already contained ("free gift", "past history"). The economical writer hunts these and cuts, not to sound curt, but to let the real words carry weight.

WastefulEconomicalWhy
at this present moment in timenowfive words doing one word's work.
a free gift given in advancea gifttautology — gifts are free, and "given" is implied.
in the event that it rainsif it rainsa padded conjunction shrunk to one word.
C2 subtlety
  • Beware hidden tautology: "advance planning", "future prospects", "each and every" all double a meaning already there.
  • Economy trims the redundant, never the necessary: cutting a qualifier that changes the meaning is not economy but error.

5. Ellipsis, register & cohesion (how far to cut, and where)

Ellipsis is a cohesive device (Unit 8): by omitting the repeated, it ties clauses together and keeps the reader's eye moving. But how much you may drop depends on register (Unit 1). Subject-dropping ("Sounds good", "Hope you're well") is fine in a note, wrong in a report; verb-phrase ellipsis and gapping travel into formal prose without a scratch.

RegisterFreely usedBest avoided
note, text, chatsubject ellipsis (Can't come), clausal ellipsisnothing much — brevity is expected
formal report, essayverb-phrase ellipsis, gapping, comparative ellipsisdropped subjects, telegram style
legal / technicalvery restrained ellipsisany cut that could blur reference
C2 subtlety
  • Ellipsis aids cohesion only when the antecedent is close and unmistakable; across a long gap the reader loses the thread.
  • The most economical prose often hides its cuts: the reader feels the flow without noticing what was left unsaid.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the economy faults that surface even in fluent C2 writing. Each is a failure of balance — too much padding, or too deep a cut. Audit your drafts against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
She can sing, and he can dances too.She can sing, and he can too.after "can", drop the verb phrase entirely; adding a mismatched verb breaks the ellipsis.
Due to the fact that it was raining, we cancelled.Because it was raining, we cancelled.a padded conjunction — four words shrunk to one, meaning intact.
Reviewed figures; found errors; corrected all.She reviewed the figures, found errors and corrected them all.over-ellipsis — dropping every subject and object reads as a telegram, not prose.
I trust her more than my classmates.I trust her more than my classmates do.ambiguity from a cut comparative — restore the verb so the sense is single.
We must plan ahead in advance for the future.We must plan ahead.tautology — "in advance" and "for the future" merely repeat "ahead".

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

A student drafts a paragraph for the class newsletter; an older friend trims it. Read it twice and watch how each note tightens a bloated line — and where the friend says that cut goes too far.

J:I wrote: "Due to the fact that the weather was disappointing, we have made the decision to postpone the sports day."Padded: "due to the fact that" and "made the decision to" carry no meaning.
E:Cut the padding: "Because the weather was bad, we postponed the sports day."Redundancy gone; the sentence says the same thing in half the words.
J:Then: "Rain fell all morning; postponed sports day; class informed; awaiting new date."Now over-cut: every subject dropped, so it reads as a telegram.
E:That's the opposite error. Restore what the reader needs: "Rain fell all morning, so we postponed the sports day and told the class."Ellipsis of the repeated subject is fine; dropping all of them is not.
J:Can I keep "The Year 7 team trained harder than the Year 8"?She fears it may be ambiguous.
E:No — add the verb: "harder than the Year 8 did." Otherwise it could mean they trained the Year 8.A cut comparative that breeds ambiguity must be restored.
Test yourself
  • Why is "Because the results disappointed" better than "Due to the fact that the results were disappointing"? (It cuts a five-word padded conjunction and a weak passive to say the same thing — pure economy.)
  • What made the telegram draft ("Rain fell all morning… postponed sports day…") fail? (Over-ellipsis: dropping every subject and connector strips the register and the flow; economy became obscurity.)
  • Why must "harder than the Year 8" become "harder than the Year 8 did"? (The bare comparative is ambiguous — restoring the verb fixes the single intended meaning.)

The Art of Leaving Out

Great writing is often praised for what it contains — the vivid image, the exact word, the well-turned clause. Yet some of its finest effects come from what it leaves out. To write with economy is to trust the reader: to say a thing once and let it stand, to trim every word that earns nothing, and to let a little silence carry part of the meaning. The plainest sentence, stripped of its padding, can strike harder than a paragraph of ornament.

The impulse to prune is old. Classical rhetoricians prized brevitas, the virtue of saying much in little, and generations of stylists since have echoed the same counsel in their own idiom — omit the needless word, distrust the adjective, let the verb do the work. Behind the advice lies a shared conviction: that prose thickened with unnecessary matter does not enrich a thought but buries it. The reader, wading through qualification and repetition, loses the thread the writer meant to hand across. A lean sentence, by contrast, arrives whole. It carries its meaning to the surface rather than sinking it, and it flatters the reader's intelligence by assuming that a single, well-placed word will land. Concision, understood this way, is less a rule of grammar than a habit of mind — a willingness to interrogate every phrase and to spare only those that earn their keep.

The grammar of omission has a name: ellipsis. When we say "She can sing, and he can too," we do not repeat "sing" — the reader supplies it. When a waiter asks "Still or sparkling?" no one misses the absent "Would you like still water or sparkling water?" Ellipsis works because language is full of the recoverable: whatever the context has already made clear need not be said again. This is not laziness but courtesy, sparing the reader a second helping of the obvious.

The varieties of omission are more numerous than they first appear. There is gapping, in which a repeated verb quietly vanishes: "She chose the coast; he, the mountains." There is nominal ellipsis, where a noun already named is left implied — "I read both reports and preferred the shorter." There is the whole clause held back because the situation supplies it, as when a reply of "Gladly" answers an unspoken "Yes, I will." In each case the omitted material does not disappear so much as migrate into the reader's mind, which reconstructs it without effort or even awareness. This quiet collaboration is the deep secret of the device: the writer supplies a frame, and the reader, drawing on the antecedent, fills it. Meaning is not lost in the gap; it is completed there. What looks like absence on the page is, in the act of reading, a presence conjured by inference.

Economy is ellipsis's wider cousin. Where ellipsis omits whole grammatical slots, economy prunes the merely redundant — the "past history," the "advance planning," the "in my personal opinion, I think." Each doubles a meaning already present. The economical writer hunts such phrases and cuts them, not to sound curt, but to let the real words breathe. Brevity, handled well, is a form of respect.

Modern prose offers a rich field for the economical pen, for it teems with phrases that say nothing twice. The report that is "completely finished," the plan drawn up through "advance planning," the crisis met with an "urgent emergency" — each pairs a word with its own echo. Institutional language is especially prone to this swelling, favouring the long Latinate construction where a short native verb would serve, and hedging every claim in a fog of qualification until the reader can no longer tell what is being asserted. Such padding is rarely deliberate. It creeps in when a writer mistakes bulk for substance, or reaches for a ready-made phrase instead of the precise one. The remedy is patient revision: to read back what one has written with a cold eye, asking of each clause whether the sentence would lose anything by its removal. Very often it would not, and the cut, once made, is never mourned.

There is, besides, a music in omission that the ear registers even when the mind does not pause to name it. A short sentence after a long one lands like a struck note; the white space around a spare line lends it weight. Poets have always known that what is withheld can resonate more than what is stated, that the unsaid casts a shadow the said cannot. The same principle governs the finest prose. A paragraph that ends a beat sooner than expected leaves the reader leaning forward, completing the cadence in silence. To leave out, then, is not merely to save space or spare the reader labour; it is to shape the rhythm of thought, to control the pace at which meaning unfolds, and to grant the sentence room in which to breathe and be heard.

None of this comes without cost to the writer, for cutting is harder than adding. It is easy to pile on clause after clause, each seeming to clarify the last; it is far more demanding to strike out a line that took an hour to compose. The habit must be learned against instinct, for we grow fond of our own phrases and mistake fondness for merit. Seasoned writers speak of the discipline almost as a moral one — the readiness to sacrifice the clever flourish for the sake of the whole, to serve the reader rather than the ego. The finished piece bears no trace of what was removed; the reader sees only the clean line and never the debris left on the workshop floor. Yet that invisible labour is precisely what distinguishes prose that has been written from prose that has merely been assembled.

For all its virtues, economy is not an end in itself, and the writer who worships it can be led astray as surely as the one who ignores it. Words exist to carry meaning, and a sentence stripped past the point of clarity has bought its elegance too dear. The goal is never fewer words for their own sake but the fewest words that do the work — a distinction easy to state and hard to hold. Between the bloated and the bare lies a narrow path, and finding it is the labour of a lifetime. The writer walks it sentence by sentence, gaining ground here, giving it back there, never quite arriving. What guides the step is judgement rather than any rule: a trained sense of how much a reader needs and how little will suffice.

Yet omission has its limits. Leave out too much, and the reader stumbles. "I admire her more than my classmates" hides its meaning: more than the classmates admire her, or more than the writer admires the classmates? Here economy has overreached, and clarity pays the price. The skilled writer therefore weighs each cut. Say less, but never at the cost of sense; leave out the predictable, never the necessary. The art of leaving out is, in the end, the art of knowing exactly what must stay.

Sample Answer (~220 words) Homework should go. Not be reduced, not be reformed — go. After six hours in class, children have learned enough for one day, and their teachers have too.

The case for economy here is simple. Homework was meant to reinforce learning; too often, it merely fills evenings. Some pupils cope; others, not at all. The confident child finishes in twenty minutes; the anxious one, in tears. When a task divides pupils by their home rather than their effort, it teaches inequality more than algebra.

Defenders say homework builds discipline. Perhaps — but so does a well-run lesson, and with far less waste. The evidence for its academic benefit, especially in primary years, is thinner than most assume. Where supporters see rigour, I see redundancy: the same skills, drilled twice, for half the gain.

None of this means abolishing effort. A short daily reading habit helps; a nightly worksheet, hardly at all. The distinction matters, and the wording must stay clear: I would keep reading, and cut the rest. Say less, but never at the cost of learning.

So let schools be brave and concise. Set less, expect more, and give families back their evenings. The best lesson we could teach is the one this whole argument rests on: know exactly what to leave out, and leave the rest well alone.
