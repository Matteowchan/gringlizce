Giving OpinionsGörüş Bildirme

Görüş bildirme: I think, In my opinion, I'd say, As far as I'm concerned, If you ask me. Kesinlik derecesi ve resmiyet (I reckon [gündelik] vs In my view [resmî]) bağlama göre seçilir; sınav yazımında güçlü tez cümlesi için de temeldir.

Giving opinions: I think, In my opinion, I'd say, As far as I'm concerned, If you ask me. The degree of certainty and the level of formality (I reckon [casual] vs In my view [formal]) are chosen to fit the context; it is also the basis of a strong thesis statement in exam writing.

Görüş bildirme (giving opinions), bir konu hakkındaki kişisel düşünceni açıkça ve doğru tonda ortaya koymaktır: I think, In my opinion, I'd say, As far as I'm concerned, If you ask me. İngilizcede görüş, çoğu zaman cümlenin başına eklenen bir kalıp (opinion marker) ile işaretlenir ve bu kalıbın seçimi hem kesinlik derecesini (I'm sure > I think > I suppose) hem de resmiyeti (I reckon [gündelik] < I think < In my view [resmî]) belirler. Bu konu, sınav yazımında güçlü bir tez cümlesi kurmanın da temelidir. Görüş bildirmenin doğal ikizleri, karşındakiyle uyuşup uyuşmadığını söylemek Katılma & Karşı Çıkma ve öneri/tavsiye vermektir Öneri & Tavsiye — üçü birlikte tartışma dilinin çekirdeğini oluşturur.

Giving opinions means putting your personal view on a topic clearly and in the right tone: I think, In my opinion, I'd say, As far as I'm concerned, If you ask me. In English an opinion is usually marked with a pattern (opinion marker) placed at the start of the sentence, and the choice of that pattern determines both the degree of certainty (I'm sure > I think > I suppose) and the formality (I reckon [casual] < I think < In my view [formal]). This topic is also the basis for building a strong thesis statement in exam writing. The natural twins of giving opinions are saying whether or not you agree with the other person Agreeing & Disagreeing and making suggestions and giving advice Suggestions & Advice — together the three form the core of the language of discussion.

Görüş bildirme, dünyayı olduğu gibi anlatmakla (The film is two hours long — bir olgu) yetinmeyip ona kendi değerlendirmeni eklemektir (I think the film is too long — bir görüş). Yani cümleye küçük bir işaret koyarak dinleyene şunu dersin: Bu, herkesin kabul ettiği bir gerçek değil; bu benim bakış açım. İngilizce bu işareti çoğunlukla cümlenin başına konan bir kalıpla verir: I think, In my opinion, I'd say gibi.

Peki bu neden ayrı bir 'konu' olacak kadar önemli? Çünkü İngilizcede görüşünü nasıl söylediğin, ne söylediğin kadar bilgi taşır. Aynı fikri buz gibi kesin (I'm certain it's wrong), ölçülü (I think it's wrong) ya da çekingen (I suppose it might be wrong) söyleyebilirsin. Seçtiğin kalıp, ne kadar emin olduğunu ve ortamın ne kadar resmî olduğunu ele verir. Bu yüzden görüş bildirme, hem günlük sohbetin hem de sınav kompozisyonundaki tez cümlesinin temelidir.

Türkçedeki karşılığı: Türkçede bu işi çoğunlukla tek kelime yapar — bence, bana göre, kanımca, bana kalırsa. İngilizce ise genelde birkaç kelimelik bir kalıp kullanır (in my opinion) ve bu kalıp cümleye ayrı durur, fiili değiştirmez. İyi haber: mantık Türkçeyle neredeyse birebir örtüşür, o yüzden kavram sana yabancı gelmez.

Neden Türk öğrenciye zor gelir? Üç sebep: (1) Türkçede tek kelimeyle hallettiğin şey İngilizcede kalıp seçmeyi gerektirir ve yanlış kalıp yanlış ton verir (I reckon sınavda tuhaf durur). (2) Olumsuz görüşte Türkçe 'Bence gelmeyecek' derken İngilizce 'I don't think he'll come' diye olumsuzluğu öne çeker — birebir çeviri (I think he won't come) doğal değildir. (3) According to me gibi Türkçe 'bana göre'yi kelime kelime çeviren bir tuzak vardır ve bu İngilizcede yanlıştır. Bu konu tam da bu farkları oturtmak için var.

"Görüşünü nasıl söylediğin, ne söylediğin kadar bilgi taşır: seçtiğin kalıp hem ne kadar emin olduğunu hem de ortamın ne kadar resmî olduğunu ele verir.

Giving an opinion means not settling for describing the world as it is (The film is two hours long — a fact) but adding your own evaluation to it (I think the film is too long — an opinion). In other words, by placing a small marker on the sentence you tell the listener: This is not a fact everyone accepts; this is my point of view. English usually gives this marker with a pattern placed at the start of the sentence: like I think, In my opinion, I'd say.

So why is this important enough to be a separate 'topic'? Because in English how you say your opinion carries as much information as what you say. You can state the same idea ice-cold and certain (I'm certain it's wrong), measured (I think it's wrong), or hesitant (I suppose it might be wrong). The pattern you choose gives away how sure you are and how formal the setting is. That is why giving an opinion is the basis of both everyday conversation and the thesis statement in an exam essay.

The Turkish equivalent: in Turkish a single word usually does this job — bence, bana göre, kanımca, bana kalırsa. English, by contrast, generally uses a pattern of a few words (in my opinion), and this pattern stands apart from the sentence; it doesn't change the verb. The good news: the logic overlaps almost exactly with Turkish, so the concept won't feel foreign to you.

Why does it feel hard to Turkish learners? Three reasons: (1) What you handle with a single word in Turkish requires choosing a pattern in English, and the wrong pattern gives the wrong tone (I reckon looks odd in an exam). (2) In a negative opinion, where Turkish says 'Bence gelmeyecek', English pulls the negative to the front as 'I don't think he'll come' — a word-for-word translation (I think he won't come) isn't natural. (3) There is a trap of translating the Turkish 'bana göre' word for word as According to me, and this is wrong in English. This topic exists precisely to settle these differences.

Grammatically, giving an opinion requires no new tense or conjugation; it adds an opinion pattern to the start (sometimes the end) of an existing sentence. So 'structure' here covers three things: (1) building an affirmative opinion, (2) building a negative opinion (English's critical difference), (3) asking an opinion question and giving a short answer.

Affirmative opinion

Formula: [opinion pattern] + [full sentence]. The conjunction that is often dropped (especially in speech). After patterns that come at the start, like In my opinion, a comma is used.

I think (that) this book is excellent.
Bence bu kitap harika.
I think + sentence; 'that' is optional and usually drops.
In my opinion, the price is too high.
Bence fiyat çok yüksek.
Front pattern + comma + sentence.
I'd say it's the best option we have.
Bence elimizdeki en iyi seçenek (derim).
I'd say = an opinion softened and made cautious with 'would'.
The service, in my view, was disappointing.
Hizmet, bence, hayal kırıklığıydı.
The pattern can also slip into the middle of the sentence between commas — a more written tone.

Negative opinion

English's most critical habit: if you think something won't happen / isn't true, you move the negative onto the verb think — I don't think + an affirmative clause. Turkish says 'Bence gelmeyecek'; English says 'I don't think he'll come'. 'I think he won't come' is not grammatically wrong, but it isn't natural and looks weak in an exam.

I don't think it's a good idea.
Bence iyi bir fikir değil.
The natural pattern: the negative goes on 'think'. NOT: I think it's not a good idea (weak).
I don't think they'll agree.
Bence kabul etmezler.
Turkish 'etmezler' — English pulls the negative to the front.
In my opinion, this isn't worth it.
Bence buna değmez.
With In my opinion, the negative stays in the normal clause (the 'don't think' rule is only for think/believe/suppose).
Wrong I think he won't pass the exam.
Correct I don't think he'll pass the exam.
Bence sınavı geçemeyecek.
With opinion verbs like think, believe, suppose, imagine, English pulls the negative onto the main verb (don't think). 'I think + won't' feels odd and weak to a native speaker.

Opinion question and short answer

The most common way to ask for an opinion is the pattern What do you think (of/about) ...? of is usually used with a noun (of the film), about with a topic/situation (about the plan) — the two are interchangeable in most contexts. Short answers are given with the opinion verb.

Opinion patterns don't serve a single purpose; which one you choose signals your intention. Below are the main functions, ordered from the most common to the more specific.

Certainty

Sets how sure you are: I'm sure > I think > I suppose. The lower rung is cautious, the upper rung assertive.

Formality

Sets the tone of the setting: I reckon casual, I think neutral, In my view formal.

Personalness

Shows how much you own the opinion: Personally / For me foregrounds it, It could be argued pulls it back.

1) Neutral, everyday opinion (most common)

I think we should leave now.
Bence artık gitmeliyiz.
I think = safe in any setting, the neutral default.
I feel this is the wrong approach.
Bence bu yanlış bir yaklaşım.
I feel = a slightly more personal/emotional tone than I think, still neutral.
For me, comfort matters more than style.
Benim için konfor, tarzdan daha önemli.
For me = very natural when stating a personal priority/taste.

2) Formal or written opinion (essay, report, presentation)

In my opinion, the policy needs revising.
Bence bu politikanın gözden geçirilmesi gerekiyor.
In my opinion = the standard pattern for written and formal settings.
From my point of view, both sides share the blame.
Benim bakış açımdan iki taraf da suçlu.
From my point of view = stresses that you are offering a perspective, staying balanced in a debate.
I would argue that education is the real solution.
Ben eğitimin asıl çözüm olduğunu savunurdum.
I would argue that = B2/C1; presents the opinion as a 'claim', adding academic force.

3) Drawing a strong personal stance

As far as I'm concerned, the matter is closed.
Bana kalırsa mesele kapanmıştır.
As far as I'm concerned = a 'whatever anyone else says, this is how I see it' tone; firmness.
If you ask me, they're making a big mistake.
Bana sorarsan büyük bir hata yapıyorlar.
If you ask me = giving a strong opinion unasked; mildly critical/familiar.
Personally, I wouldn't trust that company.
Şahsen ben o şirkete güvenmezdim.
Personally = stresses that the opinion is personal, not imposed on everyone.

4) Cautious / soft opinion (avoiding certainty)

I suppose it could work, but I'm not sure.
Sanırım işe yarayabilir ama emin değilim.
I suppose = reluctant/cautious acceptance; low certainty.
I guess we could try it.
Herhalde deneyebiliriz.
I guess = very common in American English, casual caution.
It seems to me that we're missing something.
Bana öyle geliyor ki bir şeyi kaçırıyoruz.
It seems to me = an impression-based, polite opinion.

5) Casual/familiar opinion (chatting with friends)

I reckon it'll sell out fast.
Bence çabuk tükenir.
I reckon = mostly British/Australian English, familiar; don't use it in writing.
If you ask me, that's a rip-off.
Bana sorarsan bu tam bir kazık.
Natural, relaxed style together with a casual word (rip-off).

6) Academic / detached opinion (pulling back the personal)

It could be argued that the benefits are exaggerated.
Faydaların abartıldığı öne sürülebilir.
C1; presents an opinion without saying 'I' — academic neutrality.
There is a strong case for banning it entirely.
Tümden yasaklamak için güçlü bir gerekçe var.
Objectifies the opinion as a 'case' (reason).
The evidence suggests that this is not the case.
Kanıtlar bunun böyle olmadığını gösteriyor.
Presenting the opinion tied to evidence; the strongest academic move.
Tips & Tricks: three patterns that open an opinion essay
Don't waste time on the first sentence of an exam essay; build your thesis statement directly with one of these three patterns: (1) In my opinion, ... (2) This essay will argue that ... (3) I firmly believe that ... A weak opening ('In this essay I will talk about...') loses marks; a clear opinion pattern gives the reader your position in the first second. Key trick: write the thesis statement by echoing the words of the exam question — that way you reduce the risk of 'going off topic' to zero.

As a concept, giving an opinion fits Turkish very well, but it hides serious traps at the level of word choice and the place of the negative. Settling the following differences one by one cuts out most direct-translation errors.

  • Turkish marks an opinion with a single word (bence, bana göre, kanımca); English usually uses a separate pattern of a few words (in my opinion) and this pattern doesn't change the verb.
  • The Turkish 'bana göre' is not According to me: according to is used only for others/sources (according to the report). For your own opinion, In my opinion / For me / Personally.
  • Turkish keeps the negative opinion in the main clause (Bence gelmeyecek); English pulls the negative onto the opinion verb (I don't think he'll come).
  • Turkish 'düşünüyorum' can be both 'I suppose' (an opinion) and 'I'm pondering' (an action); English distinguishes I think (that) for an opinion and I'm thinking about for an action.
  • Turkish 'ne düşünüyorsun?' can ask for both an abstract view and a passing thought; English prefers the pattern What do you think of/about...? for an opinion.
CAUTION

The Turkish 'bana göre' is never According to me. According to only reports an external source (according to the report); for your own opinion use In my opinion / For me / Personally.

Concept check Answer each question by thinking about the meaning.
  1. What is the difference between 'I don't think it'll rain' and 'I think it won't rain', and which is natural?
    The meaning is almost the same ('I think it won't rain'), but English pulls the negative onto think; so 'I don't think it'll rain' is natural. The second one looks weak and odd.
  2. Can you put 'I reckon it's great' in a university essay? Why?
    No. I reckon is a casual/familiar pattern; in academic writing it creates a register clash. Use In my view / It could be argued that instead.
  3. Is there a tone difference between 'In my opinion' and 'From my point of view'?
    Yes. In my opinion is a direct claim; From my point of view implies that the opinion is one perspective and that other views may exist — more conciliatory.
  4. Why is 'According to me' wrong?
    According to only reports external sources (according to the report). It isn't used for your own opinion; the word-for-word translation of 'bana göre' causes this mistake. The correct forms: In my opinion / For me / Personally.
  5. Do 'I suppose so' and 'Definitely' confirm with the same certainty?
    No. I suppose so is a hesitant, reluctant confirmation (low certainty); Definitely is a full, enthusiastic confirmation (high certainty). The tone flips.
  6. In a short answer, why do we say 'I think so' and not 'I think yes'?
    An English short opinion answer uses 'so' (I think so / I don't think so). 'I think yes/no' is wrong to a native; the same pattern holds in I hope so, I'm afraid so.

Where to start, what to stress
At A2, start with only two patterns: I think and In my opinion. Don't add new patterns before these are settled; students usually drown in an abundance of patterns. From day one, reinforce the I don't think rule with a physical gesture ('pulling the negative to the front') — it is the habit hardest to correct later. At B1, visualize the degrees of certainty (I'm sure / I suppose) by drawing a 'thermometer'. At B2, pair the formality axis with real settings: 'Would you say this to your boss or to your friend?'

Concept-check questions (measure that the student has truly understood): (1) Are 'I don't think it'll rain' and 'I think it won't rain' the same, and which is natural? (2) Why is 'According to me' wrong? (3) Would you put 'I reckon' in an exam essay, and why? (4) Is 'I suppose so' an enthusiastic confirmation? These questions test not the rule but the distinction; they check understanding, not memorization.

Class activity (production): 'Opinion Line' — one end of the class is 'I strongly agree', the other end 'I strongly disagree'. Say a controversial sentence (Homework should be banned); students place themselves on the line according to their stance and give an opinion with a certainty pattern that matches their chosen position. Those in the middle must use I suppose / it could be argued, those at the ends I'm convinced / As far as I'm concerned — so the certainty-pattern pairing is reinforced physically. As the single most common mistake, keep returning insistently to the place of the negative (I don't think).

  • Giving an opinion = presenting your view while marking it as your point of view, not a fact; a pattern is usually added to the start of the sentence.
  • The default neutral pattern is I think (that); for formal/written use In my opinion, (with a comma).
  • The choice of pattern sets two axes: certainty (I'm convinced > I think > I suppose) and formality (I reckon < I think < In my view).
  • English's golden rule: in a negative opinion pull the negative onto the verb — I don't think it'll work (not I think + won't).
  • Turkish traps: According to me (wrong — In my opinion), I am agree (wrong — I agree), I think yes (wrong — I think so).
  • Casual patterns (I reckon, If you ask me, I guess) are natural in friendly chat but avoided in academic writing; there It could be argued that / I would argue that is strong.
  • Deliberately lowering certainty (hedging: I suppose, I tend to think, perhaps) adds politeness and maturity; don't present every opinion ice-cold and certain.
  • An opinion is the basis of a strong thesis statement in an exam essay; take a clear position with a clear pattern.
  • Twin functions: agreeing/disagreeing Agreeing & Disagreeing and suggestions/advice Suggestions & Advice — together with opinion they build the language of discussion.
