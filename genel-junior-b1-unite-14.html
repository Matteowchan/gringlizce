Grammar: Question Forms

Study the rules, look at the examples, then do the exercises.

Asking questions like a confident speaker

You have been asking questions in English since your very first lesson: What is your name? Where do you live? Did you sleep well? These are direct questions, and you know them well. At B1, you learn to ask questions that are more polite, more precise and more natural — the way real speakers do.

This unit covers three tools. First, indirect questions (Could you tell me where the station is?) — the polite, softer way to ask. Second, the difference between subject and object questions (Who called you? vs Who did you call?). Third, question tags (It's cold, isn't it?) — the little endings that turn a statement into a question.

  • Where is the library?Direct question — clear but a little abrupt.
  • Could you tell me where the library is?Indirect question — polite; notice the statement word order.
  • You like drawing, don't you?A question tag checks something you already believe.

1. Direct questions: a quick review (the starting point)

A direct question inverts the subject and the auxiliary verb (be, do, have, can, will...). The pattern is: (question word) + auxiliary + subject + main verb.

Question wordAuxiliarySubjectVerb
Wheredoyoulive?
Whathasshedone?
—Cantheyswim?
Key point
  • In the present and past simple, the auxiliary is do / does / did, and the main verb goes back to its base form: Where did you go? ✓ Where did you went? ✗
  • Keep this pattern in mind — the rest of the unit is about changing it.

2. Indirect questions (the polite way — main focus)

When we want to be polite — with strangers, in shops, at reception, in a formal email — we often "wrap" the question inside a longer, gentler phrase. This is an indirect question.

The golden rule: after the opening phrase, use statement word order (subject + verb) and drop do / does / did. There is no inversion.

Direct questionIndirect question
Where is the gym?Could you tell me where the gym is?
What time does the film start?Do you know what time the film starts?
Why did she leave?I wonder why she left.

Common polite openers: Could you tell me...? · Do you know...? · Do you have any idea...? · I wonder...? · Can I ask...?

Yes/No questions → use if / whether
  • If the direct question has no question word (it is a yes/no question), join it with if or whether: Is the library open? → Do you know if the library is open?
  • Still statement order, still no do: Does he play football? → Do you know if he plays football? ✓

3. Subject vs object questions (Who called? / Who did you call?)

Some questions ask about the subject (the doer of the action); others ask about the object (the person or thing that receives the action). English treats them very differently.

TypeWe ask about...Example
Subject questionwho / what does the action → NO do/didWho called you? (Someone called you.)
Object questionwho / what receives the action → USE do/didWho did you call? (You called someone.)
  • Who broke the window?"Who" is the subject → no did, verb stays in the past: broke.
  • What did you break?"What" is the object → use did + base verb: break.
  • Who wants some cake?Subject question in the present → add -s, no does: wants.
Quick test
  • Try to answer with the subject. If the answer is "He did / My friend did" (the doer), it is a subject question → no do/did.
  • If the answer names the thing acted on ("I called my sister"), it is an object question → use do/did.

4. Question tags (isn't it?, do you?, will you?)

A question tag is a short question added to the end of a statement, usually to check information or invite agreement. The rule is a mirror: a positive statement takes a negative tag, and a negative statement takes a positive tag. Use the same auxiliary as the statement.

StatementTagFull sentence
You are tired,aren't you?You are tired, aren't you?
She doesn't like maths,does she?She doesn't like maths, does she?
They have arrived,haven't they?They have arrived, haven't they?
He will help,won't he?He will help, won't he?
Watch out
  • No auxiliary in the statement? Use do / does / did: You live here, don't you?
  • Imperatives usually take will you? (Close the door, will you?) and "Let's..." takes shall we? (Let's start, shall we?).

5. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B1 students make most often with question forms. Check your own speaking and writing against this list.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
Could you tell me where is the library?Could you tell me where the library is?Indirect question → statement word order, no inversion.
Do you know what time does it start?Do you know what time it starts?Drop "do/does" in indirect questions.
Do you know that the library is open?Do you know if the library is open?Yes/No indirect question needs "if" or "whether".
Who did paint this picture?Who painted this picture?Subject question → no "did".
You are Turkish, aren't you not?You are Turkish, aren't you?Positive statement → simple negative tag.

6. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

A new student asks for help on the first day at school. Read it twice and notice the polite indirect questions and the friendly question tags.

NewExcuse me, could you tell me where the art room is?Indirect question → "the art room is", no inversion.
BuddyOf course. It's just upstairs. You're the new student, aren't you?Positive statement → negative tag "aren't you?".
NewYes. Do you know if the art club is free today?Yes/No indirect question → "if" + statement order.
BuddyIt is. By the way, who showed you around this morning?Subject question → "Who showed", no "did".
NewThe head teacher did. Can I ask what time the club starts?Polite opener + indirect order "the club starts".
Test yourself
  • Find the indirect yes/no question. Which word joins it? ("if" — "if the art club is free".)
  • "Who showed you around?" — why no "did"? ("Who" is the subject, the doer.)
  • Turn "You're the new student" into a full tag question. (You're the new student, aren't you?)

Speaking: Asking & Interviewing

Learn the words, then use them to ask your own questions.

ask (v)
to say a question or make a request (sormak)
Syn: enquire · Ant: answer
"May I ask you a question about your hobby?"
reply (v)
to answer someone in words or writing (yanıtlamak)
Syn: respond · Ant: ignore
"Please reply to my note soon."
wonder (v)
to want to know; to ask yourself something (merak etmek)
Syn: be curious · Ant: know
"I wonder why my friend was late for school."
curious (adj)
wanting to learn or know about something (meraklı)
Syn: interested · Ant: indifferent
"Children are naturally curious about everything."
polite (adj)
showing good manners and respect (kibar, nazik)
Syn: courteous · Ant: rude
"It is polite to ask indirect questions."
request (n)
a polite act of asking for something (rica, talep)
Syn: appeal · Ant: demand
"I have a small request for my teacher."
permission (n)
agreement that lets you do something (izin)
Syn: consent · Ant: refusal
"Could I have permission to feed the class hamster?"
clarify (v)
to make something clearer and easier to understand (netleştirmek)
Syn: explain · Ant: confuse
"Could you clarify the homework, please?"
confirm (v)
to say that something is definitely true (teyit etmek)
Syn: verify · Ant: deny
"Can you confirm the time of the match?"
interview (n)
a formal meeting with questions and answers (mülakat, röportaj)
Syn: meeting · Ant: —
"My interview for the school magazine was fun."
information (n)
facts or details about something (bilgi)
Syn: details · Ant: —
"I need more information about the school trip."
whether (conj)
introduces a yes/no choice (olup olmadığı)
Syn: if · Ant: —
"I don't know whether my friend is coming to the party."
honest (adj)
telling the truth; not lying (dürüst)
Syn: truthful · Ant: dishonest
"Be honest when you answer the questions."

Reading: The Art of Good Questions

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think about yourself. Are you a curious person? What is one question you would love to ask a famous person?
  • The text is about a girl who writes for her school magazine. What skills do you think a great interviewer needs?
  • Look at the title, "The Art of Good Questions". Do you think good questions come naturally, or can they be learned?
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text for the answers. Notice the polite, indirect question inside the text.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): ask · wonder · curious · polite · interview · information · clarify · confirm

The right question can open any conversation.
The Art of Good Questions: Elif's Story

Elif is eleven years old, and she writes for the magazine at her school in Izmir. Everyone knows her, because she asks the best questions in the whole school. 'A good question can change a whole conversation,' she often says. Before every interview, she sits at her desk and writes her questions in an old notebook. 'When I write a question by hand, I think about it more carefully,' she explains. She always asks herself three things: is this question clear? Is it kind? Will it help the other person to talk? For Elif, the best interviews do not feel like tests; they feel like a friendly chat between two people.

Elif was not always so brave. When she was younger, she was a shy child, and she rarely asked questions in class. However, everything changed when she was eight. She joined the school newspaper club, and her very first interview was with the head teacher. Although she was very nervous, she prepared twenty questions the night before, so the interview went smoothly. Her teacher, Mr Yildiz, told her that every good reporter starts as a beginner, and that mistakes are simply part of learning. Slowly, Elif grew more confident, and soon writing questions felt natural.

Since that day, Elif has interviewed many people. She has talked to the school cook, the football coach, a local artist, and even her own grandparents. Last summer, while she was at summer camp, she also learned an important lesson. She met children from many different towns, and she quickly learned that a direct question could sometimes sound rude. Polite forms, such as 'Could you tell me why you chose that?', made new friends feel more comfortable. Elif wrote these useful phrases in her notebook and practised them until they felt natural.

Some interviews have stayed in Elif's memory for a long time. Once, she spent a whole afternoon with an old fisherman who lived near her grandmother's house. He was quiet at first, but Elif did not rush him. She asked gentle questions about his boat and his childhood, and soon he was telling wonderful stories. 'Ordinary people often have the most interesting lives,' she says. 'You just need to give them time and ask the right questions.'

Of course, not every interview is easy. Elif still remembers one shy new classmate who gave very short, cold answers. At first she felt worried, but she stayed calm and changed her plan. Instead of asking about school, she asked about his pet dog. Suddenly the boy smiled and began to talk freely. 'People love to talk about the things they care about,' Elif says. 'If one question does not work, you must be ready to try another.'

Elif believes that curiosity is a skill. 'People think reporters are born with it, but that is not true,' she explains. 'I improved my questions because I practised every day.' She still gets nervous before a big interview, but she has never lost her love of asking questions. Her main goal has not changed: to help people share their stories. These days, Elif also helps younger children who want to write for the magazine. She always gives them the same advice with a smile: 'Be curious, be polite, and always listen more than you speak.'

Writing: Someone I Would Like to Interview

Write and submit your paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Connectors turn sentences into a text (Linking Words)

A list of short, separate sentences is not real writing. What connects your ideas is linking words. At A2 you used and, but, so, because. At B1 you add four more powerful connectors: also, when, while, although and however. These let you show contrast, background events and extra information like a confident writer.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
andadd informationI am curious about her life, and I have many questions.
butshow a small contrastThe question is personal, but I would ask it politely.
soshow a resultI admire her work, so I would love to interview her.
becausegive a reasonI would ask that because I want to understand her choice.
alsoadd an extra pointI would ask about her childhood. I would also ask about her family.
whensay at what past timeWhen she was young, what did she dream of?
whiletwo things at the same timeWhile she was young, did she ever feel afraid?
althoughshow a strong contrastAlthough I am shy, I would enjoy the interview.
howevercontrast between two sentencesI have never interviewed anyone. However, I would try my best.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different connectors in your paragraph. Do not write sentence by sentence; link your ideas.
  • When when / while / although / however start a sentence, they are usually followed by a comma: "However, I would try my best."

Use polite indirect questions (Apply the grammar)

This is a writing task about questions, so show off this unit's grammar. Instead of only writing direct questions, wrap some of them in polite, indirect forms — they sound more natural in writing.

Direct (simple) ✗Indirect (better in writing) ✓
What was your childhood like?I would ask her what her childhood was like.
Did you ever feel afraid?I would like to know whether she ever felt afraid.
Do you have any regrets?I wonder if she has any regrets.

Sample Answer (~165 words) There are many interesting people in the world, but if I could interview anyone, I would choose my grandmother. She has lived through so many changes, and she has wonderful stories to tell. Although she is now eighty years old, she still remembers her childhood clearly.

First, I would ask her what life was like when she was young. I am curious to know how people communicated before mobile phones, so I would ask her to describe an ordinary day. I would also like to find out whether she ever felt afraid during difficult times.

Then I would ask her about her biggest achievement. I wonder if she has any regrets, but I would ask this politely, because it is a personal question. Finally, I would ask her what advice she has for young people today.

I have never interviewed anyone before. However, I think my grandmother would enjoy it, and I would learn a lot from her answers.
