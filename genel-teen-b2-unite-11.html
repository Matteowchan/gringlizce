Ünite 11 · Advanced Reported Speech · Bölüm 1/6

Grammar: Advanced Reported Speech

Go beyond "say" and "tell": report the meaning of what people do with their words.

Beyond "say" and "tell"

At B1 you reported speech mostly with say and tell: "She said (that) she was tired." That works, but it is flat. At B2, good speakers report the function of an utterance — whether someone advised, warned, admitted, refused or threatened. One precise verb replaces a whole clause and shows exactly what the speaker was doing.

The challenge is that each of these verbs demands its own grammatical pattern. Some take an object + a to-infinitive; some take the -ing form; some take a preposition; some take a that-clause. Choose the wrong pattern and the sentence collapses. This unit sorts the most useful reporting verbs into clear families so that the pattern comes to you automatically.

  • "Don't forget your passport." → She reminded me to take my passport.reminding function → verb + object + to-infinitive.
  • "I broke the window." → He admitted breaking the window.confession → verb + -ing.
  • "I'll help you, I promise." → She promised to help.promise → verb + to-infinitive.
  • "I'm sorry I was late." → He apologised for being late.apology → verb + preposition + -ing.

1. The patterns at a glance (the five families)

Every reporting verb belongs to a pattern family. Learn the family, and you can report dozens of situations without hesitating.

PatternReporting verbsExample
verb + sb + to-infadvise, warn, remind, encourage, tell, askShe advised me to rest.
verb + to-infoffer, promise, refuse, agree, threatenHe promised to call.
verb + -ingadmit, deny, suggest, recommendThey denied cheating.
verb + prep + -ingapologise for, insist onI apologised for being late.
verb + that-clauseadmit, insist, suggest, promise, recommendHe insisted that she stay.
Key point
  • Some verbs live in two families: admit, suggest and recommend take both -ing and a that-clause — but never object + to-infinitive.
  • The verb tells you the pattern, not the situation. Learn advise + sb + to, suggest + -ing / that as fixed shapes.

2. verb + somebody + to-infinitive (directing others)

Use this family when someone directs, guides or warns another person. There is always an object (the person spoken to) before to.

VerbOriginal wordsReported
advise"You should see a doctor."She advised him to see a doctor.
warn"Don't touch the wire!"He warned us not to touch the wire.
remind"Remember to lock the door."She reminded me to lock the door.
encourage"Go on, apply for it!"They encouraged her to apply.
Negatives — where does "not" go?
  • For a negative order or warning, put not before to: "He warned me not to go." ✓ — "warned me to not go" ✗ and "warned me don't go" ✗.
  • The person spoken to is obligatory: "advised me to rest", not "advised to rest me".

3. verb + to-infinitive (speaking about yourself)

These verbs report what the speaker promises, offers, refuses or threatens to do. There is no object before to — the subject and the doer are the same person.

  • "I'll drive you." → He offered to drive me.offer + to-infinitive (no object before "to").
  • "I promise I'll finish it." → She promised to finish it.promise + to-infinitive.
  • "No, I won't sign." → He refused to sign.refuse + to-infinitive (a firm 'no').
  • "Pay me, or I'll sue you." → She threatened to sue him.threaten + to-infinitive.
Compare the two "to" families
  • Object present → directing someone: She advised me to leave (I leave).
  • No object → talking about the speaker: She offered to leave (she leaves). Mixing these up is the classic B2 error.

4. verb + -ing (admitting, denying, suggesting)

This family reports confessions, denials and suggestions. The verb is followed directly by the -ing form — never by a to-infinitive.

VerbOriginal wordsReported
admit"Yes, I took it."He admitted taking it.
deny"I didn't do it."She denied doing it.
suggest"Let's wait a week."He suggested waiting a week.
recommend"You should book early."She recommended booking early.

Two more verbs need a preposition before the -ing form:

  • "Sorry I forgot." → He apologised for forgetting.apologise for + -ing.
  • "You must come with us." → She insisted on coming with us.insist on + -ing.
Watch out
  • suggest and recommend never take object + to-infinitive: "She suggested that I rest" ✓ or "suggested resting" ✓ — but "suggested me to rest" ✗.
  • deny takes -ing, not to: "He denied taking it" ✓ — "denied to take it" ✗.

5. verb + that-clause (insisting, suggesting formally)

Several verbs can also report with a that-clause. This is useful for a full statement or a formal demand. After suggest, recommend and insist, the verb in the clause often stays in the base form (the "subjunctive"), with an optional should.

VerbExample (that-clause)
insistThe coach insisted that everyone (should) wear a helmet.
suggestShe suggested that we (should) postpone the trip.
recommendHe recommended that the essay (should) be rewritten.
admitShe admitted that she had made a mistake.
B2 subtlety
  • After suggest / recommend / insist + that, use the base form for all subjects: "insisted that she stay" (not "stays"). Adding should is also correct: "insisted that she should stay."
  • suggest never takes a to-infinitive at all — only -ing or a that-clause.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B2 students still make with reporting verbs. Check your own writing against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
She suggested me to leave early.She suggested (that) I leave early. / suggested leaving early.suggest never takes object + to-infinitive.
He denied to take the money.He denied taking the money.deny is followed by -ing, not to.
They advised me leaving.They advised me to leave.advise + somebody + to-infinitive.
I apologised to be late.I apologised for being late.apologise for + -ing.
He warned me to not touch it.He warned me not to touch it.'not' comes before 'to'.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two friends talk about a chat with their form teacher. Read it twice and watch how one precise reporting verb replaces a whole reported sentence: admitted, apologised for, promised, advised, encouraged, recommended.

A:The teacher called me in. She admitted making a mistake with my grades.confession → admit + -ing.
B:Really? Did she apologise for it?apology → apologise for + -ing.
A:She did. She apologised for mixing up the marks and promised to fix it by Friday.apologise for + -ing; promise + to-infinitive.
B:Good. Did she say anything about the extra homework?an ordinary question, no reporting verb needed.
A:She advised me not to worry too much and encouraged me to ask questions in class.advise / encourage + object + to-infinitive; note 'not to'.
B:Fair enough. I'd recommend checking your grades online, though — just in case.recommend + -ing.
Test yourself
  • Which verb is followed by -ing here? (admit → "admitted making"; recommend → "recommend checking".)
  • Why is it "encouraged me to ask" but "recommend checking"? (encourage needs object + to-infinitive; recommend takes -ing.)
  • Rewrite "She promised to fix it" as reported speech beginning "She said...". (She said (that) she would fix it.)

Speaking: Reporting Conversations

Learn the reporting verbs, then use them to retell a real conversation of your own.

negotiate (v)
to discuss something to reach an agreement (müzakere etmek)
Syn: bargain · Ant: dictate
"We negotiated for an hour over which film to watch."
apologise (v)
to say you are sorry for something (özür dilemek)
Syn: say sorry · Ant: blame
"He apologised for missing my birthday party."
admit (v)
to accept that something is true, often unwillingly (itiraf etmek)
Syn: confess · Ant: deny
"She admitted making a mistake in her homework."
deny (v)
to say that something is not true (inkâr etmek)
Syn: reject · Ant: admit
"He denied breaking my headphones."
insist (v)
to say firmly that something must happen (ısrar etmek)
Syn: demand · Ant: yield
"She insisted that we start the game on time."
persuade (v)
to make someone agree by giving reasons (ikna etmek)
Syn: convince · Ant: discourage
"They persuaded him to change his mind."
reassure (v)
to say something to make someone feel calmer (içini rahatlatmak)
Syn: comfort · Ant: alarm
"My mum reassured me that everything would be fine."
warn (v)
to tell someone about a possible danger or problem (uyarmak)
Syn: caution · Ant: encourage
"She warned me not to trust him."
encourage (v)
to give someone confidence to do something (teşvik etmek)
Syn: motivate · Ant: discourage
"My tutor encouraged me to keep trying."
remind (v)
to help someone remember something (hatırlatmak)
Syn: prompt · Ant: —
"He reminded me to bring my gym kit."
refuse (v)
to say firmly that you will not do something (reddetmek)
Syn: decline · Ant: agree
"She refused to answer the question."
recommend (v)
to suggest that something is good or worth doing (tavsiye etmek)
Syn: advise · Ant: discourage
"I recommend buying the concert tickets early."
confront (v)
to face a problem or person directly (yüzleşmek, karşı karşıya gelmek)
Syn: challenge · Ant: avoid
"She finally confronted her friend about the rumour."
Reporting it later: "She reminded him that he had promised to call, and he apologised for forgetting."

Production Task (Üretim Görevi)

Retell a real (or imagined) conversation in 8 sentences — a disagreement, a piece of advice, or a negotiation. Use at least 8 of the words above.

Grammar requirement — apply this unit's topic
  • Use advise / warn / remind / encourage + somebody + to for directions (She advised me to wait).
  • Use admit / deny / suggest / recommend + -ing for confessions and suggestions (He admitted forgetting; she suggested waiting).
  • Use offer / promise / refuse / threaten + to for what the speaker will or won't do (He promised to help; I refused to sign).
  • Include at least one negative with not to (She warned me not to be late) and one apologise for + -ing.

Sentence starters (you can begin with these):

"She advised me to ..." "He admitted ...-ing ..." "They warned us not to ..." "I promised to ..." "In the end, we agreed to ..."

Reading: The Project That Changed Everything

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think of a time you made a mistake at school or with friends. Did you admit it, or try to hide it?
  • The text is about a tense conversation between friends working on a school project. Which reporting verbs (admit, deny, promise, threaten...) do you expect to see?
  • The title is "The Project That Changed Everything". Do you think it ends well or badly? Guess!
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text. Underline each reporting verb — admitted, apologised for, promised, threatened, warned, encouraged — and notice the pattern that follows it.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): negotiate · apologise · admit · deny · insist · warn · encourage · remind · refuse · recommend

One honest apology and a clear plan can turn a failing project into teamwork again.
The Project Nobody Forgot

When four friends almost ruined their history project, everything came down to a single tense conversation — and to what each of them was willing to say.

The four of them — Elif, Deniz, Kaan and Zeynep — had been put in the same group for a big history project on their town's past. Their teacher, Mr Aydın, had given the class three weeks to research old photographs, interview an older relative and build a ten-slide presentation. Because the topic counted for a large part of the term grade, everyone wanted it to be perfect. They created a shared online folder and a group chat, and they agreed to upload their parts every Friday so that nobody would be left doing everything at the last minute.

For the first two weeks, the plan worked smoothly. Elif designed the layout, Zeynep collected the interviews, and Kaan wrote the introduction. Deniz, though, had a busy fortnight: she was training almost every evening for the school basketball final, and she kept telling herself that she would catch up at the weekend. Messages piled up in the group chat, but she scrolled past them without replying. By the time she opened the shared folder again, two of her deadlines had slipped by, and the others had clearly noticed.

On the Thursday before the presentation, Elif called an emergency meeting in the quiet corner of the school library. She spread her notes across the table, took a deep breath, and looked straight at Deniz. Nobody was sure how the conversation would go, because Elif hated arguments as much as anyone, yet she felt responsible for the whole group's grade.

The group leader, Elif, insisted that Deniz had missed two important deadlines. Deniz did not argue. Instead, she admitted forgetting to upload her slides and apologised for the delays. She promised to finish the missing part within two days.

Deniz's cheeks went red as she listened. She did not try to defend herself or invent a clever story about a broken laptop. Speaking quietly, she explained that basketball practice had swallowed her evenings and that she had let the team down without meaning to. She opened her laptop straight away, showed the others exactly which slides were missing, and wrote the two new dates on a sticky note so that everybody could see her plan.

At first, the others refused to forgive her. One classmate, Kaan, even threatened to ask the teacher for a new group and warned Deniz not to let them down again. The mood in the library was cold.

For a few long seconds nobody spoke. Kaan folded his arms and stared at the bookshelves, still convinced that Deniz would disappear again the moment basketball season continued. Elif tapped her pen against the desk, worried that a new group would mean starting the research from zero with only days to spare. Zeynep, the quietest member, kept glancing between her friends, clearly uncomfortable with how sharp the argument had become. Somebody's phone buzzed with yet another notification, but for once nobody reached for it.

However, Deniz stayed calm. She reminded her friends that she had helped them many times before, and she encouraged them to look at the bigger picture. Then she offered to do the hardest section of the presentation herself.

She spoke slowly, without raising her voice. She pointed out that she had stayed up late helping Kaan rewrite his introduction last month, and that she had lent Zeynep her camera for the interviews. Getting angry now, she argued, would only waste the little time they had left. What mattered was finishing a project they could all be proud of, not deciding whose fault the delay had been.

Her honesty changed the mood completely. Zeynep, who had said almost nothing, finally spoke. She advised the others to give Deniz a second chance, and she suggested sharing the tasks more clearly rather than blaming one person.

Within minutes, the argument had turned into a plan. They opened a fresh document and listed every remaining task beside a name and a deadline. Elif would polish the design, Kaan agreed to check the historical facts, and Zeynep offered to practise the spoken introduction. Deniz, true to her word, took the most difficult slides about how the town had changed over a hundred years. They also agreed to send a quick message in the group chat each evening so that nobody would ever feel left in the dark again.

In the end, the group agreed to carry on together. Afterwards, Deniz denied being lazy. 'I just got overwhelmed,' she said. 'When you mess up, people don't want excuses; they want a plan.' Her friends later recommended writing a clear schedule for every future project. Since that day, the four of them remember one simple rule: admit the problem, then promise the solution.

The presentation itself went far better than any of them had expected. They rehearsed together twice after school, and Deniz uploaded her finished slides a full day before the deadline she had set herself. Mr Aydın praised the clear structure and the honest teamwork behind it, and the class voted their slides among the best in the year. Looking back, the four friends realised that the project had taught them more about trust than about history. Mistakes, they decided, are not the end of a friendship; how you own them is what really counts. A missed deadline can be forgiven in a single afternoon, as long as an honest apology arrives with a plan attached.

Writing: Reporting What Was Said

Write and submit your paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound B2 (Linking Words)

When you report a conversation, you also need connectors to show sequence and contrast. Four of them are essential this unit — and two familiar ones, although and however, become sharper tools.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
moreoveradd a stronger, extra pointHe apologised. Moreover, he promised to replace my broken game.
thereforeshow a logical resultShe had missed practice; therefore, she offered to train at the weekend.
whereascontrast two people or ideasI wanted to wait, whereas he insisted on leaving.
neverthelesssay "in spite of that"They warned me. Nevertheless, I refused to change my mind.
althoughconcede a point inside one sentenceAlthough he denied lying, nobody believed him.
howevercontrast between two sentencesShe refused at first. However, we finally persuaded her.

See the difference — the same facts, written two ways:

Basic linking ✗Confident B2 writing ✓
My best friend was upset. I said sorry. I said I would fix it. She said okay. She told me to be careful. My best friend confronted me about the secret I had shared. Therefore, I apologised for the mistake and promised to fix it. Although she was still hurt, she encouraged me to move on; however, she warned me not to let it happen again.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different B2 connectors from the table. Do not write sentence by sentence; connect your ideas.
  • Moreover, Therefore, However, Nevertheless usually start a sentence and take a comma: "However, he refused."

Paragraph structure (Paragraph Structure)

A "reported conversation" paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningSet the scene: who spoke to whom, and whyLast week my best friend messaged me to talk about a serious misunderstanding.
2. BodyReport what each person said, using a range of reporting verbs (admit, apologise for, promise, warn... not to, advise... to). Link with B2 connectors.I admitted sharing her secret and apologised for it. Nevertheless, she warned me not to do it again.
3. ClosingSay how it ended and what you learnedIn the end, we agreed to talk things through instead of staying silent.

Task (Görev)

Report a conversation that mattered — a piece of advice, a disagreement or a negotiation from school, your friendships or family life. Retell what was said using a range of reporting verbs.

WHAT TO INCLUDE

  • an opening sentence that sets the scene (who spoke, and why)
  • at least one advise / warn / remind / encourage + somebody + to (with a negative not to somewhere)
  • at least one admit / deny / suggest / recommend + -ing
  • at least one offer / promise / refuse / threaten + to
  • one apologise for + -ing or insist that ...
  • at least 3 B2 connectors: moreover, therefore, whereas, nevertheless, although, however
  • at least 4 words from the Speaking section (negotiate, apologise, admit, deny, insist, persuade, warn, encourage, refuse, recommend)

EXAMPLE OPENERS

"Last week, my parents talked to me about ..." "When I disagreed with my friend, she advised me to ..." "During the argument, my brother insisted that ..."

Sample Answer (~185 words) Last week, my best friend messaged me because she had heard that I had shared one of her secrets. I was nervous, but I decided to be honest. I admitted telling one other person and apologised for hurting her. Therefore, instead of making excuses, I promised to be more careful in future.

At first, she was not satisfied. She reminded me that I had done something similar before, and she warned me not to break her trust again. Nevertheless, she did not shout or threaten to end our friendship. Whereas some people would have stopped speaking to me, she chose to talk things through.

She encouraged me to think before I spoke, and she recommended keeping private things private. Moreover, she offered to do the same for me. Although the conversation was uncomfortable, it was fair.

In the end, we agreed to be more open with each other. I refused to make excuses, and I insisted that I would earn back her trust. I have not fully succeeded yet, but I am getting there.
