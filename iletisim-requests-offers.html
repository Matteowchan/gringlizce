Requests & OffersRica & Teklif

Rica: Can/Could/Would you...?, Do you mind...? — nezaket arttıkça dolaylılık artar (can < could < would). Teklif: Would you like...?, Shall I...?, Can I get you...? Türkçedeki "-ir misiniz / ister misiniz" inceliğine yakındır; resmiyet ve dolaylılık kültürel olarak kritiktir.

Requests: Can/Could/Would you...?, Do you mind...? — the more polite, the more indirect (can < could < would). Offers: Would you like...?, Shall I...?, Can I get you...? It is close to the Turkish nuance of "-ir misiniz / ister misiniz"; formality and indirectness are culturally critical.

İngilizcede bir şey rica etmek (birinden bir şey yapmasını istemek) ve bir şey teklif etmek (birine yardım ya da bir şey sunmak) günlük iletişimin belkemiğidir. İngilizce bu iki işlevi çoğunlukla modal fiillerle ve kalıplaşmış nazik yapılarla kurar: Can you...? / Could you...? / Would you...? / Do you mind...? (rica) ve Would you like...? / Shall I...? / Can I get you...? (teklif). Türkçedeki "-ir misiniz" ve "ister misiniz" inceliğine çok yakındır; ama İngilizcede nezaket düzeyini belirleyen şey ses tonu değil, seçtiğin kalıptır. Genel kural: ne kadar dolaylı, o kadar nazik. Bu konuyu iyi kavramak, günlük ifadeler, özür & teşekkür ve öneri & tavsiye konularının hepsini birbirine bağlar.

In English, making a request (asking someone to do something) and making an offer (offering someone help or something) are the backbone of everyday communication. English builds these two functions mostly with modal verbs and set polite structures: Can you...? / Could you...? / Would you...? / Do you mind...? (requests) and Would you like...? / Shall I...? / Can I get you...? (offers). It is very close to the Turkish nuance of "-ir misiniz" and "ister misiniz"; but in English what sets the level of politeness is not the tone of voice but the pattern you choose. The general rule: the more indirect, the more polite. Grasping this topic well ties together everyday expressions, apologizing & thanking and suggestions & advice.

İnsan ilişkilerinin çoğu iki basit hareketin etrafında döner: birinden bir şey istemek (rica) ve birine bir şey sunmak (teklif). "Şu tuzu uzatır mısın?", "Sana bir kahve getireyim mi?", "Bu formu doldurabilir misiniz?" — hepsi bu iki işlevin örneğidir. İngilizce bu işlevleri, dilbilgisel olarak soru biçiminde ama işlev olarak rica ya da teklif olarak kurar. Yani cümle soru gibi görünür (Can you...?), ama sen gerçekte bilgi sormuyorsun; birinin bir şey yapmasını istiyorsun.

İngilizcenin buradaki özelliği şudur: nezaket, kelime seçimiyle kodlanır. Türkçede çoğu zaman aynı fiili ("aç") ses tonuyla nazikleştiririz: "Aç şunu" ile "Açar mısın acaba?" arasındaki fark tonlamada ve -ir misiniz ekindedir. İngilizcede ise bu incelik doğrudan hangi modal fiili seçtiğine bağlıdır. can → could → would → I was wondering if you could hattı boyunca ilerledikçe cümle daha nazik, daha resmi ve daha dolaylı olur.

  1. Doğrudan / emir — Open the window. Yalnızca çok samimi ortamda.
  2. Nötr modal — Can you open the window? Arkadaş, aile.
  3. Nazik modal — Could you open the window? Hemen her ortam.
  4. Dolaylı / çekingen — I was wondering if you could open the window. Resmi, tanımadığın kişi.

Most of human relationships revolve around two simple moves: asking someone for something (a request) and offering someone something (an offer). "Could you pass the salt?", "Shall I get you a coffee?", "Could you fill in this form?" — all are examples of these two functions. English builds these functions grammatically as a question but functionally as a request or offer. So the sentence looks like a question (Can you...?), but you aren't really asking for information; you want someone to do something.

English's special feature here is this: politeness is coded through word choice. In Turkish we usually soften the same verb ("aç") with tone of voice: the difference between "Aç şunu" and "Açar mısın acaba?" is in the intonation and the -ir misiniz ending. In English, however, that nuance depends directly on which modal verb you choose. As you move along the line can → could → would → I was wondering if you could, the sentence becomes more polite, more formal and more indirect.

  1. Direct / imperative — Open the window. Only in a very familiar setting.
  2. Neutral modal — Can you open the window? Friends, family.
  3. Polite modal — Could you open the window? Almost any setting.
  4. Indirect / tentative — I was wondering if you could open the window. Formal, someone you don't know.
Key point
In one sentence: The more indirectly you speak, the more polite you are. "Give me the salt" (imperative) → "Can you pass the salt?" (request) → "Could you possibly pass the salt?" (very polite). Length and indirectness = politeness.

Why is this topic hard for the Turkish learner? Three reasons: (1) The correct answer to a Do you mind...? question is the reverse of Turkish logic — saying "No" means "okay, I'll do it". (2) Offers and requests are often confused: Would you like...? (I'm offering you) and Would you...? (I'm asking you) are separated by a single word. (3) Being too direct in English ("Give me...", "I want...") is felt as rude; a word-for-word translation from Turkish often produces rudeness. The aim of this section is to solve these three traps for good.

Both requests and offers are built in English in question order: first the modal verb, then the subject, then the bare verb. So the sentence starts like a question but its function is a request or an offer. The structure is surprisingly regular; once you settle it, all the patterns share the same skeleton.

When writing a formal request, remember that indirectness signals respect. Rather than asking a colleague to "send the data," a professional email typically softens the request: "I would appreciate it if you could send me the data by the end of the week." This structure does two things at once. First, it frames the request as a favour the reader is free to grant, which lowers the social pressure. Second, it keeps the tone warm without sacrificing clarity about what is needed and by when. Offers work the same way. Where to start
Start the first lesson with the function, not a list of patterns: bring the distinction between "asking someone for something" and "offering someone something" alive with real in-class situations (asking for a pen, holding a door). Present the patterns afterwards, as a politeness ladder (can → could → would). The student should first feel what they are doing, then learn how to build it.

Concept-checking questions (CCQ): "Do you mind opening the window?" — Am I asking you to do something or offering to do something? (asking). If you say "No", will you open it? (yes). These two questions open up the two most persistent mistakes (request/offer confusion and the reversed mind-answer) in one go. For "Would you like...?": Is this now or in general? (now) — this prevents confusion with "Do you like...?"

Activity suggestion: the "politeness ladder" game — write an imperative on the board ("Give me your pen") and ask students to make it one rung more polite in turn (Can you → Could you → Would you mind → I was wondering...). Then role-play cards: write a context on each card (boss, waiter, stranger, best friend) and have the student build the same request in the register that fits the context. Register sensitivity only settles through context.

A class mistake to watch for
Turkish students think the "I want..." and "Give me..." structures are neutral and use them in a request; in English this sounds rude. Flag it early and redirect to question form every time. The second frequent mistake: adding "to" after a modal (could you to). Make the correction not mechanical but a pattern-reflex.
