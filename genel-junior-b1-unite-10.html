Grammar: Modals of Obligation & Advice

Study the rules, look at the examples, then do the exercises.

Talking about rules, duties and advice

Every day we talk about what is necessary, what is forbidden, and what is a good idea. To do this in English we use a special group of small verbs called modals: must, have to, mustn't, should, ought to, had better. They never change for the subject (no -s), and most of them are followed by the bare infinitive — the verb with no to (must go, not must to go).

In this unit you will learn three things: how to express obligation (something is necessary), how to express prohibition and no obligation (the famous mustn't vs don't have to trap), and how to give advice. You will also see how to talk about a past obligation with had to.

  • You must do your homework before dinner.The rule is necessary → obligation.
  • You mustn't shout in the library.It is forbidden → prohibition.
  • You should share your toys with your friends.A good idea → advice.

1. Obligation: must and have to (necessity)

Both must and have to say that something is necessary. The form is different: must is a modal (always the same, + bare infinitive), while have to behaves like a normal verb and uses do / does in questions and negatives.

 PositiveNegativeQuestion
mustI must go.— (see mustn't below)Must I go?
have toShe has to go.She doesn't have to go.Does she have to go?
must vs have to
  • must often shows the speaker's own strong feeling: "I must call my mother — I really want to."
  • have to usually shows a rule from outside (a law, a teacher, a timetable): "I have to wear a uniform at school."
  • In everyday English the difference is small, and have to is more common in speech.

2. The key contrast (mustn't vs don't have to)

This is the most important — and most tested — point in the whole unit. The two forms look similar but mean the opposite. Read this table very carefully.

FormMeaningExample
mustn'tIt is forbidden. Do NOT do it. (prohibition)You mustn't bring your phone to class. (It is not allowed.)
don't have toIt is not necessary, but you can if you want. (no obligation)You don't have to bring your phone to class. (You can, but it's free to choose.)
Golden rule
  • mustn't = "No! It's a rule." — don't have to = "It's your choice; there's no need."
  • Compare: "You mustn't tell anyone." (keep the secret) vs "You don't have to tell anyone." (you can stay quiet if you prefer).

3. Advice: should, ought to, had better

When something is not a rule but a good idea, we give advice. Three forms are common at B1. All are followed by the bare infinitive.

FormStrengthExample
should / shouldn'tnormal advice (most common)You should tidy your room. You shouldn't eat so many sweets.
ought tothe same as should, a little more formalYou ought to brush your teeth twice a day.
had better ('d better)strong advice or a warning about nowYou 'd better hurry, or you'll miss the school bus.
Watch the form
  • Say ought to go, but should go and had better go (no to): "You should go." ✓ "You should to go." ✗
  • The negative of had better is had better not: "You'd better not be late for school."

4. Past obligation: had to (must has no past)

Here is a common surprise: must has no past form. When we talk about an obligation in the past, we must use had to for everybody.

TimeObligationExample
Present / generalmust / have toToday I have to finish my homework.
Pasthad toYesterday I had to finish my homework.
Past, no obligationdidn't have toOn Sunday I didn't have to go to school.
Key point
  • Never say "I must study yesterday" ✗. For any past obligation, use had to: "I had to study yesterday." ✓

5. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B1 students make most often with obligation and advice. Check your own writing against this list.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
You mustn't to shout here.You mustn't shout here.must / mustn't take the bare infinitive (no "to").
The park is free, so you mustn't pay.The park is free, so you don't have to pay.no obligation, not prohibition → don't have to.
Yesterday I must leave school early.Yesterday I had to leave school early.past obligation → had to (must has no past).
You should to sleep more.You should sleep more.should + bare infinitive, no "to".
Does he must wear a uniform?Does he have to wear a uniform?use have to (with do/does) for questions.

6. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

A new student, Emir, asks his classmate Sena about the rules at school. Read it twice and notice each obligation, prohibition, no-obligation and piece of advice.

EmirWhat are the main rules here? What do I have to do?
SenaWell, you must bring your books every day, and you mustn't run in the corridors, because someone could fall.
EmirOK. Do I have to wear the sports kit today?
SenaNo, you don't have to. We only wear it on Fridays, so you can wear your normal uniform — but you shouldn't wear muddy shoes inside!
EmirGood to know. Any advice for my first week?
SenaYou ought to ask the teacher when you're unsure, and you'd better not be late on Mondays — that's when we have the reading test. Last week I had to stay in at break because I forgot my book!
Test yourself
  • Find one prohibition. Which word shows it? (mustn't — "you mustn't run in the corridors".)
  • "You don't have to wear the sports kit." Does Emir have to wear it? (No — it is not necessary, but he can if he wants.)
  • Why does Sena say "I had to stay" and not "I must stay"? (It is a past obligation, and must has no past form.)

Speaking: Rules & Advice

Learn the words, then use them in your own sentences about rules in your life.

rule (n)
an instruction that says what you can or cannot do (kural)
Syn: regulation · Ant: —
"You must follow the safety rules."
obligation (n)
something you must do; a duty (zorunluluk)
Syn: duty · Ant: choice
"Doing your homework is an obligation."
allowed (adj)
that you have permission to do (izin verilen, serbest)
Syn: permitted · Ant: forbidden
"Phones are not allowed in class."
forbidden (adj)
that you must not do; not permitted (yasak)
Syn: banned · Ant: allowed
"Running by the pool is strictly forbidden."
permission (n)
when someone lets you do something (izin)
Syn: consent · Ant: refusal
"You need permission to leave the classroom."
strict (adj)
making people obey rules very carefully (katı, sıkı)
Syn: firm · Ant: relaxed
"Our teacher is very strict about homework."
flexible (adj)
easy to change; not fixed or strict (esnek)
Syn: adaptable · Ant: strict
"My bedtime is flexible at the weekend."
deadline (n)
the latest time to finish something (son teslim tarihi)
Syn: time limit · Ant: —
"The homework deadline is Friday."
responsibility (n)
a duty that you must take care of (sorumluluk)
Syn: duty · Ant: —
"Safety is everyone's responsibility."
follow (v)
to do what a rule or instruction says (uymak)
Syn: obey · Ant: break
"Everyone has to follow the rules."
break (v)
to fail to obey a rule or law (ihlal etmek, çiğnemek)
Syn: disobey · Ant: follow
"You mustn't break the game's rules."
advice (n)
an idea about what someone should do (tavsiye)
Syn: suggestion · Ant: —
"My advice is that you should rest."
warning (n)
something that tells you about a danger (uyarı)
Syn: caution · Ant: —
"The sign is a warning: you mustn't swim here."
duty (n)
something you have to do because it is right (görev)
Syn: obligation · Ant: —
"It is everyone's duty to keep the classroom tidy."

Reading: The Rules of My New School

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think about a place you know. What is one rule there that you must follow?
  • The text is about rules at a new school. Which modal verbs do you expect to see a lot?
  • Look at the title, "The Rules of My New School". Do you think all the rules will be strict, or will some be relaxed? Guess!
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text for the answers. Notice each obligation (must, has to), prohibition (mustn't) and piece of advice (should, ought to).

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): rule · obligation · forbidden · strict · flexible · deadline · follow · advice

A new school means new rules: what you must do, what is forbidden, and what is only advice.
The Rules of My New School

Defne started at a new school last month, and she quickly learned that every school has its own rules. On her first day, her teacher gave her a long list of things she had to do and things she must never do.

The school is called Green Valley, and it is next to a big park full of trees. Defne moved to the town in the summer, so she did not know anyone yet. On her first morning, she arrived early because she did not want to be late. She waited by the gate for a few minutes and took a deep breath. A new school can feel a little scary, but it can also be exciting. When she walked in, a friendly girl called Zeynep smiled and showed her the way to the classroom. Their teacher, Mr Kaya, was kind and calm. He told Defne that the first week is always the hardest, and that nobody remembers every rule on the first day. 'Take your time,' he said. 'You don't have to learn everything at once.'

Some rules were strict. Defne must wear her school uniform every day, and she mustn't run in the corridors, because someone could fall. She also has to raise her hand before she speaks in class. 'You must be kind to everyone,' Mr Kaya said, 'but you can always come to me if you have a problem.'

Defne soon understood why some of these rules were so important. Green Valley is a busy place with hundreds of children, so everyone has to follow the same rules to stay safe. In the science room, students mustn't touch the equipment without a teacher, because it can be dangerous. On the stairs, everyone should walk on the left, while the older children help the younger ones. Zeynep explained the playground rules too. 'You must share the swings,' she said, 'and you had better not push, or you will get a warning.'

However, other rules were more relaxed. Defne doesn't have to bring her own lunch, because the school has a nice canteen. She doesn't have to join a club either, although most children choose one. Defne picked the chess club, and she was very happy.

There were a few pieces of advice as well. Mr Kaya told her that she should drink water at break time, and that she ought to go to bed early so she is not tired in class. 'You had better not forget your homework,' he added with a smile, 'or you will feel sorry the next day.' Defne wrote everything in her notebook.

During her first week, Defne tried to follow all of this advice. When she did not understand a task, she raised her hand and asked, and Mr Kaya never seemed annoyed. Although some days felt long, she slowly learned where everything was.

By Friday, Defne felt much more comfortable. She knew where her classroom was, she remembered most of the rules, and she had already made a few friends. Although it was a lot to learn, she felt happy. She had a good feeling about her new school, and she knew it would be a great year.

Writing: The Rules of a Place I Know

Write and submit your paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Connectors turn sentences into a text (Linking Words)

A list of short, separate rules is not real writing. What connects your ideas is linking words. At A2 you used and, but, so, because. At B1 you add four more powerful connectors: also, when, while, although and however. These let you show contrast, background situations and extra information like a confident writer.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
andadd informationYou must wear your uniform, and you have to bring your books.
butshow a small contrastThe homework is hard, but the teachers are kind.
soshow a resultIt's a library, so you mustn't make noise.
becausegive a reasonYou have to be quiet because people are reading.
alsoadd an extra pointYou must be on time. You also have to wear a uniform.
whensay at what timeYou should ask when you don't understand a rule.
whilecontrast two situationsSome rules are strict, while others are flexible.
althoughshow a strong contrastAlthough the rules are strict, they keep us safe.
howevercontrast between two sentencesYou must follow the rules. However, you can always ask questions.

See the difference — the same rules, written two ways:

Separate sentences ✗Connected, fluent writing ✓
You must wear your uniform. You mustn't be late. The lunch is free. You don't have to bring food. You should be polite. You must wear your uniform, and you mustn't be late. However, the lunch is free, so you don't have to bring food. Although the rules are clear, you should always be polite.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different connectors in your paragraph. Do not write rule by rule; link your ideas.
  • When when / while / although / however start a sentence, they are usually followed by a comma: "However, you can always ask."

Paragraph structure (Paragraph Structure)

A "rules" paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same every time — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningA general sentence about the place and its rulesMy school has quite a lot of rules that every student has to follow.
2. BodyThe obligations (must / have to), the prohibitions (mustn't), what is not necessary (don't have to), and some advice (should). Link with connectors.You must arrive on time, and you mustn't use your phone in class. However, you don't have to wear a uniform.
3. ClosingYour opinion, or a piece of advice for a newcomerAlthough some rules are strict, I think they are fair, so my advice is that you should follow them.

Sample Answer (~165 words) My school has quite a lot of rules that every student has to follow, and most of them are fair. The first rule is that you must wear your uniform every day, because the school wants everyone to look smart. You also have to raise your hand before you speak, so the class stays quiet. Our teacher is quite strict about this.

However, some rules are more relaxed. You mustn't run in the corridors, but you don't have to bring your own lunch. The canteen is good, so you can eat there every day. While the timetable is full, break times are flexible, and you don't have to stay inside if the weather is nice.

Although these rules may sound difficult at first, they are not hard to follow. My advice for a new student is simple: you should ask the teacher when you are unsure, and you had better not forget your homework. If you follow the rules, you will enjoy school.
