Grammar: Modals of Possibility

Study the rules, look at the examples, then do the exercises.

How sure are you?

Sometimes we know a fact for certain: "It is raining." But very often in real life we are not sure, and we need to say how likely something is. Is it certain? Is it just possible? Is it impossible? English uses a small group of modal verbs to show exactly how confident we are.

In this unit you will learn the modals of possibility and deduction: may, might and could (possible), must (I'm almost sure it is true), and can't (I'm almost sure it is impossible). Choosing the right one is one of the most useful speaking skills at B1.

  • She may be at home.It is possible she is at home → may / might / could.
  • He must be tired after that long trip.All the evidence says so → a strong positive conclusion.
  • That can't be true!I'm almost sure it is impossible → a strong negative conclusion.

1. The form (modal + base verb)

Modals are easy to build. After the modal, we always use the base verb (the infinitive without to). The modal never changes for the subject — there is no -s in the third person, and we never add do / does.

SubjectModalBase verbExample
I / you / we / theymay / might / couldbe, rain, knowThey might know the answer.
he / she / itmay / might / couldbe, rain, knowShe could be right.
it (deduction)must / can'tbe, meanIt must be a mistake.
Key point
  • No -s and no to: She might come. ✓ — She mights to come. ✗
  • Make it negative on the modal: may not, might not, must not, can't — never don't may.

2. may / might / could (it is possible)

To say that something is possible now or possible in the future, we use may, might or could. In most everyday sentences the three are almost the same in meaning — each one means "perhaps".

ModalMeaningExample
mayperhaps (a little more formal)It may rain this afternoon.
mightperhaps (very common in speech)She might be at home now.
couldperhaps (one of several options)He could be right — I'm not sure.
  • I'm not sure where Deniz is. She might be in the kitchen.Present possibility.
  • Take an umbrella — it may get cold and wet later.Future possibility.

3. Degrees of certainty (certain → possible → impossible)

This is the heart of the unit. Think of a "certainty line". At one end you are sure something IS true; at the other end you are sure it is NOT true. The modal you choose shows exactly where you are on that line.

How sure?ModalExample
100% sure it is truemustShe must be exhausted.
Maybe true (about 50%)may / might / couldShe might be exhausted.
Maybe not truemay not / might notShe might not be exhausted.
100% sure it is impossiblecan'tShe can't be exhausted — she slept all day!
Golden rule
  • Strong positive conclusion → must. Strong negative conclusion → can't.
  • Only possible, not sure → may / might / could.

4. Negative possibility (may not / might not vs can't)

Be careful: there are two ways to be negative, and they mean very different things. May not / might not means "perhaps not". Can't (or couldn't) means "impossible" — much stronger.

FormMeaningExample
may not / might notit is possible that it is NOT trueHe might not come to the party. (Maybe he won't.)
can't / couldn'tit is impossible / I'm sure it is not trueHe can't be at the party — he is in Paris! (Impossible.)
Watch out
  • Do not use mustn't for a negative deduction. He can't be serious. ✓ — He mustn't be serious. ✗ (mustn't = prohibition, Unit 10)
  • might not = perhaps not; can't = definitely not. Do not mix them up.

5. Deduction from evidence (must / can't)

When we look at the evidence and reach a confident conclusion, we use must (I'm sure it is true) or can't (I'm sure it is not true). This is called deduction. Notice: can't is the negative of must here — not "mustn't".

EvidenceConclusionExample
The lights are on and I can hear music.must (positive)Someone must be at home.
It's midnight and every window is dark.can't (negative)They can't be awake now.
He hasn't eaten since morning.must (positive)He must be really hungry.
Quick test
  • Sure it IS true → must. Sure it is NOT true → can't.
  • "This must be the right house" vs "This can't be the right house."

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B1 students make most often with modals of possibility. Check your own sentences against this list.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
It can rain later, take a coat.It might rain later, take a coat.for possibility in a statement we use may/might/could, not "can".
She mights be at school.She might be at school.no -s and no "to" after a modal.
He must to be tired.He must be tired.modal + base verb, never "to".
That mustn't be true.That can't be true.negative deduction uses can't, not mustn't.
They can be Turkish, I'm not sure.They could be Turkish, I'm not sure.uncertain guess → may/might/could.
Watch out
  • We can use "can" for possibility in a question: "Where can he be?" ✓ But in a normal statement about a specific situation, use may/might/could.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two friends at the school fair see a boy they do not know and try to work out who he is. Read it twice and notice how they move along the "certainty line" — from a wild guess, to a possibility, to a confident conclusion.

A:Who's that boy by the gate? He might be a friend of Ali's.A guess → possibility.
B:He could be, but I'm not sure. He may be in the new class at our school.Two more possibilities.
A:Look, he's holding a football and our team's scarf. He must be the new player!Evidence → strong positive conclusion.
B:He can't be the new player — the new player arrived earlier and he's over there.Evidence → strong negative conclusion.
A:You're right. Then he might not be from our school at all. Let's just ask him!Weak negative → possibility not.
Test yourself
  • Find a sentence with must. What evidence made A so sure? (the football and the scarf.)
  • Why does B say can't and not mustn't? (negative deduction = can't.)
  • Change "He might be a friend" into a stronger, more certain sentence. (He must be a friend.)

Speaking: Guessing & Speculating

Learn the words, then use them to speculate about the world around you.

possible (adj)
able to happen or be true (mümkün)
Syn: feasible · Ant: impossible
"It is possible that Fındık is hiding behind the bookshelf."
possibility (n)
a thing that may happen or be true (olasılık, ihtimal)
Syn: chance · Ant: certainty
"There is a strong possibility that the hamster went towards the food."
certain (adj)
completely sure; without any doubt (emin, kesin)
Syn: sure · Ant: unsure
"I'm not certain, but he might be under the radiator."
likely (adj)
probably going to happen or be true (muhtemel, olası)
Syn: probable · Ant: unlikely
"It is likely that a hungry hamster went to the lunch corner."
unlikely (adj)
probably not going to happen (olası değil)
Syn: improbable · Ant: likely
"It is unlikely that he escaped through a closed window."
perhaps (adv)
used to say something is possible (belki)
Syn: maybe · Ant: definitely
"Perhaps he climbed out of the cage during the weekend."
probably (adv)
almost certainly; very likely (muhtemelen)
Syn: most likely · Ant: unlikely
"He is probably somewhere warm and dark."
guess (v)
to give an answer you are not sure about (tahmin etmek)
Syn: estimate · Ant: know
"I guess he must be looking for food somewhere."
wonder (v)
to want to know; to ask yourself (merak etmek, acaba)
Syn: ask yourself · Ant: know
"I wonder where the hamster could be hiding."
clue (n)
a piece of information that helps you find an answer (ipucu)
Syn: hint · Ant: —
"The chewed biscuit packet is an important clue."
evidence (n)
facts that show something is true (kanıt, delil)
Syn: proof · Ant: —
"The sunflower seeds are clear evidence that he was here."
obvious (adj)
easy to see or understand; clear (apaçık, bariz)
Syn: clear · Ant: unclear
"It's obvious that he can't be outside — the door was shut."
doubt (n/v)
a feeling of not being sure (şüphe / şüphe etmek)
Syn: uncertainty · Ant: certainty
"I doubt he could open a locked cage by himself."
suspect (v)
to think something is probably true (sanmak, şüphelenmek)
Syn: suppose · Ant: be sure
"I suspect he might be in the art cupboard."
Speculating: use may, might, could for guesses, and must / can't for confident conclusions.

Production Task (Üretim Görevi)

Write 8 sentences in which you speculate about people or situations. Use at least 8 of the words above.

Grammar requirement — apply this unit's topic
  • Use may / might / could for things that are only possible (She might be at home.).
  • Use must for a strong positive conclusion and can't for a strong negative one (He must be tired. That can't be true.).
  • Write at least 2 pairs: first a possibility (may/might/could), then a confident conclusion (must / can't). (Örn: "He might be ill. Actually, he can't be ill — I saw him running.")

Reading: Where Could It Be?

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Imagine your class pet has escaped from its cage overnight. Where might a small, hungry animal go?
  • The text is a small mystery. Which modals (may, might, could, must, can't) do you expect the characters to use?
  • Look at the title, "Where Could It Be?". Do you think the class will find their pet, and where? Guess!
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text for the answers. Notice each modal of possibility (may, might, could, must, can't).

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): possible · possibility · perhaps · certain · clue · evidence · obvious · probably · likely

A missing class pet and a few clues: read the evidence and make your deductions.
Where Could It Be?

On Monday morning, our class had a big problem. When Mr Kaya opened the door, the cage of our class hamster, Fındık, was empty. The little door was open, and Fındık was gone. Everybody started talking at once, but Mr Kaya asked us to stay calm and think like scientists.

"A hamster can't disappear into thin air," he said. "He must be somewhere in this room. Let's look at the clues." I loved this idea, because I read a lot of mystery stories, and now I could be a real detective.

First, we looked at the cage. The door was not broken, so someone might have forgotten to close it properly on Friday. There were tiny footprints in the sawdust near the door. "He could have climbed out during the weekend," said my friend Ela. "That means he might be anywhere in the classroom."

We began to think about all the possibilities. Fındık might be hiding behind the bookshelf, or he could be under the radiator, where it is warm. He may be inside a school bag, looking for food. "He can't be outside," I said, "because the windows and the door were shut all weekend." Everyone agreed that this was good thinking.

Mr Kaya wrote our ideas on the board. Hamsters love warm, dark places, so the radiator was the most likely spot. They also love food, so the lunch corner was another strong possibility. "Where would you go if you were a small, hungry hamster?" Mr Kaya asked. That question really helped us.

We searched carefully. Behind the bookshelf we found only dust and an old pencil. Under the radiator there was nothing but a lost sock. However, near the lunch corner we found something exciting: a chewed corner of a biscuit packet and a few sunflower seeds. "This is real evidence!" Ela whispered. "He must have been here."

Just then, we heard a tiny sound from the cupboard where we keep the art materials. It was a soft scratching noise. "That could be Fındık," I said, "or it might just be the pipes." We opened the cupboard very slowly, so that we would not frighten him. At first we saw nothing, but then, behind a box of paints, two bright little eyes were shining in the dark.

There he was! Fındık was sitting happily on a pile of coloured paper, with his cheeks full of seeds. He had made himself a cosy nest. Everyone wanted to grab him at once, but Mr Kaya told us not to rush. "If we all shout, he will run again," he warned. "Move slowly and stay quiet."

Ela reached in gently and lifted him out. Fındık did not seem worried at all; in fact, he looked very pleased with his little adventure. We put him back in his cage, and this time we checked the door twice.

Mr Kaya smiled at us. "Well done, detectives," he said. "You did not panic. You looked at the evidence and thought about what was likely and what was impossible. That is exactly how a good scientist solves a problem." I felt very proud, because our careful thinking had worked. A missing hamster had turned an ordinary Monday into the best mystery of the whole year. Now, whenever something is lost, my friends grin and ask me the same question: "Where could it be?" And I always answer, "Let's look at the clues."

Writing: Where Could It Be?

Write and submit your paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Connectors turn sentences into a text (Linking Words)

A list of short, separate sentences is not real writing. What connects your ideas is linking words. At A2 you used and, but, so, because. At B1 you add more powerful connectors: also, when, while, although and however. These let you show contrast, background and extra information like a confident writer — and they work perfectly when you speculate about a mystery.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
andadd informationThe door was open, and the lights were on.
butshow a small contrastHe might be here, but I can't see him.
soshow a resultThe seeds were still fresh, so he must be close.
becausegive a reasonIt can't be outside because the door was shut.
alsoadd an extra pointHe may be by the food. He could also be under the radiator.
whensay at what timeWhen I arrived, the cage was empty.
whiletwo things at the same timeWhile I was thinking, the phone rang.
althoughshow a strong contrastAlthough it looked strange, nothing was wrong.
howevercontrast between two sentencesIt might be serious. However, it was just a note.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different connectors in your paragraph. Do not write sentence by sentence; link your ideas.
  • When when / while / although / however start a sentence, they are usually followed by a comma: "However, it was just a note."

Paragraph structure (Paragraph Structure)

A "mystery" paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same every time — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningDescribe the lost thing and the strange situationLast week I lost my house key, and I felt really worried.
2. BodySpeculate: list possible places with may/might/could, then reach conclusions with must / can't. Link with connectors.It might be in my bag, but it can't be at school, because I had it after class. It must be at home.
3. ClosingSay where you found it, or what you still wonderIn the end, I found it next to my shoes by the door.

Task (Görev)

Think of something important that you lost, or imagine losing it (a key, a phone, a pet, a ring). Write about where it could be. Speculate with modals of possibility and then reach a conclusion.

WHAT TO INCLUDE

  • an opening that describes the lost thing and the situation
  • two or three possible places (Present possibility: may / might / could)
  • at least one strong positive conclusion with must
  • at least one strong negative conclusion with can't
  • a closing sentence about what happened or what you still wonder
  • at least 3 connectors: and, but, so, because, also, when, while, although, however
  • at least 4 words from the Speaking section (possible, possibility, perhaps, likely, clue, evidence, obvious, guess)

EXAMPLE OPENERS

"Last week I lost my ..." "I wonder where it could ..." "It might be ..., but it can't be ..."

Sample Answer (~165 words) Last week I lost my house key, and I still remember how worried I felt. When I got home, it was not in my bag, so I started to think about all the possibilities.

It might be in my coat pocket, or it could be at the bottom of my school bag. Perhaps I had left it at my friend's house. However, it can't be in the classroom, because I definitely had it after school. The key is quite big, so it can't be lost in a tiny place. I was almost certain that it must be somewhere at home.

Then I began to look for clues. My muddy shoes were by the door, so I checked the hall first. There, on the floor, I found the key next to my shoes. The obvious answer had been right in front of me.

In the end, I felt very relieved, and now I always put my key in the same pocket.
