Sınav Hakkında

Bu ara sınav, Ünite 1-10'da öğrendiğin her şeyi C2 (native-yakını incelik) seviyesinde ölçer: register ve üslup aralığı; öne çekme (fronting) ve tematik konum; end-weight ve end-focus; cleft yapılarının ustalığı; retorik tercih olarak devrik yapılar (inversion); hedging, boosting ve stance (duruş); nominalizasyon ve dilbilgisel eğretileme; bağdaşıklık (gönderim/yerine koyma/eksilti); söylem belirteçleri ve metin örgütlenmesi; ve modalitede ince ayrım. Speaking hariç dört beceri test edilir. Toplam süre 100 dakikadır ve tek bir sayaçla ilerler — süreyi bölümler arasında kendin yönetirsin.

C2 Midterm Sınavı · Bölüm 4/6

Reading (2 metin · 20 soru)

Suggested time: 25 minutes. (Önerilen süre: 25 dakika.) Read both texts, then answer the questions. (İki metni oku, soruları cevapla.)

Text 1 · The Economy of Attention

Every advertisement, notification and autoplaying video is a small claim on something we rarely think of as a resource: our attention. Unlike money, attention cannot be saved or borrowed; each of us has only so many waking hours, and once a minute has been spent staring at a screen, it is gone for good. It is precisely this scarcity that has made human attention one of the most valuable commodities of the digital age.

The companies that dominate the internet do not, for the most part, sell us their products; they sell us to advertisers. Their real business is to capture and hold our gaze for as long as possible, and every feature of a modern app — the endless scroll, the flashing badge, the carefully timed alert — has been refined to that single end. Engineers speak, without irony, of designing for 'stickiness', as though the highest compliment one could pay a piece of software were that users find it difficult to put down.

It would be too easy, however, to cast this simply as a tale of corporate villainy. Users are not passive victims; many of us reach for our phones out of boredom, loneliness or habit long before any notification arrives. Nor is the technology without genuine value: the same platforms that fragment our concentration also let us learn, organise and stay in touch across great distances. The problem is not that these tools exist, but that their commercial incentives pull relentlessly in one direction — towards more time spent, whatever the cost to the user.

And the cost, critics argue, is considerable. A mind trained to expect a fresh stimulus every few seconds may find sustained thought increasingly uncomfortable. Some researchers warn that the constant switching of attention leaves us not better informed but merely more agitated, mistaking the sensation of busyness for genuine understanding. Whether these effects are as severe as the gloomiest commentators claim remains, for now, an open question.

What is clear is that the debate can no longer be dismissed as the grumbling of technophobes. Even the designers who built these systems have begun to voice unease about what they have made. As one former executive remarked, the goal was never to enrich anyone's life; it was, quite simply, to keep them coming back. Until that underlying incentive changes, no amount of individual willpower is likely to tip the balance for long.

Text 2 · The Return

Marta had not set foot in the village since the winter her grandmother died, and even now, thirty years later, she could not have said exactly why she had stayed away so long. She told colleagues it was distance, or work, or the simple difficulty of finding the time; but the truth, she suspected, was closer to fear — the fear that the place she remembered so vividly might no longer exist, or worse, might never have existed quite as she recalled it.

The bus dropped her at the edge of the square, and for a moment she stood quite still, half expecting to be recognised. She was not. The bakery where her grandmother had bought bread every morning was now a mobile-phone shop; the fountain, once the noisy heart of the village, had been switched off and filled with gravel. Only the church, grey and indifferent, seemed exactly as it had always been.

She walked slowly up the familiar lane, and it was the smell, rather than any sight, that finally undid her composure. Somewhere a fire was burning — the sharp, resinous scent of pine — and all at once she was six years old again, sitting on a wooden stool in her grandmother's kitchen while snow gathered silently against the window. Memory, she thought, is a stubborn thing; it clings not to the grand events we expect to treasure, but to a smell, a sound, the particular quality of an afternoon light.

Her grandmother's house still stood, though a stranger's curtains now hung in the windows. Marta did not knock. It was enough, she found, to stand at the gate and to know that the walls were still there, that the past had left at least this small, stubborn trace. She had come, she realised, not to reclaim anything, but simply to confirm that it had all been real.

On the bus back to the city that evening, she felt lighter than she had in years. The village had changed almost beyond recognition, and yet something essential had survived — not in its streets, which belonged now to other people, but in her, carried quietly across three decades like a letter that had finally reached its address.

C2 Midterm Sınavı · Bölüm 5/6

Writing (2 görev)

Suggested time: 35 minutes — Task 1: 13 min, Task 2: 22 min. (Önerilen süre: 35 dakika — Görev 1: 13 dk, Görev 2: 22 dk.) Öğretmenin değerlendirecek; örnek cevap yoktur.

Task 1 · Opinion Email (120-150 words)

Your city council has proposed turning the main square into a car-free zone, open only to pedestrians and cyclists. A local newspaper has asked readers for their views. Write a formal email to the newspaper giving your opinion. (Belediyen ana meydanı yayalara ve bisikletlilere açık, araç girişine kapalı bir bölgeye çevirmeyi önerdi. Yerel bir gazete okuyucularının görüşünü soruyor. Görüşünü belirten resmî bir e-posta yaz.)

  • Maintain a consistent formal register throughout. (baştan sona tutarlı resmî register)
  • Use at least one cleft or inversion for emphasis ("It is the noise that..."; "Not only would this..."). (vurgu için en az bir cleft ya da devrik yapı)
  • Hedge or boost at least one claim ("The proposal may well..."; "There is no doubt that..."). (en az bir iddiada hedging/boosting)
  • Use at least 3 discourse markers: however, nevertheless, moreover, therefore. (en az 3 söylem belirteci)
  • 120-150 words. (120-150 kelime)
E-POSTA YAPISI
  • Açılış: "Dear Editor," + neden yazdığın ("I am writing to share my view on...").
  • Gövde: görüşün + en az iki gerekçe; bir cleft/devrik yapıyla vurgu ver.
  • Kapanış: görüşünü özetle + "Yours faithfully," + adın.
0 kelime

Task 2 · For-and-Against Paragraph (160-200 words)

Some people believe that online learning will one day replace traditional universities completely. Others strongly disagree. Discuss the arguments for and against this view, and then give your own opinion. (Bazıları çevrimiçi öğrenimin bir gün geleneksel üniversitelerin yerini tamamen alacağını düşünür; bazıları buna kesinlikle katılmaz. Bu görüşün lehindeki ve aleyhindeki argümanları tartış, sonra kendi görüşünü ver.)

  • Introduce the topic, then give one paragraph FOR and one AGAINST. (biri lehte, biri aleyhte)
  • Use at least one nominalisation ("the rapid rise in...") and one cleft/inversion. (bir nominalizasyon + bir cleft/devrik)
  • Signal your stance with hedging or boosting ("It could be argued..."; "There is little doubt..."). (hedging/boosting ile duruş)
  • Use at least 5 discourse markers: however, whereas, nevertheless, moreover, therefore. (en az 5 söylem belirteci)
  • End with a clear opinion. (net bir görüşle bitir)
  • 160-200 words. (160-200 kelime)
