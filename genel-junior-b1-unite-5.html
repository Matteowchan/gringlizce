Ünite 5 · Second Conditional · Bölüm 1/6

Grammar: The Second Conditional

Study the rules, look at the examples, then do the exercises.

Talking about imaginary situations

Sometimes we do not talk about real life — we talk about imaginary worlds: things that are not true now, or that are very unlikely. If I were a bird... If I had a magic wand... If I could fly... For these dreams and unreal ideas, English uses a special structure called the Second Conditional.

The core idea is simple: we imagine an unreal present or future, and then we say the imaginary result. Because the situation is not real, we "step back" one tense — we use the Past Simple in the if-part, even though we are talking about now.

  • If I had more time, I would travel the world.I don't have much time now → imaginary → Second Conditional.
  • If she studied harder, she would pass the exam.She doesn't study hard now → unreal present.
  • If I were a giant, I would help my whole family.I'm not a giant → a dream, not a fact.

1. The form (If + Past Simple, would + verb)

The Second Conditional has two parts (clauses). The if-clause uses the Past Simple, and the result clause uses would + the base verb (the infinitive without to).

If-clause (condition)Result clauseFull sentence
If + Past Simplewould + base verbIf I knew her number, I would call her.
If I won the big prize,I would buy a treehouse.If I won the big prize, I would buy a treehouse.
If we lived in Paris,we would speak French.If we lived in Paris, we would speak French.
Key point
  • You can put the clauses in either order. When the if-clause comes first, use a comma: "If I had wings, I would fly." When it comes second, no comma: "I would fly if I had wings."
  • would is often shortened to 'd in speech: I'd, she'd, we'd. The negative is wouldn't.

2. If I were (a special form)

With the verb be, the Second Conditional uses were for every subject — I, he, she and it too. In everyday speech you may also hear was, but in careful English and in exams, were is the correct choice.

SubjectStandard form ✓Example
I / he / she / itwereIf I were you, I would rest. / If he were here, he would help.
you / we / theywereIf they were older, they would travel more.
Watch out
  • The fixed phrase "If I were you..." is the most common way to give advice in English. Learn it as one block.

3. First vs Second Conditional (real vs imaginary)

This is the heart of the unit. The First Conditional talks about a real, possible future. The Second Conditional talks about something imaginary or unlikely. The grammar tells the listener how you feel about the chance.

IdeaFirst Conditional (real)Second Conditional (imaginary)
StructureIf + Present, will + verbIf + Past, would + verb
A likely future planIf it rains, I will take an umbrella.✗ (this is a real plan)
A dream / unlikely idea✗ (this is not real)If I ruled the world, I would give everyone ice cream.
Meaning"This can really happen.""This is only my imagination."
Golden rule
  • Ask yourself: is it possible? If yes → First Conditional (will). If it is a dream or almost impossible → Second Conditional (would).
  • "If I win this game, I will be happy." (I'm playing now — real.) vs "If I won the World Cup, I would cry." (a dream — imaginary.)

4. would, could and might (in the result)

The result clause does not always use would. We change the modal to change the meaning — how certain the imaginary result is.

ModalMeaningExample
wouldthe sure imaginary resultIf I had a dog, I would walk it every day.
couldwould be able to / a possibilityIf I had a bike, I could ride to school.
mightmaybe (not sure)If you asked her, she might say yes.
Key point
  • All three (would / could / might) are followed by the base verb: would go, could go, might go — never would to go.

5. Two main uses (advice & imagination)

You will use the Second Conditional mostly for two things in real life:

UseHowExample
Giving advicePut yourself in the other person's place with "If I were you..."If I were you, I wouldn't worry so much.
Imagining / dreamingDescribe an unreal situation and its resultIf I could live anywhere, I would choose the coast.
  • If I were you, I would join the team.Advice: I imagine I am you.
  • What would you do if you had a free week?Imagination: a hypothetical question.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B1 students make most often with the Second Conditional. Check your own writing against this list.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
If I would have money, I would travel.If I had money, I would travel.Never use would in the if-clause — use the Past Simple.
If she studies, she would pass.If she studied, she would pass.Imaginary condition → Past Simple, not Present.
If I had a car, I will drive.If I had a car, I would drive.Second Conditional result uses would, not will.
If I was you, I would rest.If I were you, I would rest.Use were for all subjects in careful English.
If I won, I would to buy a kite.If I won, I would buy a kite.After would, use the base verb (no to).

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two friends imagine their dream lives. Read it twice and notice the pattern: If + Past Simple in the condition, would / could in the result.

  • — If you could live in any country, where would you go?Hypothetical question → could + would.
  • — That's easy. If I had the choice, I would move to Italy.If + Past Simple, would + verb.
  • — Really? Why Italy? 
  • — Well, if I lived there, I would eat pasta every day and learn the language.Imaginary result → would.
  • — Ha! If I were you, I would think about the cold winters too.Advice → If I were you, I would...
  • — Good point. And if you found a magic lamp tomorrow, would you still go to school?Hypothetical question → If + Past, would...?
  • — Of course! If I stayed at home all day, I think I would get bored.If + Past Simple, would + verb.
Test yourself
  • Find the sentence with "If I were you". What is the speaker doing? (Giving advice.)
  • "If I lived there, I would eat pasta." Which part is the condition, which is the result? (Condition = If I lived there; result = I would eat pasta.)
  • Change "I would move to Italy" into a question. (Would you move to Italy?)

Learn the words, then use them in your own imaginary sentences.

imagine (v)
to form a picture of something in your mind (hayal etmek)
Syn: picture · Ant: —

dream (n)

Syn: hope, wish · Ant: reality

wish (n)

Syn: desire · Ant: —

Syn: gifted · Ant: hopeless

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think about your own life. If you could have any magic power, what power would you choose?
  • The text is about a child's daydreams. Which grammar structure do you expect to see a lot?
  • Look at the title, "If I Could Fly". Do you think Mila is sad, or just having fun imagining? Guess!
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text for the answers. Notice every if-clause and every would / could / might.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): dream · talent · talented · chance · opportunity · imagine · wonder · regret

A happy daydream: flying, the stars and the deep blue sea.
If I Could Fly: Mila's Daydreams

Mila is ten years old, and she loves school, but her favourite thing in the whole world is daydreaming. Like a lot of children, she often wonders what her life would be like if things were a little different. 'If I had a pet dragon,' she says with a big smile, 'I think I would fly to school every morning.'

Every day, Mila walks to school with her little brother, Kaya. The walk is long, and sometimes it is cold and rainy. She sits in class, listens to her teacher, and does her reading and maths. Mila is a good student, and she likes learning new things. But when a lesson gets boring, her mind starts to wander, and a bright, magical world appears inside her head.

In this daydream world, anything is possible. Mila imagines that she has a small green dragon called Ember. 'If Ember were real,' she thinks, 'we would fly over the mountains together, and I would never be late again.' These little dreams do not stop her from working; instead, they give her fresh energy for the rest of the day.

Mila has many other daydreams too. She has always had a talent for drawing, and she fills her notebooks with pictures of strange animals. 'If I could talk to animals, I would ask my cat where she goes at night,' she says. 'And if I had the opportunity to breathe under water, I would swim with the dolphins and find a secret city under the sea.'

When Mila was younger, she wanted to be an astronaut. She still loves the stars, and on clear nights she looks up at the moon through her bedroom window. 'If I lived on the moon,' she once told Kaya, 'I would bounce everywhere, like a big rubber ball!' Kaya laughed. 'If I were you, I would take lots of snacks,' he said, 'because the moon is very far away!'

Her best friend, Duru, is very talented at music, and the two girls love to imagine adventures together. 'But why a dragon?' Duru once asked. Mila's eyes lit up. 'If I had the chance, I would visit every country in one day,' she answered. 'I would see the jungle, the desert, and the North Pole before dinner!' However, Duru just giggled, because she thought the dragon was only a dream.

Some grown-ups do not really understand Mila's daydreams. 'You should think about real things,' her aunt sometimes says. But Mila does not think that dreaming is a waste of time, and she does not regret a single minute of it. For her, imagining a magical life is like reading a good story or watching a fun film. It costs nothing, so it hurts no one, and it makes every grey day feel brighter.

Of course, Mila knows that dragons are not real. If everyone had a dragon, she says with a laugh, the sky would be very crowded! 'I am very happy with my life,' she admits. 'But it is nice to imagine. If we never dreamed, life would be very grey.' Still, in the morning, she always wakes up ready for a real day at school.

Writing: If I Could Change One Thing

Write and submit your paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Connectors turn sentences into a text (Linking Words)

A list of short, separate sentences is not real writing. What connects your ideas is linking words. At A2 you used and, but, so, because. At B1 you add stronger connectors like also, however, although and still. When you write about imaginary situations, connectors help you move smoothly between a dream and reality.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
andadd informationIf I had a dragon, I would fly, and I would visit my friends.
butshow a small contrastI love my house, but I would still like to see the whole world.
soshow a resultI have no dragon, so I only dream about flying.
becausegive a reasonI would visit the jungle because I love wild animals.
alsoadd an extra pointIf I were a giant, I would help my friends. I would also carry their bags.
howevercontrast between two sentencesI would love to be a singer. However, I am a little shy.
althoughshow a strong contrastAlthough it is only a dream, I imagine it often.
stillsay something is true in spite of thingsMy life is fun, but I still have big dreams.

See the difference — the same ideas, written two ways:

Separate sentences ✗Connected, fluent writing ✓
I am a student. I like my school. I would change one thing. I would have a pet dragon. I have no magic. I am a student, and I like my school. However, if I could change one thing, I would have a pet dragon. Still, I have no magic, so for now it is only a dream.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different connectors in your paragraph. Do not write sentence by sentence; link your ideas.
  • When however / although / still start a sentence, they are usually followed by a comma: "However, it is only a dream."

Paragraph structure (Paragraph Structure)

An "imaginary life" paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same every time — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningA general hypothetical topic sentence (Second Conditional)If I could change one thing about my life, I would learn to fly.
2. BodyTwo or three imaginary situations and their results. Use If + Past Simple, would/could/might + verb, and link with connectors.If I could fly, I would have more adventures, and I might visit the clouds.
3. ClosingA final comment about reality or a wishOf course, it is only a dream — but if I never dreamed, life would be grey.

Task (Görev)

Imagine a magical, different life. Write about what you would do if your life were different. Use the Second Conditional.

WHAT TO INCLUDE

  • an opening hypothetical sentence (If I could..., I would...)

  • at least one sentence with could or might in the result
  • at least one sentence with "If I were..."
  • a closing sentence about reality or a wish

Sample Answer (~165 words) If I could change one thing about my life, I would learn to fly. I live in a small town, and although I love my family, I sometimes wish for more adventures. If I could fly, I would have more chances to explore, and I might visit the clouds every afternoon.

I often wonder what I would do if I had a magical talent. If I were braver, I would ride a friendly dragon, because flying has always been my dream. I would also travel far. If I had the opportunity, I would visit the jungle and stay there for a whole week.

Of course, I know that dreams are not always real. If everyone could fly, the sky would be very crowded! However, it is nice to imagine. If I were you, I would never stop dreaming, because our dreams show us who we really want to be.
