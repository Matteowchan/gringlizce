Ünite 13 · Used to · Bölüm 1/6

Grammar: Used to

Study the rules, look at the examples, then do the exercises.

Talking about the past that has changed

We often want to say that something was true in the past but is not true any more. Maybe you had a habit and you stopped it, or a place was different years ago. English has a special structure for exactly this idea: used to.

Here is the core idea in one line: used to + base verb describes a past habit or a past state that is finished now. When you hear "used to", you know two things at once: it was true before, and it is not true today.

  • I used to be afraid of the dark, but now I sleep with the light off.A past habit that has ended → used to.
  • There used to be a big tree in our garden, but they cut it down.A past state that is no longer true → used to.
  • We used to play outside every day when we were younger.A repeated childhood habit → used to.

1. Positive form (used to + base verb)

The form is wonderfully simple. The subject never changes anything: it is always used to (with a d) plus the base verb (V1), for every person — I, you, he, she, we, they.

Subjectused tobase verbExample
I / you / we / theyused toliveWe used to live in a small town.
he / she / itused tohaveShe used to have long hair.
Key point
  • Never add -s and never change the main verb: She used to have ✓ — She used to has ✗, She used to had ✗.
  • There used to be is very common for describing how a place was: "There used to be a park here."

2. Negative and question (the d disappears!)

This is the most important spelling point in the whole unit. In the negative and the question, we use did / didn't. Because did already shows the past, the d of used drops off — we write use to, not "used to".

FormStructureExample
Positiveused to + V1I used to walk to school.
Negativedidn't use to + V1I didn't use to like vegetables.
QuestionDid + subject + use to + V1?Did you use to play football?
Short answerYes, I did. / No, I didn't."Did she use to dance?" "Yes, she did."
Watch the spelling
  • After did / didn't, write use to (no d): "Did you use to bite your nails?" ✓ — "Did you used to bite your nails?" ✗
  • The d only stays in the positive: I used to be shy. ✓

3. used to vs the Past Simple (habit or single event?)

This is where students make the biggest error. Used to is only for repeated habits or long states. For a single, finished action (something that happened once), you must use the Past Simple.

Ideaused toPast Simple
Repeated habit over timeI used to visit my grandparents every summer.I visited them every summer. (also possible)
One single action (once)✗ not usedLast week I visited them.
Long past stateShe used to live in Paris.She lived in Paris for ten years.
Golden rule
  • If it happened many times or was true for a long time, you can use used to.
  • If it happened only once (yesterday, last week, in 2020), you must use the Past Simple, never "used to".

4. be used to / get used to (a different structure!)

Careful: be used to and get used to look similar but mean something completely different. They mean be / become accustomed to — something feels normal because you do it often. The form is be/get used to + noun or -ing, NOT the base verb.

StructureMeaningExample
used to + V1past habit, now finishedI used to get up late. (before, not now)
be used to + -ingaccustomed to (it feels normal)I am used to getting up early. (no problem now)
get used to + -ingbecoming accustomed toI am getting used to my new school.
Quick test
  • Can you replace it with "was accustomed to"? Then it is be used to + -ing: "She is used to waking up early."
  • Does it mean a past habit that has stopped? Then it is used to + base verb: "She used to be afraid of dogs."

5. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B1 students make most often with "used to". Check your own writing against this list.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
Did you used to live here?Did you use to live here?after "did", the d drops: use to.
I didn't used to like carrots.I didn't use to like carrots.after "didn't", the d drops: use to.
Yesterday I used to see my old friend.Yesterday I saw my old friend.a single event → Past Simple, not used to.
She used to has long hair.She used to have long hair.always the base verb after used to.
I am used to get up early now.I am used to getting up early now.be used to + -ing, not the base verb.

6. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Deniz walks around the old neighbourhood with his grandpa and they talk about how it has changed. Read it twice and notice how they use "used to" for the past and the Past Simple for single events.

  • — This street used to be so quiet, Grandpa! What happened?Past state, now different → used to be.
  • — I know. There used to be a little toy shop here, but it closed in 2019.Past state + a single event (closed) → Past Simple.
  • — Did you use to play here when you were a boy?Question form → did + use to.
  • — Yes! We used to buy sweets here every day. I didn't use to like the busy streets, though.Positive + negative of used to.
  • — And now? Do you like it?Present question.
  • — I am used to the noise now. I got used to it after a few years.be / get used to = accustomed → different structure.
Test yourself
  • Find one negative of "used to". Which spelling does it use? (didn't use to — no d.)
  • "It closed in 2019." Why the Past Simple and not "used to"? (A single, finished event.)
  • What is the difference between "I used to" and "I am used to"? (past habit vs accustomed to now.)

Life Before Smartphones

Last weekend, the electricity in our flat went off for a whole evening. There was no internet, no television, and of course no charged phones. At first my little brother and I felt bored and a little lost. Then our grandmother, who was visiting us, laughed and said, "This is exactly how life used to be when I was your age." That evening, by the light of a few candles, she told us all about life before smartphones, and it was one of the most interesting talks we have ever had.

"When I was a child," Grandma began, "we did not have mobile phones at all. Our family used to have one telephone, and it was fixed to the wall in the hall. If you wanted to call a friend, everybody in the house could hear your conversation! We didn't use to have any privacy, so we learned to keep our secrets in a different way."

I asked her how people used to arrange to meet without messages. She smiled. "We used to plan everything in advance," she said. "If I agreed to meet a friend at the park at four o'clock, I simply had to be there. There was no way to send a quick message to say I was late, so we used to wait patiently, and we always kept our promises."

Grandma also told us that children used to spend much more time outside. "Every afternoon, we would play in the street until it got dark," she remembered. "We used to climb trees, ride our bikes and invent our own games. We did not use to stay inside staring at screens. Our parents used to call us home for dinner by shouting from the window."

Then she talked about how people used to find information. "There was no internet," she said, "so we used to use big, heavy books called encyclopaedias. When I had homework, I used to walk to the library, which was a special place full of quiet and paper. Finding one fact could take an hour, but I think we used to remember things better because of it."

My brother asked if life was boring without phones. Grandma shook her head firmly. "Not at all! We used to write long letters to our cousins, and we waited days for a reply. We used to listen to the radio together as a family, and we used to know all our neighbours. Life was slower, but in some ways it was richer, because we really talked to each other."

Of course, Grandma admitted that not everything used to be better. "If you got lost, it was a real problem," she laughed, "because there were no maps on a phone. And if you missed your favourite programme on television, you simply missed it forever. Today you can watch anything whenever you like, and that is wonderful."

By the time the lights came back on, my brother and I did not even want them. We had spent the whole evening talking, laughing and playing an old card game that Grandma used to love. It felt strange to realise that we had not looked at a single screen for hours, and that we had not missed them at all.

Now, once a week, our family has a "no-screen evening", just like the old days. We play games, read books and tell stories, exactly as Grandma used to do. Smartphones are useful, and I would never want to give mine up completely. However, that evening taught me something important: sometimes the best way to feel close to people is simply to put the screen down and talk.

Writing: Life Before Smartphones

Write and submit your paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Connectors turn sentences into a text (Linking Words)

A list of short, separate sentences is not real writing. What connects your ideas is linking words. At A2 you used and, but, so, because. At B1 you add four more powerful connectors: also, when, while, although and however. These let you compare the past and the present like a confident writer.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
andadd informationWe used to play outside, and we knew all our neighbours.
butshow a small contrastLife was simple, but we were happy.
soshow a resultWe had no phones, so we talked more.
becausegive a reasonI loved summers because we used to visit my grandparents.
alsoadd an extra pointOur town used to be small. It also used to be very quiet.
whensay at what past timeWhen I was a child, we used to walk to school.
whiletwo things at the same timeWhile my parents worked, I used to stay with my grandmother.
althoughshow a strong contrastAlthough we had little money, we did not use to worry.
howevercontrast between two sentencesI did not use to like the city. However, I got used to it.

See the difference — the same facts, written two ways:

Separate sentences ✗Connected, fluent writing ✓
I used to live in a village. It was small. We had no cars. We used to walk everywhere. I moved to the city. I like it now. I used to live in a small village, and we had no cars, so we used to walk everywhere. When I moved to the city, I did not like it. However, I got used to it, and now I love it.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different connectors in your paragraph. Do not write sentence by sentence; link your ideas.
  • When when / while / although / however start a sentence, they are usually followed by a comma: "However, I got used to it."

Paragraph structure (Paragraph Structure)

A "how life used to be" paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same every time — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningA general topic sentence with "used to"Life used to be very different when I was a child.
2. BodyOld habits and states with "used to" and "didn't use to", plus one single event in the Past Simple, linked with connectors.We used to play outside all day. One summer, I fell and broke my arm.
3. ClosingA comment about now, using "nowadays" or "get used to"Nowadays life is faster, but I am used to it now.

Task (Görev)

Write about how life used to be before smartphones and the internet — for you, your family, or your parents' generation. Use "used to" a lot, and mix in the Past Simple for single events.

WHAT TO INCLUDE

  • an opening sentence with "used to" about the past
  • two or three past habits or states (used to + base verb)
  • at least one thing that was NOT true before (didn't use to)
  • one single past event in the Past Simple (last year, one day, in 2010...)
  • a closing sentence about now, using nowadays or get used to
  • at least 3 connectors: and, but, so, because, also, when, while, although, however
  • at least 4 words from the Speaking section (childhood, habit, neighbourhood, memory, tradition, generation, change, nowadays)
