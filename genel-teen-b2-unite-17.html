Grammar: Cleft Sentences

Split a sentence in two to shine a spotlight on the one idea that matters most.

Cleft sentences: putting the spotlight where you want it

A normal sentence spreads its emphasis evenly: "My brother broke the window." But sometimes you want to highlight one part and push it into the listener's focus. To do this, English speakers split ("cleave") one idea into two clauses. The result is a cleft sentence, and it is one of the clearest signs of a confident B2 speaker.

This unit covers the three main patterns. The it-cleft (It was my brother who broke the window) fronts a person, thing, time or reason. The wh-cleft or pseudo-cleft (What broke the window was a stone) builds up to the focus with a what-clause. The reversed wh-cleft (A stone is what broke the window) flips it round. The single skill behind them all is knowing where to place the focus and which link word to use — that, who or what.

  • It was Maria who called the police.it-cleft: emphasises the person (Maria, not anyone else).
  • What I need is a good night's sleep.wh-cleft: builds up to the thing needed.
  • A good night's sleep is what I need.reversed wh-cleft: the focus comes first.
  • It was not until midnight that the guests left.it-cleft with "not until": emphasises how late.

1. The it-cleft (It + be + focus + that / who)

The it-cleft is the most common pattern. You start with empty It, add a form of be, then the word you want to stress (the focus), and finish with a relative-style clause introduced by that (for things) or who (for people).

Focus typePatternExample
a personIt + be + person + who/thatIt was Tom who scored the winning goal.
a thingIt + be + thing + thatIt is the noise that bothers me.
a time / placeIt + be + time/place + thatIt was in 2020 that we first met.
Key point — the two classic errors
  • The opening verb agrees with It, not with the focus: "It is the students who are late" ✓ (not "It are the students"). The verb inside the clause agrees with the focus: "...who are late".
  • Never use what to link an it-cleft: "It was Tom what paid" ✗ — use who or that ✓.

2. The wh-cleft / pseudo-cleft (What + clause + be + focus)

The wh-cleft opens with a What-clause that behaves like a noun, then a form of be, then the focus at the very end. Because the stressed idea comes last, this pattern is perfect for building up to a point.

What-clause (topic)beFocus (stressed)
What I really wantisa quiet week off.
What surprised uswasthe sudden silence.
What the team needsisa clear plan.
B2 subtlety
  • The verb be agrees with the What-clause, which counts as singular: "What I need is answers" ✓ (even though "answers" is plural).
  • To stress an action, use What ... do/does/did + be + (to) verb: "What she did was (to) apologise." Do not repeat the subject: "What she did was she apologised" ✗.

3. The reversed wh-cleft (Focus + be + what-clause)

Here the order of the wh-cleft is flipped: the focus comes first, then be, then the what-clause. It sounds natural in speech when you name the important thing straight away and then explain it.

  • A good teacher is what every child deserves.Focus first, then "is what + clause".
  • Honesty is what I value most in a friend.Same meaning as "What I value most is honesty."
  • Rest is what you need, not more coffee.The reversed form has a punchy, spoken feel.
Watch out — keep "what", drop the extra pronoun
  • The link word stays what, never that/which: "Rest is what you need" ✓ / "Rest is that you need" ✗.
  • Do not add an extra it: "Rest is what you need" ✓ / "Rest is what it you need" ✗.

4. Other cleft openers (All / The reason / The place / The person)

The What-clause is not the only way to build a pseudo-cleft. You can open with All, The reason, The place or The person to focus on exactly what you mean. Each one still ends with be + focus.

OpenerMeaningExample
All (that)"the only thing"All I want is a little peace.
The reason (why)focuses on a causeThe reason we left is that it was dull.
The place / personfocuses on where / whoThe place I feel safest is home.
Punctuation & link words
  • After All use that (usually left out), never what: "All that I need" / "All I need" ✓, but "All what I need" ✗.
  • After The reason use why or that, and finish with is that — not "is because": "The reason is that ..." ✓.

5. It-clefts for time, reason and contrast (It was not until... / It is because...)

The it-cleft can front more than just a person or object. Use it to stress when, why or which one — and to correct a wrong idea with the frame not X but Y.

  • It was not until she spoke that I recognised her."It was not until + time + that ..." stresses how late.
  • It is because of the cost that we hesitate.it-cleft focusing on a reason.
  • It was the ending, not the acting, that ruined the film."not X, Y" corrects a false idea.
Key point
  • After It was not until, the second clause still uses that, and its verb stays positive: "It wasn't until noon that he woke up" ✓ (not "that he didn't wake up").
  • The not ... that frame is powerful for corrections: "It wasn't me that you saw."

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B2 students still make with cleft sentences. Check your own writing against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
It was Sam what won the prize.It was Sam who won the prize.use who/that in an it-cleft, never "what".
What I need are new shoes.What I need is new shoes.the verb agrees with the singular What-clause.
What she did was she left early.What she did was leave early.do not repeat the subject; use (to) + verb.
All what I want is quiet.All I want is quiet.after "All" use "that" (often omitted), not "what".
The reason is because it rained.The reason is that it rained."The reason is that ...", not "is because".

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two friends plan a surprise party. Read it twice and watch how each cleft moves the spotlight: a person after It was ... who, an idea after What ... is, a reversed cleft, and the emphatic It wasn't until ... that.

A:What we need is a plan that Deniz won't notice.wh-cleft: focus on "a plan".
B:Agreed. It was Elif who nearly gave it away last time.it-cleft: focus on the person.
A:True. Secrecy is what matters most now.reversed wh-cleft: focus first.
B:It wasn't until yesterday that I finally booked the room."not until ... that" stresses how late.
A:The reason I chose Saturday is that everyone is free.reason-cleft: "The reason ... is that".
B:Perfect. All we have to do now is keep quiet!All-cleft: "the only thing".
Test yourself
  • Rewrite "Elif nearly gave it away" as an it-cleft focusing on Elif. (It was Elif who nearly gave it away.)
  • Turn "We need a plan" into a wh-cleft. (What we need is a plan.)
  • Fix: "The reason is because everyone is free." (The reason is that everyone is free.)

Reading: The Real Secret of a Long Life

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • What do you think is the single most important thing for a long, healthy life?
  • The text is full of cleft sentences that stress the writer's key points. Which patterns do you expect to see?
  • The title promises a "real secret". Do you expect a surprising answer, or an obvious one?
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text. Underline every cleft sentence — the It is / It was ... that/who and What ... is/was patterns — and ask which idea each one puts in the spotlight.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): striking · crucial · significant · matter · key · priority · highlight · remarkable

The real secret is plain: it is small daily habits, not miracle products, that lengthen a life.
The Real Secret of a Long Life

When our class was handed a debate topic for the term — "Can we really choose how long we live?" — most of us groaned. It sounded like a question for our grandparents, not for teenagers who spend their afternoons at football practice or scrolling through their phones. Yet the more our project group read, the closer to home the whole thing began to feel. If the habits that shape a long life are built slowly, then they are probably being built right now, in the ordinary choices we make at fourteen or fifteen. That was the thought that turned a boring assignment into an argument none of us could drop.

Every year, magazines promise a new secret to a long life: a special diet, an expensive supplement, or the perfect exercise plan. Yet what scientists have actually discovered is far simpler and far harder to sell. It is not one magic food that keeps people healthy into their nineties, but the whole shape of their everyday lives.

That idea unsettled our group at first. We have grown up seeing adverts that treat health like something you can download or buy: a new app, a protein shake, a gadget that counts your steps. It is tempting to believe that one clever purchase could fix everything at once. What the studies kept showing, though, was the opposite of a quick fix. The people who lived longest had not bought their health; they had simply lived, year after year, in a way that quietly built it.

Researchers who study the world's longest-living communities have noticed a striking pattern. In these places, people rarely "exercise" in the way we imagine. What keeps them moving is ordinary life — walking to a neighbour's house, working in a garden, climbing the hills where they live. It is this constant, gentle activity, not the gym, that protects their hearts.

For the sporty members of our team, this was the hardest part to accept. At school we are taught to picture fitness as something intense: a timed run in PE, a match on Saturday, a personal best to beat. Those things are fun and they matter, but the research suggested that the real winners of the longevity race were not athletes at all. They were ordinary people who never sat still for very long. Instead of one exhausting hour of training followed by a whole day slumped on a sofa, their movement was spread thinly across everything they did. One of my friends, who plays basketball, admitted that he trains hard twice a week and then barely moves for the rest of it — and that this balance might be exactly backwards.

Diet matters too, of course. However, what these communities share is not a strict menu but a relaxed attitude to food: they stop eating before they feel full, and their meals are slow and social. It is the company around the table, as much as the food on it, that seems to matter.

Food led us into another argument. As teenagers, we tend to eat quickly, often standing up, sometimes with one hand on a game controller or a phone. Meals in these long-lived communities sounded like the opposite of our snatched lunches between lessons. There was no counting of calories and no guilt, only good company and enough time to notice when they had eaten enough. It made us wonder how many of our own meals we barely taste because a screen is glowing beside the plate. Nobody in the group wanted to give up snacks, but several of us confessed that we rarely stop because we feel full — we stop because the packet is empty.

Perhaps the most surprising finding concerns relationships. What predicts a long life most reliably is not wealth or even good genes; it is strong social ties. People who feel needed, who see friends daily and belong to a community, live measurably longer than those who do not.

That part hit closest of all. We live in a strange time: we can message a hundred people in one evening and still feel completely alone. Our group talked about how a long list of online followers is not the same as a friend who notices when you go quiet, or a table that saves you a seat at lunch. If strong, face-to-face ties really do add years to a life, then the friendships we are forming now — awkward, loud and imperfect as they are — may turn out to be one of the healthiest things about being young. Belonging, it seems, is not a luxury but a kind of medicine that no app can send you.

None of this can be bought in a bottle. On balance, the real secret is almost disappointingly plain: move naturally, eat gently, and stay close to other people. It is these small daily habits, repeated for decades, that quietly add years to a life.

By the time we stood up to present, our debate had changed shape entirely. We had started out expecting to argue about miracle diets and ended up talking about walking to school, eating with our families, and putting the phone down long enough to really listen to a friend. None of these things will win a medal or earn a single "like". That, perhaps, is the point our whole class took away: the choices that matter most for a long, healthy life are small, unglamorous and completely within our reach, even now. We do not have to wait until we are old to begin — we only have to start noticing the ordinary habits we are already building, one quiet day at a time.

Writing: Making Your Point Stand Out

Write and submit an emphatic paragraph that uses clefts to spotlight your key ideas; your teacher will review it.

Cleft sentences that sound B2 (Emphasis & Focus)

At B1 you stated ideas flatly: "I like this city." At B2, a strong paragraph uses the cleft family from this unit to put the spotlight on the idea that matters. The trick is choosing the right pattern and the right link word — that, who or what.

PatternUse it to...Example
it-cleftstress a person or thingIt was the people that won me over.
wh-cleftbuild up to your pointWhat I value most is honesty.
reversed wh-cleftname the focus firstHonesty is what I value most.
It was not until ...stress when something happenedIt was not until later that I understood.
The reason ... is thatstress a causeThe reason I stayed is that I felt safe.
All ... isstress "the only thing"All I wanted was a fresh start.

See the difference — the same facts, written two ways:

Flat writing ✗Confident B2 writing ✓
My town is quiet. The people are friendly. I like the market. I moved here for the calm. I feel at home now. What I love about my town is the calm. It is the market that brings everyone together, and it is the people who make it special. The reason I moved here is that I wanted peace, and peace is what I finally found.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different cleft patterns from the table, and choose the correct link word (that / who for it-clefts, what for wh-clefts).
  • Remember the singular verb after a What-clause: "What I need is ...".

Paragraph structure (An emphatic opinion paragraph)

An emphatic opinion paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningName your topic and state your main claim with a cleft.What makes a good friend is not luck but effort.
2. BodySupport the claim, using clefts to highlight the key points (It is ... that, The reason ... is that).It is trust that holds a friendship together. The reason it lasts is that both people give something.
3. ClosingRestate your view with a final, punchy cleft.On balance, it is the small, daily kindnesses that matter most.

Sample Answer (~185 words) What makes a good friend is not luck but effort, and it is this simple truth that many people overlook.

It is trust, above all, that holds a friendship together. A friend who keeps your secrets and turns up when you need them is worth far more than a hundred casual contacts. What I value most is honesty; a person who will gently tell me the truth is what I want beside me, not someone who only flatters. The reason these qualities are so crucial is that they cannot be bought or faked.

It was not until I moved to a new city, knowing no one, that I truly understood this. What I missed was not parties or crowds but one or two people who really knew me. It is those quiet, loyal friendships that make the biggest difference to how happy we feel.

On balance, then, it is not the number of friends that matters but their quality. Loyalty, honesty and time are what turn an acquaintance into a friend for life.
