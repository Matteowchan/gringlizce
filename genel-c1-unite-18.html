Grammar: Collocation & Idiomatic Precision

Choose the word that keeps the right company — not just a correct word, but the expected one.

The words that belong together

At C1, the gap between grammatically correct and genuinely idiomatic English is closed by collocation — the habitual partnerships that words form. Native speakers say heavy rain, not strong rain; they make a decision, they do not do a decision. Nothing in the grammar forbids the wrong pairing, and the meaning still comes through, yet the mismatch quietly marks a writer as non-native. Precision at this level is less about rules than about hearing which words travel together.

This unit treats collocation as a system rather than a list to memorise. You will work through the four great support verbs (make, do, take, have), the adverb + adjective pairings that intensify meaning (highly likely, deeply concerned, bitterly disappointed), and the verb + noun combinations that carry academic weight (reach a decision, pose a threat, draw a conclusion, raise awareness). You will also learn to spot the most frequent errors and to match a collocation to its register. The goal is fluency you can hear: the right partner, every time.

  • The board will make a decision once it has done the research.make + decision, do + research → the standard support-verb partners.
  • Investors who take a risk early can have a real impact on a start-up.take + risk, have + impact → each noun keeps its own verb.
  • A delay now is highly likely, and staff are deeply concerned.adverb + adjective → the intensifier is fixed by the adjective it modifies.
  • The new levy could pose a threat to jobs and raise awareness of the issue.verb + noun → idiomatic pairings that a literal choice would miss.

1. Support verbs: make vs do (make a decision / do research)

The commonest collocation errors involve the "empty" support verbs make and do, which carry little meaning of their own and simply activate a noun. A rough guide: make tends to go with producing or deciding something (a decision, a suggestion, progress, an effort), while do tends to go with work, tasks and vaguer activity (research, the housework, business, harm). The guide is only a tendency, so the safest habit is to learn the noun and its verb together.

VerbTypical partnersExample
makea decision, a suggestion, progress, an effort, a mistakeShe made a suggestion that saved the project.
doresearch, business, harm, the work, a favourThe team spent months doing research.
make an effort / do your besttwo fixed pairings learners often swapIf you make an effort and do your best, that is enough.
Register & nuance
  • The meaning is unforgiving: "make a mistake" ✓ but "do a mistake" ✗; "do damage" ✓ but "make damage" ✗.
  • Some nouns take either verb with a shift in sense: "do the dinner" (prepare it) vs "make dinner" (cook it) — both idiomatic, not interchangeable.

2. Support verbs: take vs have (take a risk / have an impact)

The other two support verbs behave the same way. Take collocates with actions you choose to perform (a risk, a break, a decision, responsibility, a look), while have collocates with experiences and states (an impact, an effect, a chance, difficulty, an argument). Once again the noun fixes the verb, and swapping them — "do a risk", "make an impact" instead of "have an impact" — is a classic C1 slip.

VerbTypical partnersExample
takea risk, a break, responsibility, a look, actionThey were willing to take a risk on an unknown supplier.
havean impact, an effect, difficulty, a chance, an argumentThe ruling will have an impact on every region.
take action / have an effecttwo high-frequency academic pairingsUnless we take action, the policy will have no effect.
C1 subtlety
  • make and have both go with "impact"-type nouns, but idiom decides: "have an impact" ✓, yet "make an impression" ✓ — each noun keeps its own verb.
  • take is not the same as bring or get in these frames: "take responsibility" ✓ but not "get responsibility" for the same sense.

3. Adverb + adjective (highly likely · deeply concerned)

To intensify an adjective, English rarely lets you use just very. Each adjective attracts its own intensifying adverb, and the pairing is fixed: something is highly likely (not strongly likely), a person is deeply concerned (not highly concerned), and a supporter is bitterly disappointed (not strongly disappointed). Getting the intensifier right is one of the clearest signals of an advanced speaker.

AdverbAdjectives it partnersExample
highlylikely, unlikely, effective, controversialA rerun of the vote is highly unlikely.
deeplyconcerned, rooted, divided, gratefulThe community remains deeply divided.
bitterly / painfullydisappointed, opposed / aware, obviousFans were bitterly disappointed by the loss.
widely / fullyavailable, believed / aware, awareThe report is now widely available online.
Register & nuance
  • The partnership is one-directional: you can be "highly critical" ✓ but not "deeply critical", and "deeply grateful" ✓ but not "highly grateful".
  • Highly favours evaluative adjectives (likely, effective); deeply favours emotional or structural ones (concerned, rooted). Learn each pair, not a rule.

4. Verb + noun (reach a decision · pose a threat)

Beyond the four support verbs, academic and professional English relies on stronger, more specific verb + noun pairings. You reach (or make) a decision, draw a conclusion, pose a threat, raise awareness, meet a deadline, strike a balance and play a role. Each verb has been chosen by convention, not logic, so a literal alternative — "do a conclusion", "give a threat" — sounds instantly wrong.

CollocationMeaningExample
draw a conclusionarrive at a judgement from evidenceIt is too early to draw a conclusion.
pose a threatbe a danger to somethingRising seas pose a threat to coastal cities.
raise awarenessmake people more conscious of an issueThe campaign aims to raise awareness.
strike a balance / play a rolefind a compromise / contribute to an outcomeRegulators must strike a balance and play a key role.
C1 subtlety
  • Verbs are not interchangeable across nouns: you "reach a decision" but "draw a conclusion" — swapping them ("draw a decision") breaks the collocation.
  • Many pairings take a fixed adjective slot: "play a key/pivotal/central role", "strike a delicate balance" — learn the whole chunk.

5. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

A handful of collocation errors recur even in otherwise strong C1 writing: swapping make and do, choosing the wrong intensifying adverb, forcing a literal verb onto a noun, and mixing registers. Audit your writing against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
The interns did several mistakes in the report.The interns made several mistakes in the report."mistake" collocates with make, never do.
We need to do a decision before Friday.We need to make (or reach) a decision before Friday.a decision is made or reached, not done.
The new tax makes a serious threat to farmers.The new tax poses a serious threat to farmers.a threat is posed; "make a threat" means to threaten someone directly.
Residents are strongly concerned about the plan.Residents are deeply concerned about the plan."concerned" partners deeply, not strongly.
In the essay he grabbed a bite of the main argument.In the essay he engaged with the main argument."grab a bite" is informal and out of register in academic prose.

6. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two editors polish a draft report before it goes out. Read it twice and watch the collocations work as a system: a support-verb pairing, an adverb + adjective intensifier, a specific verb + noun combination, and a register fix that swaps a casual phrase for a formal one.

A:You wrote "the study did a big impact" — that pairing sounds off.Wrong support verb for "impact".
B:You're right. It should have a significant impact, shouldn't it?have + impact → the idiomatic pairing.
A:Exactly. And "strongly likely" in the summary — readers will notice.Wrong intensifier for "likely".
B:Change it to highly likely, then. The delay is also bitterly disappointing for the team.adverb + adjective → highly likely, bitterly disappointing.
A:Good. One more: "the plan makes a threat to funding" — wrong verb.Prompts a verb + noun fix.
B:It should pose a threat. And I'll cut "grab a chat with the board" — too casual; let's arrange a meeting.pose a threat + a register fix for tone.
Test yourself
  • Why is "have a significant impact" better than "do a big impact"? ("impact" collocates with have; "do" and "big" are both off.)
  • What is wrong with "strongly likely"? ("likely" partners highly; the intensifier is fixed by the adjective.)
  • Why replace "grab a chat with the board" with "arrange a meeting"? (The casual pairing clashes with the formal register of a report.)

The careful writer sets the dial deliberately — neither at blind certainty nor at empty doubt.
The Careful Voice of Science

To a casual reader, scientific writing can seem frustratingly indecisive. Researchers rarely claim that something simply is true; instead, they report that the evidence suggests, indicates or appears to support a conclusion. This caution is not weakness. It is, arguably, the very heart of the academic method, whereas the confident certainties of advertising or politics often rest on far weaker foundations.

Hedging — the deliberate softening of a claim — allows a writer to be accurate about uncertainty. A responsible study tends to qualify its findings: results may hold to some extent, or by and large, or under certain conditions. However, such phrases are not mere padding. They mark the precise boundary of a claim, and consequently they protect the writer if later evidence proves more complex.

Overstatement, by contrast, is a genuine risk. A single experiment seldom proves anything conclusively; therefore a paper that announces its results as categorical often invites the very scrutiny that undermines it. Yet hedging can itself be overdone. When every sentence is buried under "it could perhaps possibly be argued that", the prose becomes evasive and the argument disappears. To generalise wildly and to hedge endlessly are opposite failures of the same skill.

Skilled academics nonetheless balance caution with the occasional booster: where the evidence is strong, they will state, clearly and without apology, that a result is well established. This balance, presumably, is why hedging is taught as a core academic skill rather than a mere stylistic habit. Seemingly modest, the hedged sentence is in fact a quietly confident one: it claims exactly what it can defend, and not a word more. What looks tentative is often the most plausible claim on the page; what sounds categorical is frequently the most speculative.

Speaking Task 2 (Re-tell with Measured Caution)

Below is an over-confident paragraph. Every sentence overgeneralises or overstates: it claims far more than any evidence could support. Your job is to re-tell it in a cautious academic register, hedging each claim to exactly what could reasonably be defended.

Social media destroys the attention span of every young person. It always makes teenagers anxious and never has any benefit at all. This study proves beyond doubt that screens are the sole cause of the problem, and everyone knows it. The solution is obvious and works for absolutely everyone.
Grammar requirement — apply this unit's topic
  • Replace absolute claims (every, always, never, proves, everyone) with hedged verbs (appears to, tends to, suggests) and limited scope (some, in many cases).
  • Use a modal hedge (may well, may possibly) and at least one adverbial hedge (arguably, to some extent, by and large, presumably).
  • Keep one booster only if a claim is genuinely secure, and concede a limitation.
  • Write 6–8 sentences. Keep the topic; change only how strongly you claim it.

Sentence starters (you can begin with these):

"There is a tendency for social media to ..." "The evidence suggests that it may well affect some ..." "Arguably, screens contribute to ..., to some extent." "This single study does not prove ...; it merely indicates ..." "I concede that ..., yet no solution works for everyone."

Writing: A Measured Academic Argument

Write and submit a discursive paragraph in cautious academic register; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound C1 (Linking Words)

Cautious argument still needs to flow. At C1 you gain a sharper, more formal set of connectors — whereas, however, consequently, by contrast, nonetheless and therefore — which let you concede, contrast and conclude without losing your measured tone. Used precisely, they hold a hedged argument together.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
howeverqualify or contrast the previous sentenceThe trend is clear. However, the sample was small.
by contrastset one case sharply against anotherOlder users are cautious; younger ones, by contrast, are not.
consequentlystate a measured result or effectData were limited; consequently, the claim is tentative.
whereasdraw a direct, balanced contrastThe theory is elegant, whereas the evidence is thin.
nonethelessconcede a point, then hold your positionThe effect is small; nonetheless, it appears real.
thereforedraw a cautious conclusionThe data are mixed; therefore, caution is advisable.

See the difference — the same idea, written two ways:

Over-confident ✗Measured C1 register ✓
Remote work destroys teamwork. It always isolates staff and never helps anyone. This proves offices are essential. Remote work may well weaken some forms of teamwork; however, the evidence suggests that its effects vary widely. By and large, it isolates some staff, whereas others thrive. This indicates that offices remain valuable to some extent, though hardly essential for all.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different C1 connectors from the table. Weave your argument; don't list claims.
  • However, Consequently, Nonetheless, Therefore usually open a sentence and take a comma: "Consequently, the claim must stay tentative."

How to structure a measured argument (Argument Structure)

A strong C1 argument is not a run of bold assertions. It states a position, concedes what is uncertain, weighs the counter-case, and lands a conclusion that is exactly as strong as the evidence allows. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the shape:

PartWhat to writeLanguage to reach for
1. Positionstate your claim, already hedgedthe evidence suggests / it could be argued that
2. Supportgive a reason, limiting its scopetends to / by and large / to some extent
3. Concessionadmit a counterpoint or limitationI concede that / however / whereas
4. Conclusioncommit only as far as the evidence allowstherefore / arguably / clearly (once, if secure)

Task (Görev)

Write a discursive paragraph on a debatable question — for example, "Does artificial intelligence threaten more jobs than it creates?" or a topic of your choice. Argue like a careful academic: claim only what you can defend, and calibrate every sentence.

WHAT TO INCLUDE

  • a hedged position using a cautious verb (the evidence suggests / it could be argued that)
  • at least one modal hedge (may well, may possibly) grading your certainty
  • at least two adverbial or phrasal hedges (arguably, presumably, to some extent, by and large, in general)
  • one concession and, if a claim is genuinely secure, one booster (clearly, undoubtedly)
  • at least 3 C1 connectors: however, whereas, consequently, by contrast, nonetheless, therefore
  • at least 4 words from the Speaking section (tentative, qualify, overstate, plausible, presumably, speculative, concede, nuance, categorical, generalise)
  • no over-certainty (avoid always, never, everyone, proves) and no hedge-stacking

EXAMPLE OPENERS

"It could be argued that automation ..." "The evidence appears to suggest that, by and large, ..." "While I concede that ..., the claim remains, to some extent, ..."

Sample Answer (~205 words) It could be argued that artificial intelligence threatens more jobs than it creates, though the evidence is, presumably, more mixed than headlines suggest. Automation tends to displace routine tasks, and by and large those roles are the most exposed. However, it would overstate the case to claim that machines simply destroy work; the same technologies may well generate demand for skills that did not previously exist.

To some extent, the outcome depends on how quickly workers can retrain. I concede that this is a genuine limitation: retraining is slow, and the transition may possibly leave some communities behind for years. Consequently, any confident forecast in either direction remains largely speculative.

What is clearly true is that the effect will vary by sector, whereas earlier waves of automation were narrower. Manufacturing appears more vulnerable than caring professions, which arguably depend on a human nuance that algorithms struggle to reproduce.

Therefore, rather than making a categorical prediction, it seems more plausible to qualify the claim: artificial intelligence will, in general, reshape employment more than it eliminates it, provided that policy keeps pace. Nonetheless, that reassurance holds only if retraining is taken seriously; without it, the disruption could well prove severe.
