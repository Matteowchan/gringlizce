Ünite 10 · Advanced Passive & Reporting · Bölüm 1/6

Grammar: Advanced Passive & Reporting

Report what is claimed without taking the blame for it. Learn the grammar of distance.

Reporting at a distance

By C1 you already handle the ordinary passive. The advanced skill is different: it is using the passive to report what people say, think and claim while keeping yourself, the writer, at a careful distance. This is the grammar of the news bulletin, the academic paper and the cautious official statement — the grammar that lets you present a claim as a claim, not as a fact you personally guarantee.

Two engines drive this unit. The impersonal report — It is thought / believed / claimed / reported that... — opens with an empty it and states the view without naming who holds it. The personal report — She is thought to know... / He is said to have left... — lifts the subject to the front and follows it with a to-infinitive. Master the switch between them, add a perfect infinitive for earlier events, and you gain the objective, non-committal tone that marks writing as unmistakably C1.

  • It is believed that the tomb dates from the Bronze Age.Impersonal report → a shared view, no source named, empty "it".
  • The minister is said to be under considerable pressure.Personal report → subject fronted, to-infinitive for a present state.
  • The company is alleged to have misled its investors.Perfect infinitive → the act is earlier than the reporting.
  • Two hikers got stranded on the ridge overnight.Get-passive → a sudden, unfortunate event that befell them.

1. The impersonal report (It is said that...)

The impersonal pattern begins with a "dummy" subject it, a passive reporting verb, and a full that-clause. Because no one is named as the source, the writer reports the idea while distancing themselves from it. This is the workhorse of academic and journalistic prose: It is thought that..., It is widely believed that..., It has been claimed that...

PatternWhat it signalsExample
It is said/thought that + clausea general, unattributed beliefIt is thought that the vaccine reduces infection.
It is reported/claimed that + clausea news claim the writer won't guaranteeIt is reported that talks have collapsed.
It was believed/alleged that + clausethe same, set in the pastIt was believed that the disease spread by air.
Register & nuance
  • The reporting verb, not the that-clause, carries the tense: "It was reported that prices had fallen." keeps a clean past sequence.
  • Adverbs sharpen the stance: It is widely believed..., It is generally agreed..., It is officially denied...

2. The personal report (S is said to + inf)

The personal pattern fronts the subject and follows a passive reporting verb with a to-infinitive — never a that-clause. Use to + base when the reported situation is at the same time as the report, and to have + V3 when it is earlier. This is where most C1 errors live, so learn the two shapes precisely.

FormTime relationExample
is said to + basesame time as the report (state/habit)She is said to own three galleries.
is said to be + -inghappening now, in progressHe is believed to be hiding abroad.
is said to have + V3earlier than the reportThey are said to have left the country.
C1 subtlety — the "that" trap
  • After a personal passive report, never use "that": "He is said that he owns..." ✗ → "He is said to own..." ✓
  • Match the infinitive to the timeline: "He is believed to have escaped last night" (earlier), not "to escape" (still to come).

3. Reporting in the past & with other verbs (was alleged to be)

The same two patterns work with a wide family of reporting verbs — expect, know, understand, allege, report, consider, presume — and in the past tense. When the reporting verb is past, the perfect infinitive still marks an even earlier event. The passive is chosen precisely because it lets you drop the agent: you need not say who alleged or expected anything.

PatternNuanceExample
was alleged to + basea past accusation, source withheldHe was alleged to be the ringleader.
was alleged to have + V3a past accusation about an earlier actShe was alleged to have taken the funds.
is expected/understood to + basea cautious present forecast or readingPrices are expected to rise sharply.
Why the passive here?
  • It removes the agent for objectivity: "The reforms are considered a success" sounds neutral; naming "we consider" sounds partisan.
  • The active-to-passive shift promotes the object: "Analysts expect prices to fall" → "Prices are expected to fall."

4. The get-passive (get arrested)

Alongside the be-passive, informal English uses get + V3. The get-passive is more dynamic and often carries a nuance of something sudden, accidental or unfortunate happening to the subject — frequently beyond their control. It belongs to speech and lighter registers, not to the most formal academic prose.

Get-passiveNuanceExample
got arrestedsudden, unfortunate, to the subjectHe got arrested at the border.
got injured / got lostan accident, unplannedThree fans got injured in the crush.
get promoted / get paida change the subject undergoesShe finally got promoted last month.
C1 subtlety — register
  • There is no "was got": "He was got arrested" ✗ → "He got arrested" ✓ or "He was arrested" ✓.
  • Prefer the be-passive in formal writing: "Two workers were injured" reads as more neutral than "got injured".

5. Choosing an objective register (distance & hedging)

Reporting structures are a writer's main tool for objectivity. Instead of committing to a fact, you frame it as reported, expected or alleged — and you drop the agent so no single person is named. This is how academics and journalists stay cautious and fair. Choosing the right frame is a matter of how much you are willing to vouch for.

You want to...Reach forExample
state a shared view neutrallyIt is generally agreed that...It is generally agreed that the plan failed.
report an unverified accusationis alleged to have...The firm is alleged to have hidden losses.
forecast cautiouslyis expected / is likely to...Turnout is expected to reach a record.
C1 subtlety
  • Reporting language lets you present opposing claims fairly: "The site is claimed to be ancient, yet the evidence has not been corroborated."
  • Over-use sounds evasive. Mix reported claims with committed statements so your own voice is still audible.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors that still surface in otherwise strong C1 writing. Each is a reporting slip, not just a form slip. Audit every report you write against these rows.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
He is said that he speaks six languages.He is said to speak six languages.the personal report takes a to-infinitive, never "that".
She is believed to left the country.She is believed to have left the country.an earlier event needs the perfect infinitive "to have + V3".
It is thought the plan to be risky.It is thought that the plan is risky.the impersonal "it" pattern needs a full that-clause.
The suspect was got arrested at dawn.The suspect got arrested at dawn.there is no "was got"; use "got" or the be-passive "was arrested".
Analysts are expected the prices to fall.Prices are expected to fall.in the passive report the object is promoted to subject.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

An editor and a junior reporter argue over how to word a breaking story. Read it twice and watch the reporting choices work as a system: an impersonal report for an unattributed claim, a personal report with a perfect infinitive for an earlier act, a passive to withhold the source, and a get-passive for the sudden misfortune.

E:The minister is said to have resigned — but has anyone confirmed it?Personal report + perfect infinitive → an earlier act, unconfirmed.
R:Not yet. It is reported that she left the building an hour ago.Impersonal report → a claim we don't personally guarantee.
E:Then write "is believed to have left", not "left" — we can't verify it.The passive keeps a careful distance from the fact.
R:Understood. And the aide who got arrested last night?Get-passive → a sudden, unfortunate event.
E:He is only alleged to have leaked the files. Keep the source out of it."is alleged to have" → an unproven accusation, agent dropped.
R:So nothing is stated as fact until it has been corroborated.Passive again → neutral, cautious, objective tone.
Test yourself
  • Why "is said to have resigned" rather than "is said to resign"? (The resigning is earlier than the report, so the perfect infinitive is needed.)
  • What does "It is reported that..." add that "She left..." would not? (It frames the claim as reported, not personally guaranteed by the writer.)
  • Rewrite "got arrested" formally. Does the tone change? ("was arrested" — more neutral, less of the sudden-misfortune colour.)

Reading: The City That May Never Have Existed

Read the feature, then work through the five tasks.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Skilled readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think of a "fact" you once accepted that later turned out to be only a claim. How was it first presented to you — as certain, or as reported?
  • The text is a newspaper feature about a disputed archaeological discovery. Which structures will let the writer report the claim without endorsing it?
  • Watch how often the writer avoids naming a source — "it is believed", "is said to have". Ask what that distance achieves.
How to read
  • First read: read quickly for the whole arc — what was claimed, and why the claim is now doubted. Don't stop for every word.
  • Second read: track the reporting structures — an impersonal report ("It is thought that"), a personal report with a perfect infinitive ("is believed to have identified"), a past accusation ("they allege"), and a distancing passive ("has never been corroborated"). Ask what each one does.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): allege · corroborate · substantiate · disseminate · ostensibly · hearsay · unsubstantiated · scrutinise

One inscription, one bold claim — and a discipline still waiting for the evidence to be corroborated.
The City That May Never Have Existed

For decades, the ruins beneath the desert plateau have been claimed to be the remains of a legendary trading capital. When the discovery was first announced, it was reported around the world as one of the great finds of the century. Recently, however, the claim has been called into question, and the site is now said to be at the centre of a bitter academic dispute.

To appreciate why the announcement caused such a stir, it is worth recalling how little had previously been known about the region. For generations the plateau was thought to be little more than a windswept expanse of sand and rubble, of interest only to the occasional surveyor. Local traditions, it is true, had long spoken of a fabled market city buried beneath the dunes, yet such stories were routinely dismissed by specialists as folklore rather than testimony. When aerial and satellite imagery is said to have revealed a grid of unnaturally regular shadows beneath the surface, that indifference is reported to have given way to feverish speculation almost overnight. Expeditions were hastily assembled, permits were sought, and a plateau that had been ignored for a century was suddenly described as the most promising archaeological prospect in the entire region.

The lead archaeologist is believed to have identified the city from a single inscription. It is thought that the carving names a ruler mentioned in ancient chronicles; consequently, the team argued, the settlement must be the lost capital itself. The interpretation was widely disseminated before it had been independently checked.

Much of the controversy, it should be stressed, turns on questions of method rather than of honesty. The inscription is said to have been recovered from a collapsed chamber that had already been disturbed in antiquity, and its precise archaeological context is therefore difficult to reconstruct. Radiocarbon dating, it is pointed out, cannot be applied to the stone itself, while the organic material found alongside it is thought to have been contaminated by later intrusions. Supporters of the identification maintain that the style of the lettering is consistent with the period in question; sceptics counter that comparable scripts are known to have remained in use, with little discernible change, for many centuries. Because the object is believed to have been removed before the surrounding layers had been properly documented, both sides are effectively reasoning from an incomplete record, and each is convinced that the missing evidence would ultimately have vindicated its own position.

Critics are far less convinced. The inscription, they allege, is ostensibly royal but may in fact have been added centuries later. Whereas the original report presented the identification as certain, rival scholars insist that it has never been corroborated. One specialist is said to have dismissed the evidence as "hearsay dressed up as history."

The stakes, it must be said, extend well beyond a single settlement. Were the identification to be confirmed, the map of the region's ancient commerce would have to be substantially redrawn, since a capital of that magnitude is thought to have anchored a network of routes stretching across several modern frontiers. Museums are reported to be competing quietly for the right to display any artefacts recovered, and at least one government is said to have anticipated a surge in cultural tourism should the story hold. Precisely because so much has been invested in the outcome, sceptics warn, the temptation to see confirmation where none exists is thought to be unusually strong. A discovery of this kind, they argue, is all too easily shaped by the hopes of those who make it, and expectation can quietly harden into conviction long before the underlying evidence has been secured.

Nor, it seems, is the royal reading the only interpretation on offer. Several epigraphers have proposed that the carving may commemorate a merchant guild or a provincial dignitary rather than a sovereign, in which case the grander identification would collapse entirely. Others suggest that the settlement, though undoubtedly ancient, is more likely to have been a modest caravan halt than a fabled capital: prosperous, perhaps, but hardly the metropolis of legend. It has even been proposed that two distinct sites, occupied in different eras, have been mistakenly conflated into a single, conveniently spectacular narrative. What unites these rival accounts is a shared insistence that the most exciting explanation ought not to be preferred simply because it is the most exciting. Extraordinary claims, the critics repeatedly remind us, are ordinarily expected to rest on extraordinary evidence, and that standard, they contend, has manifestly not yet been met.

The affair has also come to be treated as a cautionary tale about the pressures that now shape research. The initial discovery is thought to have been publicised through press releases and televised interviews long before any peer-reviewed study had appeared, and the resulting excitement is said to have made a sober reassessment all the more difficult. Grants, it is widely acknowledged, tend to flow towards headline-grabbing finds, and a public retraction can prove professionally costly in a way that a bold assertion rarely is. Some observers have gone so far as to argue that the discipline itself is partly to blame, having quietly created incentives that reward speed and spectacle over patience and proof. Whether or not the site is eventually vindicated, the episode is expected to be cited for years as an illustration of how readily a compelling story can outrun the facts that are supposed to support it. Younger researchers, in particular, are said to be watching the outcome closely, uncertain whether caution or boldness is the safer path in a field where careers are increasingly made and unmade in public.

The dispute matters because reputations and funding depend on it. The findings are expected to be re-examined by an independent panel next year, and every claim will be scrutinised in detail. Notwithstanding the doubts, the excavation continues, and further inscriptions are believed to have been buried nearby. Nonetheless, until the central claim can be substantiated, the "lost city" is likely to remain, in one critic's words, a story that was told before it was proven.

Writing: A Balanced Report

Write and submit an objective report on a contested claim; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound C1 (Linking Words)

At B2 you linked ideas with however, therefore, although, whereas. At C1 you gain a sharper, more formal register with connectors such as moreover, consequently, notwithstanding, whereas and nonetheless. In a balanced report they let you weigh a claim against its counter-evidence without ever sounding partisan.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
moreoveradd a stronger, further pointThe source is anonymous. Moreover, the claim is unproven.
consequentlystate a formal result or effectNo proof was offered; consequently, the report was retracted.
notwithstandingconcede "in spite of" (very formal)Notwithstanding the doubts, the study was published.
whereasdraw a direct contrast between two ideasSupporters call it certain, whereas critics call it hearsay.
nonethelesssay "even so", after a concessionThe claim is disputed; nonetheless, it is still cited.
howevercontrast between two sentencesThe story spread fast. However, it was never verified.

See the difference — the same claim, written two ways:

Committed & reckless ✗Reported & balanced C1 ✓
The manager stole the funds. The auditor helped him. They destroyed the records. The company failed because of them. The manager is alleged to have taken the funds, and it is claimed that the auditor assisted him. The records are said to have been destroyed; consequently, the firm collapsed. Notwithstanding these claims, nothing has yet been corroborated.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different C1 connectors from the table. Weigh the claim against the evidence; don't just list sentences.
  • Moreover, Consequently, However, Nonetheless usually open a sentence and take a comma: "Consequently, the claim was withdrawn."

How to structure a balanced report (Report Structure)

A strong C1 report does not blurt out a verdict. It presents a claim as reported, weighs the evidence for and against it, and reaches a cautious conclusion — all while keeping the writer's own voice measured and fair. The skeleton stays the same; you change the content, not the shape:

PartWhat to writeStructures to reach for
1. The claimstate what is claimed, source withheldIt is claimed / believed that... ; is said to be...
2. The case forreport the supporting argumentis thought to have... ; is expected to...
3. The case againstreport the doubts and counter-evidenceis alleged to... ; has not been corroborated
4. Cautious verdictreach a measured, hedged conclusionuntil it is substantiated... ; is likely to remain...

Sample Answer (~205 words) It has recently been claimed that a common food dye may significantly increase the risk of certain cancers. The assertion, first made in a single laboratory study, was rapidly disseminated online before it had been reviewed. It is now believed to have alarmed millions of consumers, several of whom got caught up in a wave of panic buying of "dye-free" products.

The dye is said to have caused tumours in mice given extremely high doses. Its supporters argue that the mechanism is plausible; moreover, they insist that caution costs little. Consequently, several retailers have already withdrawn the affected products.

The critics, however, are unconvinced. The doses, they allege, were ostensibly realistic but in fact hundreds of times higher than any human intake. Whereas the original study presented the danger as established, independent laboratories report that the result has never been corroborated, and one regulator has dismissed it as unsubstantiated.

Notwithstanding the headlines, then, the science remains unsettled. The findings are expected to be re-examined by a larger trial next year. Nonetheless, until the claim can be properly substantiated, the reported risk is likely to remain exactly that — reported, and not yet proven.
