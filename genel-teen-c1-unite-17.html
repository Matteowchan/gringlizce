Grammar: The Subjunctive & Formulaic Structures

A quiet, formal mood that survives in demands, wishes and fixed phrases.

The mood that refuses to die

English is often said to have lost its subjunctive, yet the mood is very much alive — it has simply retreated into the most formal, most emphatic corners of the language. The subjunctive is not a tense; it is a mood that expresses something unreal, demanded or merely imagined rather than stated as a plain fact. Where the indicative reports what is (he goes), the subjunctive reaches for what should, might or must be (that he go, if he were). At C1, recognising and producing it is what separates confident formal writing from careful-but-flat prose.

This unit treats the subjunctive as a small, tidy system. You will learn the mandative subjunctive after verbs, nouns and adjectives of demand and importance (insist, demand, suggest, require; it is essential that…), the were-subjunctive for unreal and wished-for situations (if I were, as if, I wish, would rather), and the fossilised formulae in which the mood survives whole (be that as it may, come what may, suffice it to say, if need be). You will also see how British and American English differ, and where the base form is not the same as an ordinary present tense.

  • The student council demanded that the treasurer resign at once.Mandative subjunctive → base form (resign, not resigns) after a verb of demand.
  • It is essential that every applicant be informed in writing."be" (not "is") after an adjective of importance — the tell-tale sign of the mood.
  • If I were in her position, I would decline the nomination.Were-subjunctive → hypothetical, unreal situation (not "was").
  • Be that as it may, the decision still stands.Fossilised formula → a fixed subjunctive that no longer changes.

1. The mandative subjunctive (insist / demand that he go)

After certain verbs of demanding, suggesting or urging, the that-clause takes the base form of the verb — the bare infinitive with no -s, no do, and no change for tense. So it is demand that he go, never goes; insist that she be present, never is. The base form stays the same for every subject, and the negative simply adds not before it (ask that he not attend).

TriggerStructureExample
verb: insist, demand, suggest, recommend, require, propose, urge, requestverb + that + subject + baseThe student council insisted that he step down.
adjective: essential, vital, important, imperative, crucial, advisableit is + adj + that + subject + baseIt is vital that she be told immediately.
noun: demand, request, recommendation, requirement, proposalnoun + that + subject + baseTheir demand that the entry fee be waived was refused.
Register & nuance
  • The base form never takes -s: "The rules require that each member renew annually" ✓ (not renews).
  • Negation goes straight before the base verb: "We ask that students not talk" ✓ — never "do not talk" in this frame.
  • Beware: suggest and insist can also just report a fact, and then they are not mandative: "The data suggest that he is right" (a report) vs "I suggest that he be elected" (a proposal).

2. The were-subjunctive (if I were · as if · I wish)

For unreal, hypothetical or wished-for situations, formal English uses were for every subject — if I were, if he were, if it were — not the everyday was. This "past subjunctive" appears after unreal if, after as if / as though, after wish and if only, after would rather and suppose, and in the formal frame if it were not for (or, with inversion, were it not for).

TriggerMeaningExample
if + werean unreal or hypothetical conditionIf I were you, I would wait.
as if / as though + werea comparison with something untrueHe talks as if he were in charge.
wish / if only + werea wish about the presentI wish it were that simple.
were it not forformal inversion = "if it were not for"Were it not for the delay, we would be finished.
C1 subtlety
  • In careful/formal English, use were, not was, even with I / he / she / it: "If she were here…" ✓ sounds more formal than "If she was here."
  • Inversion drops the if and fronts were: "Were the offer genuine, we would accept" = "If the offer were genuine…"
  • Keep the indicative for real past situations: "When I was young…" is a fact, not a hypothesis — were would be wrong here.

3. Formulaic subjunctives (be that as it may · come what may)

The oldest layer of the subjunctive survives in fixed formulae that must be learned whole. These are fossilised: you cannot change the verb form or reorder the words. Some concede a point (be that as it may); some express determination (come what may); some open a summary (suffice it to say); and some cover every option (be it… or…). Used sparingly, they lend real weight to formal speech and writing.

FormulaMeaningExample
be that as it may"even if that is true" — concedes, then moves onBe that as it may, the deadline cannot change.
come what may"whatever happens" — firm resolveShe vowed to finish, come what may.
suffice it to say"it is enough to say" — a short summarySuffice it to say, the presentation did not go well.
be it… or… / if need be"whether it is…" / "if necessary"Everyone, be it student or teacher, must sign; we will stay late, if need be.
Register & nuance
  • These are frozen: "be that as it may" ✓ never becomes "is that as it may" ✗.
  • Other fossils to recognise: far be it from me to…, so be it, God/heaven forbid, long live…, as it were, come rain or shine.
  • They are emphatic, so use one per paragraph — a page of formulae quickly sounds theatrical.

4. The "should" alternative & BrE/AmE (that he should go)

The bare subjunctive (demand that he go) is the norm in American English and in formal British English. In everyday British English, however, many speakers prefer should + base form (demand that he should go) — the so-called "putative should". Both are correct; what you must avoid is the third option, an ordinary present-tense verb (demand that he goes), which mixes the two systems.

VersionRegister / varietyExample
bare subjunctiveAmE & formal BrE; conciseThey recommended that she apply early.
should + basecommon in BrE; slightly softerThey recommended that she should apply early.
indicative ✗avoid in this frameThey recommended that she applies early.
C1 subtlety — choose one system
  • Pick base or should + base — never double up: "insist that he should be told" ✓ or "insist that he be told" ✓, but not "should be be".
  • With be, the contrast is clearest: "It is essential that he be present" (subjunctive) vs "…that he is present" (only correct as a plain report, not a demand).

5. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

Four errors recur even in otherwise strong C1 writing: adding -s to a mandative subjunctive, mis-negating it, using was where the mood needs were, and tampering with a frozen formula. Audit your writing against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
The council demanded that he resigns immediately.The council demanded that he resign immediately.the mandative subjunctive uses the base form — no -s, whatever the subject.
We ask that students do not enter this area.We ask that students not enter this area.negate a subjunctive with plain not + base, not do not.
If I was in charge, I would change the rule.If I were in charge, I would change the rule.an unreal condition takes the were-subjunctive in formal English.
It is essential that the project is finished today.It is essential that the project be finished today."essential that…" is a mandative trigger, so use be, not is.
Is that as it may, we must proceed.Be that as it may, we must proceed.the formula is fossilised; the verb stays be and cannot be conjugated.

6. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

A student council chair and a member discuss a disciplinary decision about a club captain. Read it twice and watch the subjunctive work as a system: a mandative base form for a demand, an adjective trigger with be, a were-subjunctive for a hypothetical, and a fossilised formula to concede a point.

A:The report is damning. I move that the captain be suspended at once.Mandative "be" after "move that" → a formal demand.
B:I would rather we waited for her account first."would rather" + past subjunctive → a hypothetical preference.
A:It is essential that every party be heard, I agree."it is essential that… be" → adjective trigger, base form.
B:If I were in her position, I would want that chance.Were-subjunctive → an unreal, hypothetical situation.
A:Be that as it may, the rules demand that we act within a week.Fossilised concession + mandative "act" (not "acts").
B:Agreed. We proceed, come what may — but only after the hearing.Formulaic "come what may" → resolve, used once for weight.
Test yourself
  • Why is it "that the captain be suspended" and not "is suspended"? ("move that" is a mandative trigger, so the base form is required.)
  • Why "If I were" rather than "If I was"? (It is an unreal, hypothetical situation — formal English uses the were-subjunctive.)
  • Could you change "be that as it may" to "is that as it may"? (No — it is a frozen formula; the verb stays "be".)

Declared dead, the subjunctive simply sank out of sight — surviving in law, ceremony and fixed idiom.
The Ghost in the Grammar

Every few decades, someone announces that the English subjunctive is dead. The claim is understandable, for the mood no longer changes the verb in most ordinary sentences. Instead of a distinct set of endings, English now leans on word order and modal verbs to do much of the work the subjunctive once did. Yet the mood has not vanished; it has merely gone underground, surviving wherever the language turns formal, legal or ceremonial.

For most teenagers, the first brush with this hidden mood comes not in a grammar lesson but in the wild. A debating society opens its motions with "This house demands that the school abolish exams"; a favourite film ends with a hero who vows to press on, come what may; a set text for literature class carries a line as old as "if music be the food of love". Few students pause to label these as subjunctives, and fewer still could explain why the verb looks so bare. To the untrained ear they register merely as something faintly grand, a little old-fashioned — the way a friend sounds when a moment turns serious. That instinct is exactly right, and it is the thread this article means to follow.

Consider the courtroom. It is essential that every witness tell the truth, and the court demands that the accused be heard before any verdict is reached. These are not careless slips; they are deliberate survivals. Legal English is prescriptive by design, and consequently it preserves older forms long after everyday speech has abandoned them. Whereas a friend might casually say "I suggested that he leaves", a statute will insist that a party "comply" — the base form, with no ending at all.

Schools, as it happens, are miniature versions of that courtroom. When a student council draws up its constitution, it reaches instinctively for the same grave register: the rules may require that every candidate submit a manifesto, or stipulate that no motion be debated twice in a single term. A debate chair who rules that the proposer be heard in silence is, without knowing it, wielding a mood older than the building around them. The reason is exactly the one that governs the law. A club that writes "we ask that each member is punctual" sounds as though it is merely hoping; a club that writes "we ask that each member be punctual" sounds as though it has laid down a binding rule. Teenagers feel that difference long before they can name it, and the sharpest debaters learn to exploit it on purpose.

The were-subjunctive is equally stubborn. A judge may write as if the statute were a living creature, and millions of us say "if I were you" without a second thought. However, the most fossilised survivals are the fixed formulae. The phrase "come what may" expresses resolve; "be that as it may" concedes a point before pressing on; and "so be it" accepts an outcome with grim finality. By contrast with productive grammar, these cannot be altered at all: the verb is frozen for ever.

The fixed formulae, for their part, travel even further from the lawcourt. They surface on school crests and mottos, in the captions students write beneath their photographs, and in the half-joking oaths friends swear to one another before a difficult exam. "Come rain or shine", "so be it", "be that as it may" — each is a fossil, a tiny survival of a grammar that once ranged freely across the whole language. What makes them so durable is precisely that no one assembles them afresh; they are lifted whole, like a quotation, and dropped into a sentence to lend it gravity or wit. A student who writes "be that as it may, the project is still due on Friday" borrows, in a handful of words, centuries of formal weight. Learned in bulk and used with restraint, such phrases can make a young writer's prose sound assured well beyond their years.

History lends the mood still more of its solemn colour. Generations of students have chanted "long live the school" at assemblies, sung anthems that plead "God save the team", or recited pledges in which they promise that they "be loyal" and "do their duty". Each of these is a ceremonial subjunctive, kept alive by ritual rather than by any everyday need. Ceremony is conservative by nature: it prizes the familiar words precisely because they are familiar, and so it becomes a kind of museum in which archaic grammar is preserved intact. Indeed, this is why the mood feels so at home at a graduation or a prize-giving, and so oddly out of place in a hurried text message. The occasion, as much as the grammar itself, tells us which register we have entered.

None of this is idle antiquarianism, least of all for a student staring at a blank essay page. Examiners at this level reward a candidate who can change register at will, and the subjunctive is one of the quietest, most economical ways to do so. A single well-placed "it is imperative that the policy be reviewed" signals command of the language far more efficiently than a paragraph crammed with long words. Recognising the mood also protects a writer from error: knowing that "demand that he go" takes the base form saves them from the tempting but faulty "demand that he goes". The grammar that looks most archaic, then, turns out to be one of the most practical tools a teenager can carry into an academic argument.

Some argue that such forms are archaic and should therefore be dropped in the name of plain English. Nonetheless, they persist, because they carry a weight and a formality that plainer wording simply cannot supply. A demand phrased with the subjunctive sounds like a rule; the same idea in the indicative sounds like a mere suggestion. Suffice it to say, then, that the ghost in the grammar shows no real sign of leaving — and that the writer who commands it holds a quiet advantage over the one who does not.

Writing: A Formal Argument

Write and submit a discursive paragraph in a formal register that uses the subjunctive; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound C1 (Linking Words)

A formal argument still needs to flow. At C1 you gain a sharper, more formal set of connectors — whereas, however, consequently, by contrast, nonetheless and therefore — which let you concede, contrast and conclude without losing your formal tone. Used precisely, they hold a subjunctive-rich argument together.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
howeverqualify or contrast the previous sentenceThe rule is clear. However, it is rarely enforced.
by contrastset one case sharply against anotherSeniors follow the code; juniors, by contrast, ignore it.
consequentlystate a formal result or effectThe vote was tied; consequently, I move that it be retaken.
whereasdraw a direct, balanced contrastThe proposal is bold, whereas the budget is modest.
nonethelessconcede a point, then hold your positionThe change is small; nonetheless, it must be recorded.
thereforedraw a firm conclusionThe evidence is plain; therefore, I demand that action be taken.

See the difference — the same demands, written two ways:

Informal indicative ✗Formal, subjunctive register ✓
I think the captain should drop him. It's really important everyone comes on time, and people need to stop using phones. If I was in charge, I'd change the rules whatever happens. I move that the captain be replaced; however, I ask that he first be heard. It is imperative that every member arrive on time, whereas at present many do not. Consequently, I propose that phones not be used during practice; and if I were in charge, the new rules would apply to all, come what may.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different C1 connectors from the table. Weave your argument; don't list claims.
  • However, Consequently, Nonetheless, Therefore usually open a sentence and take a comma: "Consequently, I propose that the rule be revised."

How to structure a formal argument (Argument Structure)

A strong C1 argument is not a run of blunt orders. It states a proposal, gives a reason, concedes a counter-point, and lands a firm recommendation. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the shape:

PartWhat to writeLanguage to reach for
1. Proposalstate your position as a demandI propose that… be / it is essential that… be
2. Supportgive a reason, contrasting the presentthis requires that… / whereas at present…
3. Concessionadmit a counter-point, then press onbe that as it may / however / I concede that
4. Conclusioncommit to a firm recommendationtherefore I move that… be / come what may

Sample Answer (~210 words) I propose that every school introduce a single, clearly stated code of conduct, and that this code be displayed in every classroom. Whereas at present the rules are scattered and often archaic, one plain decree would make expectations clear. It is imperative that each pupil be told the rules on the first day; consequently, I also recommend that teachers not enforce any penalty that has not been announced in advance.

Be that as it may, a code is worthless unless it is applied consistently. I therefore move that a small committee be formed to review each contingency as it arises, and I stipulate that its decisions be recorded in writing. Some may object that such formality is prescriptive; however, a fair system must be predictable, come what may.

If I were designing the code myself, I would keep it short. A long, formulaic document invites the very confusion it is meant to prevent, whereas a handful of solemn, memorable rules can be invoked by anyone. Nonetheless, I concede that no code can foresee everything.

Suffice it to say, I wish the present arrangement were clearer; a single, well-drafted set of rules would, I am convinced, serve everyone far better.
