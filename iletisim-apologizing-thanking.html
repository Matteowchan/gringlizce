In English, apologizing and thanking are not grammatical tenses, but rather communicative functions: there are many phrases that convey the same thought, and choosing the correct one depends more on the level of formality, the relationship, and the seriousness of the situation than on the word itself. In Turkish, a single "I'm sorry" or "thank you" corresponds to a range in English: there is a huge difference between Sorry and I do apologize, as well as between Thanks and I really appreciate it. This section explains both the phrases and when to use them, the ways to respond, and the traps that Turkish students often fall into with elite detail. For related functions, see Requests & Offers and Everyday Expressions.

Türkçedeki benzeri

Türkçede "özür dilerim / pardon / kusura bakma" ve "teşekkürler / sağ ol / eyvallah / minnettarım" gibi kelimelerimiz vardır ve bunların da resmiyet düzeyleri farklıdır ("kusura bakma" gündelik, "özür dilerim" nötr, "özür dilerim efendim" resmi). İngilizce de tam olarak böyle çalışır. Dolayısıyla mantık sana tanıdık gelecek; öğrenmen gereken şey hangi İngilizce kalıbın Türkçedeki hangi tona denk düştüğüdür.

Neden zor?

  • Türkçedeki tek bir kelime ("teşekkürler"), İngilizcede birden çok kalıba karşılık gelir ve yanlış register seçmek garip durur (arkadaşına "I am most grateful" demek gibi).
  • Özre karşılık ile teşekküre karşılık farklıdır: Türkçede ikisine de "rica ederim" diyebiliriz ama İngilizcede özre "You're welcome" demek YANLIŞTIR.
  • Edat (preposition) seçimi tuzaklıdır: sorry for + -ing, sorry about + isim, thank you for + -ing/isim. Türkçe düşünen öğrenci "sorry to being late" gibi hatalar yapar.
  • "Sorry" hem gerçek pişmanlık hem de nazik geçiştirme ("Sorry, we're closed") anlamına gelebilir; ton her şeyi değiştirir.

This topic is not a grammatical tense, but a communicative function. That is, "apologizing" and "thanking" are a set of phrases we use to achieve a specific purpose in language, just like requests and offers. The important thing is not to memorize a single "correct sentence", but to recognize a range that serves the same purpose and to know which one to choose in which situation.

The glue of human relationships is these two functions. The thing that repairs the relationship when you make a mistake is an apology; the thing that nourishes the relationship when someone does you a favor is a thank you. In cultures where English is spoken, these two phrases are used much more frequently and much more automatically than in Turkish: the British reflexively say "Sorry" even with the slightest contact, and Americans say "Thank you so much" for the smallest favor. Using these sparingly can lead to being perceived as cold, rude, or ungrateful.

Similar in Turkish

In Turkish, we have words like "I'm sorry / pardon / excuse me" and "thank you / thanks / I appreciate it" and they also have different levels of formality ("excuse me" is casual, "I'm sorry" is neutral, "I'm sorry sir" is formal). English works exactly like this. Therefore, the logic will feel familiar to you; what you need to learn is which English phrase corresponds to which tone in Turkish.

Why is it difficult?

  • A single word in Turkish ("thank you") corresponds to multiple phrases in English, and choosing the wrong register feels awkward (like saying "I am most grateful" to a friend).
  • Responding to an apology is different from responding to a thank you: in Turkish, we can say "You're welcome" for both, but in English, saying "You're welcome" to an apology is WRONG.
  • Choosing prepositions is tricky: sorry for + -ing, sorry about + noun, thank you for + -ing/noun. A student thinking in Turkish might make mistakes like "sorry to being late".
  • "Sorry" can mean both genuine regret and polite dismissal ("Sorry, we're closed"); tone changes everything.
  • Özür = sorry / apologize; Teşekkür = thank you / thanks / appreciate. Bunlar zaman değil, işlevdir.
  • Özürde gerekçe eklemek için: Sorry + cümle ("Sorry I'm late") ya da Sorry for + V-ing ("Sorry for being late").
  • Teşekkürde sebep eklemek için: Thank you for + V-ing/isim ("Thanks for coming", "Thanks for the coffee").
  • Resmiyet arttıkça kalıp uzar: Sorry → I'm sorry → I apologize → Please accept my apologies.
  • Teşekkürde de: Thanks → Thank you → Thank you so much → I really appreciate it → I'm most grateful.
  • Özre karşılık: No worries / It's fine / Don't worry about it (asla "You're welcome" değil).

Since this is a function, it is more useful to see how the phrases are constructed and how they are expanded instead of just using "positive/negative/question". There are two basic structures: apology structure and thank you structure. Both start from a short core and expand with justification.

Structure of the Apology

Formula 1: (I'm) sorry + (independent clause) — "Sorry I'm late." Here, "that" is omitted, and a direct sentence is added. Formula 2: Sorry / I apologize + for + V-ing — "Sorry for being late." Formula 3: Sorry + about + noun — "Sorry about the mess." for usually follows an action (verb), about usually follows a situation/object (noun).

  1. Start with the core: (I'm) sorry.
  2. Add a direct sentence: Sorry I'm late.
  3. Or connect the justification with a preposition: for + verb-ing, about + noun.
  4. If formality is needed, extend: I apologize for the confusion.

The following questions do not check the rule, but rather check meaning and function. Consider whether the sentence is an apology or a thank you, whether it is sincere or dismissive.

concept check For each sentence: is this a real regret/gratitude, or a polite phrase/dismissal? Briefly explain.
  1. "I'm sorry, but the store is closed." — Is this real regret?
    No. Here, "sorry" is a polite buffer softening the bad news; the seller is not regretful about the store being closed.
  2. "I can't thank you enough." — Is this a neutral thank you or strong gratitude?
    Very strong, emotional gratitude. It means "no matter how much I thank you, it's not enough"; it is much more intense than "thanks".
  3. Someone said "Sorry!", is it correct for you to say "You're welcome"?
    No. You do not say "You're welcome" to an apology; you say "No worries / It's fine / Don't worry about it". "You're welcome" is only given in response to thanks.
  4. "I'm sorry that you feel that way." — Is the speaker taking the blame?
    No, not exactly. This phrase expresses sadness for the other person's feelings but does not clearly take the blame; it can sometimes be perceived as a defensive apology.
  5. Is "Thank God!" a thank you to a person?
    No. "Thank God" is a fixed expression, meaning "thank goodness / I'm glad"; it is not a thank you to a specific person.

Where to start?
Start with two reflex phrases: Sorry and Thank you. Students already know these; the main gain is the response system. The most effective single lesson goal: to make the distinction between "For apologies, No worries, for thanks, You're welcome" permanent. This single distinction solves the most common mistake in the classroom.

Concept check questions (for teachers): Write "Sorry!" on the board and ask: "What is the response? Is it You're welcome?" (No — No worries). Then write "Thank you!": "Is You're welcome correct now?" (Yes). This contrast distinction will stick. Then show the pair "for come / for coming" and have everyone repeat "after the preposition -ING".

Activity suggestions: (1) Role-play cards: write a situation on each card (spilled coffee / received a gift / arrived late), students should act out the appropriate phrase + response. (2) Register ordering: have students arrange the cards "My bad → Sorry → I'm sorry → I apologize → Please accept my apologies" from informal to formal. (3) Email writing: have students write the same apology to both a friend and a boss, discuss the difference.

Common classroom error
Students produce a hybrid of "Thank you" and "Thanks" as "Thanks you". Catch this early: it's either Thank you (verb + pronoun) or Thanks (noun) — there is no in-between. It's a small but lasting correction.

  • Apologies and thanks are both communication functions; the correct phrase is chosen according to the register, relationship, and the seriousness of the situation.
  • Apology ladder: My bad → Sorry → I'm sorry → I apologize → Please accept my apologies (from informal to formal).
  • Thank you ladder: Thanks → Thank you → Thank you so much → I really appreciate it → I'm most grateful.
  • For justification: for + V-ing (for coming), about + noun (about the noise). After a preposition, the verb is ALWAYS -ing.
  • appreciate requires an object (appreciate it); grateful is an adjective and is used with the verb to be (I'm grateful).
  • Responses to apologies: No worries / Don't worry about it / It's fine. Responses to thanks: You're welcome / No problem / My pleasure — do not mix the two.
  • "Sorry" is not always regret; it can soften bad news or (with a rising tone) mean "Excuse me?".
