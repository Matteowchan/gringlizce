Grammar: Modal Deduction in the Past

Use modal verbs + have + V3 to work out what probably, certainly or impossibly happened.

One structure, endless conclusions

In Unit 6 you learned to make deductions about the present: She must be tired. Now you will do the same for the past. When you have no direct proof but you can read the clues, English lets you say how certain you are with a single, powerful structure: a modal verb + have + past participle (V3).

This is the language of detectives, scientists and confident speakers. Instead of "Maybe he forgot" you can say "He must have forgotten" (I'm sure), "He can't have forgotten" (that's impossible) or "He might have forgotten" (perhaps). One small change of modal, and your whole level of certainty changes with it.

  • She must have left already — her coat is gone.I'm almost certain it happened → must have + V3.
  • He can't have known the answer; he only guessed.I'm certain it was impossible → can't have + V3.
  • They might have missed the train.Perhaps it happened → might / may / could have + V3.
  • You could have been hurt in that bike crash!It was possible, but luckily did not happen → could have + V3.

1. The form at a glance (quick review)

The pattern never changes: pick your modal, then always add have and the past participle (V3). The modal shows how sure you are; the have + V3 shows that you are talking about the past.

FunctionStructureExample
Certain — it happenedmust + have + V3She must have left early.
Certain — it did NOT happencan't / couldn't + have + V3He can't have known the truth.
Possible — perhapsmight / may / could + have + V3They might have missed the bus.
Continuous ideamodal + have been + V-ingYou must have been sleeping.
Key point
  • The modal is never followed by a past tense verb: it is always have + V3. Say "must have gone" ✓ — never "must have went" ✗ or "must went" ✗.
  • In natural speech, have is reduced to /əv/ (must've, might've). Never write it as "must of" — that is a spelling mistake, not a form.

2. Degrees of certainty (how sure are you?)

Think of a line from 100% yes to 100% no. The modal you choose places your idea somewhere on that line. This is the whole point of deduction: not the fact, but how confident you are about it.

CertaintyModalExample
~100% — I'm sure it happenedmust haveThe road is wet; it must have rained.
~50% — perhaps it happenedmight / may / could haveShe's late; she may have overslept.
~0% — I'm sure it did NOT happencan't / couldn't haveHe can't have finished so quickly.
Golden rule
  • For a certain positive conclusion, use must have. For a certain negative one, the opposite is can't have / couldn't have — not "mustn't have".
  • might / may / could have are interchangeable for a simple past possibility: "She might/may/could have forgotten."

3. must have vs can't have (certainty, both ways)

These two are a pair: must have for what you are sure did happen, can't have for what you are sure did not. You read the same evidence, but it points in opposite directions.

  • The cake is gone. The children must have eaten it.Strong positive deduction from evidence.
  • The children can't have eaten it — they were at school all day.Strong negative deduction: impossible.
  • She passed with full marks; she must have studied hard.must have = I'm certain it is true.
  • He can't have cycled here; his bike has a flat tyre.can't have = I'm certain it is impossible.
B2 subtlety — the negative of "must have"
  • To say something was impossible in the past, use can't have or couldn't have, never "mustn't have": "They can't have seen us." ✓ — "They mustn't have seen us." ✗
  • can't have and couldn't have mean the same thing here; both are correct for past impossibility.

4. could have & might have (possibility & missed chances)

could have + V3 does two useful jobs. First, like might/may have, it shows a past possibility. Second — and this is very B2 — it describes a missed opportunity or a near miss: something that was possible but did not actually happen.

MeaningExampleIdea
Past possibility (= might have)She could have taken the earlier train.Perhaps she did.
Missed opportunityWe could have won, but we played badly.It was possible; it did not happen.
Near miss / lucky escapeYou could have hurt yourself!It nearly happened; luckily it did not.
Watch out
  • For a simple past possibility, could have = might have = may have. But for a missed chance, only could have carries the feeling of "it was possible, yet it did not happen".
  • Do not confuse it with the present: "I can't have been asleep" (past deduction) is different from "I can't be asleep" (present).

5. Present vs Past deduction (must be vs must have been)

The only difference is when the situation is true. Add have to move a present deduction into the past. Compare each pair carefully — the certainty is the same, only the time changes.

IdeaPresent (Unit 6)Past (Unit 7)
CertainShe must be tired.She must have been tired.
ImpossibleHe can't be at home.He can't have been at home.
PossibleThey might be lost.They might have been lost.
B2 subtlety — stative verbs
  • Stative verbs (know, be, believe, understand) avoid the continuous, even here: "You can't have known." ✓ — "You can't have been knowing." ✗
  • Use the continuous deduction (must have been V-ing) only for an action in progress: "He was covered in paint; he must have been decorating."

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B2 students still make with past deduction. Check your own sentences against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
She must have went home.She must have gone home.use the participle (V3 = gone), not the past (went).
He must left already.He must have left already.the structure needs have: must + have + V3.
They mustn't have seen us.They can't have seen us.for past impossibility use "can't have", not "mustn't have".
You can't have been knowing that.You can't have known that."know" is stative → no continuous form.
It must have rain last night.It must have rained last night.after "have" you need the participle (rained).

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two friends wonder why Dan never came to their group study session. Read it twice and watch the certainty shift: must have for a sure conclusion, can't have for an impossible one, and might/could have for a guess.

A:Dan never showed up. He must have forgotten our study session.A sure positive conclusion → must have + V3.
B:He can't have forgotten — I reminded him twice at school today.A sure negative conclusion → can't have + V3.
A:Then he might have missed the bus.A past possibility → might have + V3.
B:Possibly. Or he could have stayed behind for football practice again.Another possibility → could have + V3.
A:Well, he can't have been ill — I saw him laughing in the canteen at lunch.Impossible, based on evidence → can't have been.
B:True. He must have simply changed his mind, then.Back to a sure conclusion → must have + V3.
Test yourself
  • Why does B say "can't have forgotten" and not "mustn't have forgotten"? (Past impossibility always uses "can't have".)
  • Which two lines express only a possibility, not certainty? ("might have missed the bus", "could have stayed behind for practice".)
  • Rewrite "He must have forgotten" as a present deduction. (He must be forgetful. / He must forget things easily.)

Speaking: Mysteries, Clues & Conclusions

Learn the words, then use them to explain what probably happened.

clue (n)
a piece of information that helps solve a mystery (ipucu)
Syn: hint · Ant: —
"The muddy shoes were the first clue we found."
evidence (n)
facts or signs that show something is true (kanıt, delil)
Syn: proof · Ant: —
"The evidence suggests he must have left in a hurry."
suspect (n)
a person thought to be guilty of something (şüpheli, zanlı)
Syn: culprit · Ant: —
"The main suspect can't have been at the scene."
witness (n)
someone who saw an event happen (tanık, görgü tanığı)
Syn: observer · Ant: —
"A witness says the car might have turned left."
trace (n)
a small sign that something was there (iz, eser)
Syn: mark · Ant: —
"There was no trace of a forced entry."
vanish (v)
to disappear suddenly and completely (aniden yok olmak)
Syn: disappear · Ant: appear
"The money had simply vanished overnight."
deduce (v)
to reach a conclusion from the evidence (çıkarım yapmak)
Syn: infer · Ant: —
"From the clues, we deduced that it must have been an inside job."
assume (v)
to believe something without real proof (varsaymak)
Syn: presume · Ant: verify
"Don't assume he lied; he may have been confused."
conclude (v)
to decide something after thinking carefully (sonuca varmak)
Syn: determine · Ant: —
"The police concluded that no one could have escaped."
alibi (n)
proof you were elsewhere when a crime happened (olay anında başka yerde olma kanıtı)
Syn: defence · Ant: —
"Her alibi proves she can't have done it."
coincidence (n)
when things happen together by chance (tesadüf)
Syn: chance · Ant: plan
"It might have been just a coincidence."
motive (n)
a reason for doing something (saik, güdü)
Syn: reason · Ant: —
"He had no motive, so he can't have planned it."
reveal (v)
to make something known or visible (açığa çıkarmak)
Syn: disclose · Ant: conceal
"The camera revealed what must have happened."
suspicious (adj)
making you feel something is wrong (şüpheli, kuşku uyandıran)
Syn: doubtful · Ant: innocent
"His story sounded suspicious from the start."

Grammar requirement — apply this unit's topic
  • Use must have + V3 for a conclusion you are sure of (The thief must have used a key).

Reading: The Locked Study

Read the mystery, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think of a time something disappeared and you had to work out what happened. What did you conclude?
  • The text is a detective story. Which past modals (must have, can't have, might have) do you expect to see?
  • The title is "The Locked Study". If the door was locked from the inside, how could a thief get in? Guess!
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Follow the mystery; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text. Notice each deduction — a "must have", a "can't have", a "might have" — and ask what evidence led to it.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): clue · evidence · suspect · witness · trace · vanish · deduce · suspicious

A locked study, an open window, a rope — and a missing necklace.
The Locked Study

When Inspector Hale arrived at the old house, the study door was still locked from the inside, yet the famous diamond necklace had vanished from the safe. The owner, Mr Vance, insisted that nobody could have entered the room. Nevertheless, Hale was certain that someone must have taken it, because jewels do not simply disappear on their own.

The theft had happened during a busy family weekend. Mr Vance had invited his sister, her three teenage children and two of their classmates, so the old house had been crowded and noisy until well past midnight. Because so many young people had been drifting between the games room, the kitchen and the garden, Hale realised that almost anyone could have slipped upstairs without being noticed. Nevertheless, most of the teenagers had spent the evening filming a dance video for social media in the front hall, and the time stamps on their phones proved it. They can't have been anywhere near the study when the necklace vanished; therefore, the inspector crossed them off his list one by one.

Hale examined the scene carefully. The window was wide open, although it was a freezing winter night. Therefore, he reasoned, the thief must have climbed in from the garden. However, there were no ladders and no footprints in the flowerbed below. The criminal couldn't have jumped that high, and he can't have flown, so how had he reached the second floor?

Back inside the study, the inspector studied the heavy old safe. It had not been forced or scratched, so whoever had opened it must have known the four-digit code. Only someone close to the family could have learnt those numbers, which meant a passing stranger can't have been responsible. A younger cousin named Leo had boasted at dinner that he could crack any lock in the world; yet his story fell apart at once, since he had spent the entire evening losing noisily at video games in front of six laughing witnesses. He might have wanted the diamonds, Hale admitted, but he certainly couldn't have taken them. Another cousin insisted the family cat must have knocked the necklace somewhere, although even a clever cat can't open a locked safe.

That evening, the teenagers had actually turned the mystery into a noisy debate of their own. One of them argued that a professional burglar must have broken in from the roof, whereas another insisted the necklace could simply have been mislaid. A third pointed out that no window downstairs was broken, so an outsider can't have got in at all. Hale listened quietly from the doorway. Their guesses were wild, yet one comment stayed with him: 'Whoever did it must have been able to climb,' a girl said, 'because there's no other way up to that balcony.' She might not have realised it, but she had just described the thief perfectly.

Then the inspector noticed a thin rope mark on the balcony rail. Someone must have used a rope, he thought; moreover, that person must have been both strong and light. Whereas an ordinary burglar would have struggled, this thief had moved like an acrobat.

The rope mark told its own quiet story. An untrained climber would have slipped on the icy rail; whereas a trained one could have moved up it silently and quickly. Moreover, the marks were smooth and even, which suggested calm confidence rather than panic. Hale measured the distance from the balcony to the frozen ground below and shook his head. An ordinary teenager can't have made that climb, however brave or determined. Whoever had done it must have practised the movement hundreds of times before, until it felt as natural as walking to the bus stop.

Hale remembered a useful piece of evidence: Mr Vance's young nephew had once worked in a circus. The nephew had claimed to be asleep upstairs, but he might have lied. Since the rope was still slightly warm from friction, the theft could only have happened minutes earlier.

The nephew, Daniel, was only sixteen, but he had grown up travelling with a small circus before his family finally settled down in the town. At his new school he still trained with the gymnastics club every single afternoon, and only the week before he had proudly posted a video of himself climbing a rope to the gym ceiling in under ten seconds. 'You must be rather proud of a skill like that,' Hale said gently. Daniel shrugged and stared at the floor. Since the boy clearly had the ability, the knowledge of the house and no proper alibi, the inspector was now almost certain. He thought to himself that he couldn't have invented a more obvious suspect even if he had tried.

'You can't have slept through this,' Hale told the nephew quietly. 'You must have known exactly where the safe was.' The young man turned pale. Although he said nothing, his face had already confessed.

Later that night, the missing necklace was found hidden inside a hollow gym trophy in Daniel's room, exactly where a clever teenager might have assumed no adult would ever bother to look. He never actually said the words 'I took it'; nevertheless, everyone standing in the room understood the truth. Mr Vance, although still furious, agreed not to call the police as long as the boy returned everything and promised to talk honestly with a counsellor at school. As Hale finally packed away his notebook, he allowed himself a small smile. The locked door had seemed impossible at first, yet in the end the answer had been hanging quietly in the freezing air, on one thin, still-warm rope, all along.

Writing: Explaining a Mystery

Write and submit your account; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound B2 (Linking Words)

At B1 you linked ideas with and, but, so, because. At B2 you sound noticeably more advanced when you use formal, precise connectors. Four of them are essential this unit — and two familiar ones, although and however, become sharper tools.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
moreoveradd a stronger, extra pointThe door was locked. Moreover, the window was shut.
thereforeshow a logical resultThere were no keys; therefore, someone must have climbed in.
whereascontrast two ideas directlyI suspected the neighbour, whereas my brother blamed a stranger.
neverthelesssay "in spite of that"Nothing was missing. Nevertheless, it felt suspicious.
althoughconcede a point inside one sentenceAlthough the door was locked, the necklace had vanished.
howevercontrast between two sentencesHe denied it. However, the evidence proved otherwise.

See the difference — the same facts, written two ways:

Basic linking ✗Confident B2 writing ✓
The money was gone. The door was locked. Nobody came in. I thought about it. I found the answer. The money had vanished, although the door was still locked. Therefore, I studied every clue. Whereas the others assumed a thief, I concluded that it must have been an inside job; moreover, the evidence proved me right.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different B2 connectors from the table. Do not write sentence by sentence; connect your ideas.
  • Moreover, Therefore, However, Nevertheless usually start a sentence and take a comma: "However, the evidence proved otherwise."

Paragraph structure (Paragraph Structure)

A "mystery" paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningSet the scene: what strange thing happenedOne morning we found the back door wide open.
2. BodyPresent the clues and your deductions. Use must have / can't have / might have + V3 to say how sure you are. Link with B2 connectors.Nothing valuable had vanished; therefore, a thief can't have been inside.
3. ClosingReveal or reflect on what you finally concludedIn the end, it must have been the neighbour's dog.

Sample Answer (~190 words) Last summer, something strange happened at my grandmother's house, and I have never forgotten it. One morning, we woke up and found the back door wide open, although we had definitely locked it the night before. At first, my brother assumed that a thief must have broken in; nevertheless, nothing valuable had vanished.

I decided to look for clues myself. There were muddy paw prints on the kitchen floor, therefore I concluded that an animal must have pushed the door open. The old lock was loose, so a burglar can't have been necessary at all. Whereas my brother still suspected a stranger, I was certain the evidence pointed to our neighbour's dog.

Later that day, the neighbour apologised: her dog had escaped during the night. My deduction had been correct. Moreover, I realised something important — you should never assume the worst without real evidence.

Since then, I have become much more careful about jumping to conclusions. I have not solved any real crime yet, but that morning taught me to think like a detective, one clue at a time.
