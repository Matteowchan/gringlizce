Grammar: Ellipsis & Substitution

Say it once. Let grammar carry the rest — and your English will sound native, fluent and cohesive.

The grammar of leaving things out

English hates repetition. Where a beginner writes every word out in full, a fluent speaker deletes what the listener can already supply, or replaces a long phrase with a short stand-in. These two moves — ellipsis (leaving words out) and substitution (swapping in a shorter form) — are what make advanced English sound light, quick and connected instead of clumsy and mechanical.

They are also invisible until you learn to see them. "I like coffee and my sister likes coffee too" is correct, but no native speaker would say it; they say "I like coffee and so does my sister." This unit teaches the whole toolkit: so / neither agreement, do so / do it / do that, one / ones, auxiliary and to-infinitive ellipsis, gapping, and the little words so and not that replace a whole clause. Master them and your writing gains cohesion — the quality examiners reward most at C1.

  • 'I loved the film.' 'So did I.'so + inversion → short agreement replacing "I loved the film too."
  • 'I haven't finished.' 'Neither have I.'neither + inversion → negative agreement, no repetition.
  • These shoes are worn; I need new ones.one/ones → substitute for a countable noun already mentioned.
  • 'Will you help?' 'I'd love to.'to-infinitive ellipsis → the verb is deleted, "to" is kept.

1. so / neither / nor (short agreement + inversion)

To say you feel the same, you don't repeat the whole clause — you use so (for positives) or neither / nor (for negatives), followed by an inverted auxiliary that matches the first verb's tense and form. The word order is so/neither + auxiliary + subject. Getting that inversion right is the single most common slip at this level.

First speakerAgreementWhy this auxiliary?
'I love jazz.''So do I.'present simple → do
'She enjoyed it.''So did he.'past simple → did
'I can't swim.''Neither can I.'modal, negative → neither + can
'We haven't eaten.''Nor have we.'present perfect → nor + have
Register & nuance
  • Beware the inversion: it is "So do I", never "So I do". "So I do" exists, but it means "yes, indeed I do" — a confirmation, not agreement.
  • neither and nor are interchangeable in agreement; nor is a touch more formal. Both take the positive auxiliary (the negative is already inside neither/nor): "Neither can I." ✓ — not "Neither can't I." ✗

2. do so / do it / do that (verb-phrase substitution)

Instead of repeating a whole verb phrase, English substitutes a form of do plus so / it / that. The three are not identical: do so is formal and refers back to the same action described; do it is neutral and everyday; do that often points to a specific, sometimes surprising, action. Choosing the right one is a matter of register.

FormRegister / useExample
do soformal; repeats the exact actionMembers must sign the sheet; those who fail to do so miss the trip.
do itneutral, spoken; a known task'Can you fix the printer?' 'I'll do it after lunch.'
do thatpoints to a specific/marked actionHe apologised in public. I'd never do that.
C1 subtlety — register
  • In an essay or report, prefer do so: "Readers who wish to comment may do so below."
  • do so normally replaces a dynamic action verb, not a stative one: "She owns two bikes." — "So does her brother." ✓ but not "her brother does so." ✗ (use so does for states).

3. one / ones (noun substitution)

To avoid repeating a countable noun, replace it with one (singular) or ones (plural). It usually carries a describing word — an adjective, this/that, or which. Note that one/ones substitutes only countable nouns; uncountable nouns take some or nothing at all.

Full formWith substitutionNote
I prefer the red dress.I prefer the red one.singular countable → one
The old chairs broke; we bought new chairs....we bought new ones.plural countable → ones
'Which cup?' 'The blue cup.''The blue one.'after an adjective
I need advice. (uncountable)I need some. (not "an advice / a one")uncountable → some / ∅
Common error
  • Plural must be ones: "the clean ones" ✓ — not "the clean ones... the clean one" ✗ when you mean more than one.
  • Don't use one for uncountables: "I'll have a coffee." "I'll have one too." ✓ (a cup, countable) but "I need information — do you have any?" not "a one."

4. Auxiliary & to-infinitive ellipsis (I can't, but she can · I'd love to)

When the main verb is obvious from context, English deletes it and keeps only the auxiliary or "to". This is the backbone of natural short answers and contrasts. Keep the auxiliary that carries the tense and polarity; drop everything after it.

FullElliptical (natural)What is kept
I can't drive, but she can drive.I can't, but she can.modal auxiliary kept, verb dropped
He said he would come, and he did come....and he did.did carries tense
'Will you join us?' 'I'd love to join you.''I'd love to.'to-infinitive ellipsis: keep "to"
You don't have to stay if you don't want to stay....if you don't want to.keep "to", drop the verb
C1 subtlety
  • Keep the "to" in infinitive ellipsis; deleting it sounds wrong: "I'd love to." ✓ — not "I'd love." ✗
  • The auxiliary must match: "She has finished, but I haven't." ✓ — not "...but I don't." ✗

5. Gapping & that-clause substitution (others, coffee · I think so / I'm afraid not)

Two more advanced moves. Gapping deletes a repeated verb from the second half of a balanced sentence, leaving just the new information: "Some ordered tea; others [ordered] coffee." And after verbs like think, hope, believe, suppose, expect, be afraid, English replaces a whole that-clause with so (positive) or not (negative).

StructureHow it worksExample
gappingdelete the repeated verb in clause 2My sister ordered the salmon; I, the steak.
so-substitutionso replaces a positive that-clause'Is it open?' 'I think so.' (= I think that it is open)
not-substitutionnot replaces a negative that-clause'Will we be late?' 'I hope not.' (= I hope that we won't be)
C1 subtlety — so vs not
  • Some verbs allow both patterns for the negative: "I don't think so" and "I think not" (more formal) are both fine; hope and be afraid prefer not: "I'm afraid not."
  • Don't say "I hope so not" or "I think it" ✗. The clause becomes exactly one word: so or not.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors that survive into otherwise strong C1 writing. Each is a cohesion slip — a failure of ellipsis or substitution. Audit your own work against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
'I like tea.' 'So I do.''I like tea.' 'So do I.'agreement needs inversion: so + aux + subject.
'I can't cook.' 'Neither can't I.''I can't cook.' 'Neither can I.'neither is already negative; use the positive auxiliary.
These apples are bad; give me the fresh one....give me the fresh ones.a plural noun needs ones, not one.
'Will you help?' 'Yes, I'd love.''Yes, I'd love to.'keep "to" in infinitive ellipsis.
'Is she coming?' 'I hope so not.''I hope not.'a negative that-clause is replaced by "not" alone.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two friends compare weekends. Read it twice and watch the devices work as a system: so/neither agreement, one/ones substitution, do so, auxiliary and to-ellipsis, and so/not for a whole clause. Notice how little is repeated — and how natural it sounds.

A:I finally saw the exhibition on Saturday.The action stated in full.
B:So did I! Which room did you like — the modern one or the classical?so + inversion agreement; one substitutes "room".
A:The modern, definitely. The classical rooms were closed, so I couldn't visit them.gapping ("The modern [one]") + full clause.
B:Oh no — I hadn't heard that. Will they reopen soon?natural response.
A:I hope so. Visitors who want a refund can apparently do so online.so replaces the whole clause; do so = "request a refund".
B:Well, I won't bother, but you probably should. I'd rather see it again, honestly — I'd love to.auxiliary ellipsis ("should [bother]") + to-ellipsis.
Test yourself
  • Why "So did I" and not "So I did"? ("So did I" = agreement; "So I did" would mean "yes, indeed I did".)
  • What does "one" stand for in "the modern one"? (It substitutes the countable noun "room".)
  • Rewrite "I'd love to" in full. What was deleted? ("I'd love to see it again" — the verb phrase is dropped, "to" is kept.)

Reading: The Art of Leaving Things Out

Read the feature, then work through the five tasks.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Skilled readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think of a text message you sent today. How many words did you delete because the reader already knew them?
  • The text argues that "saying less can mean more". Do you agree — or can leaving things out ever cause confusion?
  • Watch for the moment the writer shows a long sentence rewritten as a short one. What exactly was cut, and what was substituted?
How to read
  • First read: read quickly for the whole argument — why concision matters and where it began. Don't stop for every word.
  • Second read: hunt for the devices in action — an agreement with so ("so does my sister"), a noun substitute ("one"), a clause replaced by so ("I think so"), and a gapped sentence. Ask what each one saves.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): concise · terse · verbose · redundant · coherent · succinct · omit

An editor deletes what the reader can supply and substitutes short forms for long ones — the meaning survives; the clutter does not.
The Art of Leaving Things Out

For as long as people have written, the finest stylists have known a paradox: saying less can mean more. Where a novice writes "I like coffee and my sister likes coffee too," an editor writes "I like coffee and so does my sister." The meaning is identical; the second version, however, is more concise, and somehow more human.

What looks like mere thrift is, on closer inspection, a form of respect. A sentence that says everything twice treats its reader as inattentive; a sentence that leaves a little unspoken pays the reader the compliment of trusting them to keep up. Much of the pleasure we take in a well-turned line comes from precisely this collaboration — from the sense that the writer and we are finishing the thought together. Concision, then, is not the enemy of warmth but is frequently its source, for nothing feels more mechanical than a passage that laboriously spells out what we had already grasped.

This economy has a long history. In the age of the telegram, every word cost money, so senders stripped their messages to the bone. Whereas a rambling letter might fill a page, a terse telegram carried the same news in ten words. Moreover, the discipline it imposed taught a generation of journalists to cut the verbose and the redundant without mercy.

Long before the wires hummed, however, orators and essayists prized the very same restraint. Classical rhetoric gave the habit a name, brevitas, and treated the ability to compress an argument as a mark of mastery rather than of haste. Centuries later, the great style guides distilled the lesson into a slogan: omit needless words. The advice sounds disarmingly simple, yet it conceals a demanding question, since the writer must decide which words are truly needless and which merely appear so. A phrase that strikes one reader as decorative may carry, for another, the exact nuance that keeps a sentence honest.

Editors have understood this better than most. The old image of the blue pencil, drawn firmly through a swollen paragraph, captures a truth about all durable prose: much of what we admire in a finished text is what an unseen hand quietly removed. A skilled editor does not so much add sparkle as clear away the dross that dimmed it, deleting the phrase the reader could readily supply and shortening the clause that had plainly overstayed its welcome. The result reads as though it had always been that lean, though it rarely began that way.

Two quiet devices do most of the work. Ellipsis simply omits the words that context makes obvious: "Some praised the plan; others, the caution behind it." Substitution swaps a short form for a longer phrase — "one" stands in for a noun, "do so" for a whole verb phrase, and "so" for an entire clause, as in "I think so." Since both devices lean on shared knowledge, they knit sentences together; consequently, a paragraph reads as one coherent thought rather than a list of separate claims.

What makes such devices possible at all is a quiet architecture beneath the surface of the language. Pronouns point backwards to something already named; auxiliaries stand in for verbs we have only just used; a single "so" can gather up a whole proposition and carry it forward. Linguists call this web of connections cohesion, and it is what allows a paragraph to behave as a single fabric rather than as a loose heap of threads. When a writer omits or substitutes well, the reader's mind reaches automatically for the antecedent, and the gap closes so smoothly that we scarcely register that it was ever there.

Speech, unsurprisingly, leans on these shortcuts far more heavily than the printed page. In conversation we abandon whole halves of sentences, confident that tone, gesture and the moment itself will supply the rest. Writing enjoys no such luxury: the reader cannot ask us what we meant, and the page cannot raise an eyebrow. This is why the written economy that seems so effortless is so often the fruit of labour, of successive drafts in which the redundant is pruned only once the meaning has been made secure. The finished ease is, in truth, a performance, and the effort behind it is deliberately hidden from view.

There is, moreover, a genuine cognitive pleasure in the small work these gaps require. Researchers who study reading observe that a mind given a little to do stays more alert than one spoon-fed every particular; the tiny inference needed to expand an ellipsis keeps the reader leaning in. A text that withholds nothing risks a curious flatness, for it never lets us feel the quiet satisfaction of completing a pattern for ourselves. Well-judged omission, in this light, is less an absence than an invitation, and a reader who has been trusted repays that trust with attention.

There are limits. Notwithstanding its elegance, ellipsis can mislead when the missing word is ambiguous, and substitution can sound cold: a report may say "do so," yet a friend would say "do it." Handled clumsily, the very economy that should clarify will instead confuse, and a succinct line becomes a puzzle.

The danger, in the end, is always this drift into ambiguity. "She told her sister she was wrong" leaves us guessing who exactly erred, and no amount of elegance can repair a sentence the reader is forced to decode twice. Accomplished writers therefore treat omission as a series of small wagers: each cut is a bet that the context will hold, and the best of them know precisely when the odds have turned against them. To leave a word out is easy; to leave out only the right words is very nearly the whole of the craft.

It is tempting to imagine that our messages, chats and captions have invented this compression, yet they have merely inherited it. The medium is new; the instinct is ancient. When a friend answers an invitation with a bare "so would I," or a headline drops every article it can spare, we are watching the same forces that once shaped the telegram and the aphorism at work within a smaller frame. What changes across the centuries is the technology; what endures is the human preference for the line that says just enough and then, wisely, stops.

Nonetheless, the principle endures. The writers we reread are seldom the ones who said the most; they are the ones who trusted us to supply what they left unsaid. To omit a word, well, is to invite the reader in.

Writing: A Cohesive Opinion Essay

Write and submit a tightly linked discursive paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound C1 (Linking Words)

At B2 you linked ideas with however, therefore, although, whereas. At C1 you gain a sharper, more formal register with connectors such as moreover, consequently, notwithstanding, whereas and nonetheless. Combined with ellipsis and substitution, they make a paragraph read as one cohesive argument rather than a list.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
moreoveradd a stronger, further pointThe message was shorter. Moreover, it was clearer.
consequentlystate a formal result or effectEvery word cost money; consequently, senders cut hard.
notwithstandingconcede "in spite of" (very formal)Notwithstanding its risks, ellipsis saves space.
whereasdraw a direct contrast between two ideasA report says 'do so', whereas a friend says 'do it'.
nonethelesssay "even so", after a concessionThe style has risks; nonetheless, it endures.
howevercontrast between two sentencesThe words are gone. The meaning, however, remains.

See the difference — the same idea, written two ways:

Repetitive ✗Cohesive C1 version ✓
Some people write briefly. Some people write at length. Brief writing is clearer. Long writing is less clear. I prefer brief writing. Some write briefly; others, at length. The brief version is usually clearer, whereas the long one can bury its point; consequently, I prefer the former, and so, I suspect, do most readers.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different C1 connectors from the table. Weave your paragraph; don't list sentences.
  • Moreover, Consequently, However, Nonetheless usually open a sentence and take a comma: "Consequently, the message was cut."

How to build a cohesive paragraph (Cohesion Structure)

A strong C1 opinion paragraph does not repeat its nouns and verbs; it links back to them with substitutes and deleted verbs. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the shape:

PartWhat to writeDevice to reach for
1. Positionstate your view on the questiona clear topic sentence
2. Supportgive a reason, then agree/extend itso / neither agreement + one / ones
3. Counterconcede the other side brieflynotwithstanding / whereas + do so
4. Conclusionrestate your view without repeating itso / not substitution + gapping

Sample Answer (~205 words) Few questions divide opinion as sharply as this one. Some insist that texting has made us admirably concise; others, that it has simply made us careless. I incline towards the first view, though not without reservations.

On the positive side, digital tools reward economy. A message that rambles is ignored; a succinct one is answered at once. Where a formal letter once demanded three redundant paragraphs of courtesy, a text omits them entirely, and no one minds. Moreover, the platforms themselves streamline our habits: character limits force us to cut, and so they should.

Critics, however, counter that concision has curdled into carelessness. Punctuation vanishes; nuance does too. Notwithstanding these losses, I am not persuaded that brevity is the culprit; laziness is. A terse message can still be coherent, whereas a long one can still be muddled.

On balance, I concur with those who see progress rather than decline. The best communicators have always trusted their readers to fill the gaps, and digital writing merely rewards what they always did. Consequently, if our messages sound careless, the remedy is not more words — it is better ones. I think so, at least; my friends, I suspect, would say the same.
