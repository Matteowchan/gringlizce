Grammar: Discourse Markers & Text Organisation

At C2, a text is not a pile of correct sentences — it is a route. Discourse markers are the signposts that tell the reader which way the argument turns next.

Markers are the reader's road signs

By C2 you can build any sentence you like. What now marks the expert is architecture: the ability to make a whole text move — to let a reader feel, at every turn, whether the next idea adds, contrasts, results or restates. That guidance is the work of discourse markers: words and phrases such as moreover, however, consequently, in other words that signal how one stretch of text relates to the next.

Think of them as signposts on a road. A marker makes a small promise about what is coming: however promises a reversal, consequently promises a result, namely promises a specific example. The C2 skill is not merely knowing the markers — it is choosing the one that names the true logical relation, placing it correctly, and using it sparingly enough that the reader trusts every sign.

  • The experiment was tricky. Moreover, it was inconclusive.Addition → a second point is stacked onto the first.
  • The evidence was thin; nevertheless, the rumour spread.Contrast → what follows runs against expectation.
  • The batteries died; consequently, the robot stalled.Result → the second clause is an outcome of the first.
  • The plan failed for one reason — namely, poor timing.Exemplification → the marker specifies exactly what is meant.

1. The four core relations (addition · contrast · result · sequence)

Most text-organising markers do one of four jobs. Learn the family a marker belongs to and you will never signal the wrong relationship. Each family points the reader in a different direction: onward, sideways, forward-to-an-outcome, or step-by-step.

RelationTypical markersExample
additionmoreover, furthermore, in addition, what is moreThe roof leaked; furthermore, the wiring was unsafe.
contrasthowever, nevertheless, conversely, on the other handCosts rose; however, demand held firm.
resultconsequently, hence, thus, therefore, as a resultThe bridge closed; consequently, traffic doubled.
sequencefirstly, subsequently, ultimately, finallyFirstly, we gathered the data; subsequently, we modelled it.
C2 subtlety
  • The relation must be true, not decorative: use consequently only where a genuine cause-and-effect exists, not merely to sound weighty.
  • Conversely is stronger than however: it signals a full mirror-image reversal, not just a mild qualification.

2. Three more relations (reformulation · exemplification · concession)

Beyond the four core families sit three that a C2 writer uses to refine an argument: restating an idea more clearly, pinning it to an example, and conceding a point before countering it. These are the markers that make prose feel considered rather than merely linked.

RelationTypical markersExample
reformulationin other words, that is to say, to put it differentlyThe scheme is regressive; in other words, it hits the poor hardest.
exemplificationnamely, for instance, for example, such asTwo players left the team — namely, the captain and the keeper.
concessionadmittedly, granted, to be sure, of courseAdmittedly, the data are limited; the trend is nonetheless clear.
C2 subtlety — namely vs for instance
  • Namely introduces the complete, exact set ("two members — namely, A and B"); for instance offers one example among several.
  • A concession marker such as admittedly works best when paired with a contrast marker afterwards (admittedly… nevertheless…) — concede, then turn.

3. Register: formal vs conversational markers (however vs but / though)

Markers carry register. Each relation has a formal member for essays and reports, and a plain, conversational twin for speech and messages. The meaning is close; the tone is not. Dropping notwithstanding into a text to a friend sounds pompous, whereas but in an academic conclusion sounds slack.

RelationFormal markerConversational twin
contrasthowever, nevertheless, notwithstandingbut, though, still
additionmoreover, furthermore, in additionalso, plus, and
resultconsequently, hence, thusso, that's why
sequencesubsequently, ultimatelythen, in the end
C2 subtlety
  • Keep the register of the marker consistent with the prose around it: a formal report that suddenly says "so anyway" jars, exactly as a chat that says "notwithstanding" does.
  • Position also shifts register: however mid-sentence ("The claim, however, is weak") reads more formal than but at the front of a clause.

4. Placement & punctuation (✗ / ✓)

A marker in the wrong place, or with the wrong punctuation, breaks the very flow it should create. Sentence connectors such as however, therefore, moreover are not conjunctions: they cannot splice two clauses with a comma. Use a full stop or a semicolon, and fence the marker with commas.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
The team was strong, however it lost.The team was strong. However, it lost."however" cannot join two clauses with a comma (a comma splice); it needs a full stop or semicolon.
It rained therefore we stayed in.It rained; therefore, we stayed in.a sentence connector needs a semicolon or full stop before it, and a comma after.
The plan, however was abandoned.The plan, however, was abandoned.a mid-sentence marker must be fenced by commas on both sides.

5. Overuse: don't stack the signposts (✗ / ✓)

A marker is only useful if the reader trusts it. Cramming several into one sentence — or opening every sentence with one — makes prose feel anxious and over-managed. The best writers reserve the marker for the genuine turn and let plain logic carry the rest.

Over-marked ✗Cleaner ✓Why?
Moreover, furthermore, in addition, the noise rose.The noise rose; moreover, the mess spread.three addition markers do one job; keep one and let it work.
Consequently, therefore, thus, the trip was cancelled.Consequently, the trip was cancelled.stacked result markers add noise, not logic.
However, but, though, the score held all the same.The score held all the same.the contrast is already clear from "all the same"; no marker is needed.
C2 paradox
  • The more transparent your argument, the fewer markers it needs: strong structure does most of the signposting on its own.
  • Vary your markers, too — repeating "however" five times reads as clumsily as stacking three in a row.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the marker faults that surface even in fluent C2 writing. Each is a failure of logic or control, not of grammar. Audit your drafts against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
She trained hard; however, she won.She trained hard; consequently, she won.winning follows training — this is a result, not a contrast; "however" names the wrong relation.
The clouds cleared, moreover, the wind dropped too.The clouds cleared; moreover, the wind dropped too.comma splice: "moreover" needs a semicolon or full stop before it.
Two players left, for instance, the captain and the keeper.Two players left — namely, the captain and the keeper.the exact, complete set calls for "namely", not the one-of-many "for instance".
Notwithstanding, the forecast, the trip was cancelled.Notwithstanding the forecast, the trip was cancelled."notwithstanding" here is a preposition — no comma splits it from its noun.
Firstly… secondly… and lastly at the end finally to conclude.Firstly… secondly… finally,…piling up sequence markers ("lastly… finally… to conclude") is redundant; one closes the list.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

A writer drafts a report paragraph; an editor tightens the signposting. Read it twice and watch how each change makes the logical relation clearer — and why fewer, truer markers beat many loud ones.

W:I wrote: "The play started late, however, and moreover, therefore the audience grumbled."Three markers collide: the relations cancel out and the reader is lost.
E:Decide the real relation first. The late start caused the grumbling, so it's a result: "The play started late; consequently, the audience grumbled."One marker, correctly chosen, does the whole job.
W:Then I add a contrasting point: the cast still remembered every line.A genuine turn against expectation — this one earns a contrast marker.
E:Good — that's a real reversal: "Nevertheless, the cast remembered every line." Put the marker at the front, fenced with a comma.Placement and punctuation signal the turn cleanly.
W:Is "nevertheless" too formal? It's just our class blog.Register must match the document and its readers.
E:For a school report it fits perfectly. To a friend in a message you'd write "but they still nailed it" — same relation, plainer register.Choose the marker's register to suit the reader.
Test yourself
  • Why is "consequently" better than "however" in the first fix? (The complaints are a result of the lateness, so the relation is cause-and-effect, not contrast.)
  • What single change turns "The team was strong, however it lost" into correct punctuation? (Replace the first comma with a full stop or semicolon: "The team was strong. However, it lost.")
  • Rewrite "Nevertheless, the cast remembered every line" for a casual chat. ("But they still nailed it" — the relation stays, the register drops.)

Reading: Signposts for the Reader

Read the essay, then work through the five tasks.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Skilled readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think of the last time a paragraph confused you. Was the problem the ideas themselves, or the missing links between them?
  • The essay compares discourse markers to signposts on a road. What does a signpost promise, and what happens if it points the wrong way?
  • Writers sometimes warn against overusing markers. Watch for the moment the essay makes that point, and ask why more can mean less.
How to read
  • First read: read quickly for the whole argument — what discourse markers do and why they matter. Don't stop for every word.
  • Second read: track the markers themselves — addition ("moreover"), contrast ("however", "nevertheless"), result ("consequently", "hence"), sequence ("firstly", "ultimately"), and the moment the writer says they can be overused. Ask what each one signals.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): discourse marker · cohesion · transition · signpost · sequential · concession

Signposts for the Reader

Good prose does not merely state its points; it shows the reader how those points connect. This is the quiet work of discourse markers — the small words and phrases that signal addition, contrast, consequence and sequence, turning a heap of sentences into an argument a reader can follow. A skilled writer treats them as signposts: firstly to orient, subsequently to guide, and ultimately to deliver the reader to a conclusion that feels earned.

To see the machinery at work, it helps to sort markers by the relation they encode. Some are additive, gathering evidence into a single heap: moreover, furthermore, in addition. Others are adversative, pivoting the argument against itself: however, whereas, on the contrary. A third family is causal, drawing conclusions from what has gone before: therefore, consequently, hence. A fourth is sequential, laying claims out in order so that the reader always knows where in the argument she stands: firstly, subsequently, finally. These families are not watertight, and a single word may serve two masters at once; nevertheless, the rough taxonomy earns its keep, because it reminds the writer that every marker makes a claim about structure and not merely about tone. To choose one is to declare, in miniature, how the next idea stands to the last.

Consider how much a single marker can do. "However" warns of a reversal ahead; "consequently" promises that a result follows; "in other words" offers to restate a difficult idea more plainly. Each marker is a small contract with the reader. Break it — signal contrast and then add mere agreement — and the reader stumbles, however elegant the individual sentences may be.

Linguists draw a helpful distinction here between cohesion and coherence. Cohesion is the surface stitching — the visible thread of pronouns, repetitions and, above all, discourse markers that holds a text together at the level of the sentence. Coherence lies deeper: it is the sense that the whole hangs together, that one idea genuinely earns the next. Markers belong to cohesion, and therein lies their peril. A text can be lavishly cohesive, studded with "therefore" and "moreover" at every join, and yet remain incoherent, because the underlying ideas were never truly connected in the first place. The markers, in such cases, are promissory notes the argument cannot honour. Conversely, a deeply coherent passage may wear its cohesion lightly, trusting the reader to supply the links that the logic has already made plain.

The trouble deepens when one moves between languages. Discourse markers rarely map one to one across tongues; the German "doch", the French "or", the Turkish "oysa" each carry shades that no single English word quite reproduces. Translators know this intimately, and much of their art lies in reconstructing, marker by marker, the logical skeleton of a text that its original language expressed through altogether different means. A clumsy translation is often betrayed not by mistaken vocabulary but by mishandled connectives — by an argument whose joints have been set at the wrong angle. Hence the seasoned translator attends less to individual words than to the invisible architecture the markers quietly hold up.

Register, moreover, governs the choice. In an academic paper one writes "nevertheless" and "furthermore"; in a chat one writes "but" and "also". The meaning is close; the register is not. A formal marker dropped into casual speech sounds pompous, whereas a casual one in a report sounds careless. Namely, the writer must match the signpost to the road.

Register, it should be added, is not a single dial but a cluster of them. Formality is one; another is the gulf between speech and writing. In conversation we lean on "so", "anyway" and "well" — markers that manage the give and take of talk, buying time, yielding the floor, signalling a change of subject. On the page these have little place; the writer, denied the listener's nods and interruptions, must instead anticipate the reader's questions and answer them with sturdier connectives. Admittedly the boundary blurs in an age of email and instant messaging, genres that are written yet speak in the cadence of talk. Even so, the underlying principle holds firm: the marker must suit not only the subject but the channel through which it reaches its reader.

There is, moreover, a psychological dimension that the metaphor of the signpost only half captures. A marker does not merely label a turn after the fact; it shapes the reader's expectation before the sentence has even arrived. Read the word "however" and the mind braces for a qualification, leaning slightly forward, ready to weigh what follows against what came before. This is precisely why a betrayed marker is so disorienting: the reader has already committed attention to a relation that never materialises, and must double back to rebuild the sense of the passage. Good markers, then, do not simply record the writer's logic after the event; they lend the reader a kind of foresight, letting her read the next clause in the right frame of mind.

Whereas the novice tends to scatter markers as a nervous habit, the practised writer adds and removes them during revision with almost surgical care. In a first draft the connectives serve as scaffolding, helping the writer discover the very shape of the argument; in later drafts many of them can be quietly dismantled, the building now standing on its own. This is delicate work, for the same marker that clarifies one join may clutter another. The test is simple, if not always easy: read the sentence without the marker and ask whether the relation is still felt. If it is, the marker is very likely dead weight; if the reader would stumble without it, it has earned its place on the page.

There is, finally, a dimension that lifts the stakes above mere tidiness: discourse markers are instruments of persuasion. To write "consequently" is to assert that a conclusion follows of necessity; to write "admittedly" is to concede a point while quietly reserving the right to overturn it; to write "nevertheless" is to grant the objection its due and then step calmly past it. Each is a small rhetorical manoeuvre, a way of managing not only the reader's understanding but the reader's assent. The skilled essayist deploys them as a chess player deploys tempo, conceding here in order to press an advantage there. Whereas a bare list of facts leaves the reader to draw her own conclusions unaided, a well-marked argument leads her, step by consenting step, toward the writer's own.

That said, markers can be overused. A paragraph crammed with "moreover", "consequently" and "furthermore" reads as if it distrusts the reader's intelligence. Admittedly, some links must be made explicit; nevertheless, the best writers let logic carry much of the weight, reserving the marker for the genuine turn. Hence a paradox: the more transparent an argument, the fewer signposts it needs. Ultimately, discourse markers are not decoration but direction — and, like any direction, they help only when they point the right way.

Writing: Build a Signposted Argument

Argue a case in a single, well-organised paragraph, letting discourse markers make every turn of the logic transparent. Your teacher will review it.

Markers by relation (Reference Table)

At C1 you learned individual connectors; at C2 you learn to deploy them by relation, choosing the one that names the true logical turn and matches the register. Keep this table beside you as you draft: pick the relation first, then the marker.

RelationFormal markersFormal example

Rule
  • Name the relation before you reach for a marker: is this a result, a contrast, or merely an addition? The wrong family misdirects the reader.
  • Punctuate sentence connectors correctly (full stop or semicolon before, comma after), and do not stack them — one per genuine turn.

Sample Answer (~225 words) Should schools ban homework?
Firstly, the case for banning homework is strong on well-being grounds: children who spend every evening on worksheets have no time to play, read for pleasure or simply rest. Moreover, the hours freed from copying and cramming can become sport, music or family time, so the benefit is not merely academic but personal. Admittedly, such a ban carries real costs: some skills, such as spelling and times tables, are cemented by practice, and parents worry that grades will slip. Nevertheless, these objections argue for smarter tasks, not for keeping the pile as it is; short, purposeful practice can be signposted clearly within the school day. The evidence from schools that have acted is, in other words, a matter of design rather than principle. Where a ban is paired with a coherent lesson plan — that is to say, focused classwork and quick feedback — the transition proves smooth and marks actually rise. Where it is imposed without that cohesion, however, it fails, and the complaints are loud. Consequently, the lesson is not whether to set homework but how: the discussion around the policy must be as sequential and explicit as the timetable itself. Hence my position. A homework-free evening is neither lazy nor risky; it is simply a choice that a thoughtful school, having weighed the trade-offs, can make. Ultimately, learning belongs in the classroom; the evening, I would argue, belongs to the child.
