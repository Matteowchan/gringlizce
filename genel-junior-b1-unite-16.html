Grammar: Determiners & Quantifiers

Study the rules, look at the examples, then do the exercises.

From vague amounts to exact quantities

At A2 you used a few basic words to show amount: a lot of, many, some. That works for simple sentences, but real B1 English needs to be more precise. Determiners and quantifiers are the small words that come before a noun to show which one or how much / how many: all, most, some, any, no, both, each, every, (a) few, (a) little.

The most important skill is agreement. Some quantifiers go only with countable nouns (few, both, each, every), some only with uncountable nouns (little, much), and some with both (all, most, some, any, no). Choosing the wrong one is the most common B1 mistake, so we will focus on it carefully.

  • Most people recycle at home.most + plural countable noun → general amount.
  • I have very little free time.little + uncountable noun (time).
  • Each student has a locker.each + singular noun + singular verb.

1. General quantity (all / most / some / any / no / none)

These words show quantity from 100% down to 0%. They work with both countable (plural) and uncountable nouns. The tricky parts are some vs any and no vs none.

WordMeaning / useExample
all100% of the groupAll children need sleep.
mostthe biggest partMost people like music.
somea part (in positive sentences & offers)I bought some apples.
anyin questions & negativesIs there any milk? / We don't have any bread.
no + nounzero (with a positive verb)There is no time.
nonezero (a pronoun; stands alone)How many are left? None.
Key point
  • Use some in positive sentences and offers; use any in questions and negatives: "We have some sugar." / "We don't have any sugar."
  • no + noun takes a positive verb: "There is no sugar." (NOT "There isn't no sugar.")
  • none replaces the noun and stands alone: "None of them came."

2. A specific group (all / most / some ... of the)

Use a quantifier alone to talk about things in general, but add of the / of my / of these when you mean a specific, known group. Compare: "Most people..." (people in general) and "Most of the people at the party..." (a specific group).

General (no "of")Specific (+ of the)Note
Most students study hard.Most of the students in my class study hard.a known group → add "of the".
Some cheese is expensive.Some of the cheese was old.works the same with uncountable nouns.
All children play.All (of) the children were tired.after all / both, "of" is optional.
The rule that saves marks
  • Before the / my / these / us / them you must use of: "some of the water", "none of us".
  • After all and both, "of" is optional: "all (of) the students".
  • Never say "most of people" ✗ — either "most people" or "most of the people" ✓.

3. Two things (both / either / neither)

Use these three only when you talk about two things or people. The verb form is the key: both is plural, but either and neither are singular.

WordMeaningStructure & example
boththe two togetherBoth restaurants are good. (both + plural + plural verb)
eitherone OR the otherEither day is fine. (either + singular + singular verb)
neithernot one and not the otherNeither answer is correct. (neither + singular + singular verb)
Watch the verb
  • both + plural noun + plural verb: "Both shops are open."
  • either / neither + singular noun + singular verb: "Neither shop is open."
  • Pairs: both...and, either...or, neither...nor: "I like both tea and coffee." / "Neither Ali nor Sam came."

4. Every individual member (each / every)

Both talk about all the members of a group, but seen one by one. They are followed by a singular noun and a singular verb — this surprises many students.

WordFocusExample
eachthe members one by one (2 or more)Each player gets a card.
everyall members as a group (3 or more)Every player was tired.
each of the + pluralone by one, a specific groupEach of the players was ready.
Singular, always
  • each / every + singular noun + singular verb: "Every room has a window." (NOT "Every rooms have")
  • Use each of the / my + plural noun: "Each of my brothers lives abroad."
  • For only two things, use each, not every: "She had a bag in each hand."

5. Small amounts ((a) few / (a) little)

These four are easy to mix up, but two questions decide everything: is the noun countable or uncountable, and is there "a" or not?

WordGoes withMeaning
a fewcountable pluralsome — a small but positive amount
fewcountable pluralnot many — almost none (negative)
a littleuncountablesome — a small but positive amount
littleuncountablenot much — almost none (negative)
The "a" changes the feeling
  • "I have a few friends here." = some friends (positive). "I have few friends here." = almost none (negative).
  • "There is a little milk." = some milk. "There is little milk." = almost no milk.
  • few / a few + countable (books); little / a little + uncountable (water). Never "a few water" ✗ or "a little books" ✗.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

Almost every B1 mistake with quantifiers comes from wrong agreement (countable / uncountable) or a missing or extra "of".

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
Every students have a book.Every student has a book.every + singular noun + singular verb.
I don't have some money.I don't have any money.use "any" in negatives, not "some".
Most of people like it.Most people like it.no "of" without "the"; or "most of the people".
There isn't no bread.There is no bread."no" already means zero; don't add a second negative.
I have a little friends.I have a few friends.friends is countable → use "a few".
Both my sister is a pupil.Both my sisters are pupils.both + plural noun + plural verb.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two classmates check the art cupboard before their drawing lesson. Read it twice and notice how each quantifier fits its noun — singular after each / every, plural after both / a few, and an uncountable noun after a little.

  • — Do we have any glue? — There's a little, but not much.any in a question; a little + uncountable (glue).
  • — How many crayons? — Only a few. Most of them are gone.a few + countable (crayons); most of + them.
  • — Both shelves near us are full, so either one is fine.both + plural verb; either + singular noun.
  • — Let's check each box. Every paint pot here is empty!each / every + singular noun + singular verb.
  • — We need some paper, but we don't need any tape.some in a positive sentence; any in a negative.
  • — None of the pens work. Let's ask for all of them again.none of the (zero); all of them (100%).
Test yourself
  • Why is it "a little glue" and not "a few glue"? (Glue is uncountable, so we use a little.)
  • Change "Every box is empty" to talk about a specific group with each of. (Each of the boxes is empty.)
  • Why "any tape" in "we don't need any tape"? (It is a negative sentence, so we use any, not some.)

Speaking: How Much We Use, Waste & Share

Learn the words, then use them — with quantifiers — to talk about your habits.

quantity (n)
how much or how many of something there is (miktar)
Syn: amount · Ant: —
"We throw away a large quantity of paper each week."
amount (n)
how much of something (usually uncountable) (miktar)
Syn: quantity · Ant: —
"A small amount of glue is enough for the model."
plenty (pron)
a large amount; more than enough (bol, yeterinden fazla)
Syn: a lot · Ant: hardly any
"There is plenty of paper for everyone in the class."
enough (det)
as much or as many as you need (yeterli)
Syn: sufficient · Ant: too little
"Some children do not have enough books to read."
several (det)
more than two but not very many (birkaç, birçok)
Syn: a few · Ant: none
"I read several books before I chose my favourite."
various (adj)
of many different kinds (çeşitli, farklı)
Syn: different · Ant: identical
"The shelf holds various kinds of toys and games."
majority (n)
most of a group; more than half (çoğunluk)
Syn: most · Ant: minority
"The majority of my classmates help out."
spare (adj)
extra; not being used at the moment (fazladan, yedek)
Syn: extra · Ant: needed
"If you have spare toys, please share them."
donate (v)
to give something to help others (bağışlamak)
Syn: give · Ant: keep
"A bookshop donates some story books every month."
reduce (v)
to make something smaller or less (azaltmak)
Syn: cut down · Ant: increase
"We try to reduce the amount of paper we waste."
waste (v/n)
to use badly or throw away for no reason (israf etmek; atık)
Syn: throw away · Ant: save
"Most children waste a little paper each week."
share (v)
to give a part of something to others (paylaşmak)
Syn: split · Ant: keep for yourself
"They share all of the toys fairly."
container (n)
a box or jar used to hold or store things (kap, kutu)
Syn: box, jar · Ant: —
"Each container has a name label on it."
neighbour (n)
a person who lives near you (komşu)
Syn: local resident · Ant: stranger
"Both of my neighbours have children my age."
Talking about quantities: use all, most, some, a few, a little and none.

  • Use each / every with a singular noun and verb, and both / either / neither for two things.

Reading: The Sharing Shelf

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think about how many toys or books you forget in a month. Could any of them be shared?
  • The title is "The Sharing Shelf". What do you think a shelf in a classroom for sharing toys and books is for?
  • This unit is about quantifiers. As you read, underline every all, most, some, each, a few and a little you find.
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text for the answers. Notice how each quantifier fits its noun (countable or uncountable).

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): quantity · amount · plenty · spare · donate · reduce · waste · share

From a few books to a shelf that helps the whole class.
The Sharing Shelf in Room 5

A few months ago, the children in Room 5 had a problem. Every week, most pupils brought too many toys and books to school, and some of them were soon forgotten. At the same time, a few classmates had almost nothing to read or play with at break time. One rainy morning, two friends, Emma and Sam, decided that neither of them wanted to see all these good things wasted. They shared a simple idea: a shelf where anyone in the class could leave a toy or a book and take one they needed.

Emma loved drawing, and Sam was mad about space and science. Neither of them had many toys, but both of them cared about the children around them. One afternoon, Emma saw several good books in the bin near the gate, while a little further away a quiet boy sat alone with no book at all. “This does not make any sense,” she said to Sam. “There are plenty of books in this school. The problem is that they are in the wrong places.” Sam agreed at once, and together they began to plan.

At first, few children believed the plan would work. There was very little room on the first small shelf, and none of the other classes wanted to join. Even so, the two friends did not give up. They asked each classmate to give a little time or a few spare toys. Slowly, both the shelf and the trust between the children began to grow.

Setting up the shelf was not easy. A kind teacher offered a little space by the door, and a boy called Deniz spent a whole lunchtime making sure each box was clean. Each of the first helpers brought something different: one girl brought bright stickers, another brought a box of jars for the pencils, and a few pupils painted a big sign that said “Take a little, give a little.”

Today, the Sharing Shelf stands by the door of Room 5. Every morning, the helpers check it, and each toy is cleaned so that all of the things stay safe to use. Most of the books come from the children’s own homes, and some of the games arrive from older pupils. If you look at the shelf, you will almost never find it empty; there is always at least a little of something for everyone.

The rules are easy. Take only what you need, and leave some for others. Both giving and taking are welcome, and either one helps the whole class. There is no charge, and no child is ever turned away. Several families now donate any spare books instead of throwing them out.

All of the helpers agree on one thing: none of this would have happened without a few determined children and a lot of patience. The project has reduced the number of things the class wastes, and the majority of the children say they feel closer than ever before.

The children of Room 5 now dream of doing more. They hope to add a second shelf in another classroom. Emma often reminds the helpers that big changes usually begin with small actions. “We did not have much at first,” she says with a smile, “but we had each other, and that was enough.”

Writing: Wasting Less, Sharing More

Write and submit your paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Quantifiers make writing precise (Quantifiers)

Weak B1 writing repeats the same vague words (a lot of, many) or lists short, separate sentences. What makes writing mature is precise, correct quantity. This unit gives you a full set of quantifiers for general amounts, two things, individuals and small amounts. Use them — and remember the golden rule: match the quantifier to its noun.

QuantifierUse it to...Example
all / most / someshow a general amountMost of my friends share toys.
no / noneshow zeroNone of us wastes paper on purpose.
both / either / neithertalk about two thingsBoth my parents help at school.
each / everytalk about individualsEvery child can help a little.
(a) few / (a) littleshow a small amountI waste a little paper and only a few crayons.
... of thepoint to a specific groupSome of the toys in my room are old.

See the difference — the same facts, written two ways:

Vague & separate ✗Precise, fluent writing ✓
I have toys. I do not play with them all. I throw toys away. My friends are the same. Every month my family buys a lot of toys, but we do not use all of them. Most of them are fine, yet a few are wasted, and none of us likes throwing them away.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different quantifiers from this unit. Do not repeat "a lot of" everywhere.
  • Remember: a few + countable, a little + uncountable; each / every + singular noun and verb; add of the only for a specific group.

Paragraph structure (Paragraph Structure)

A "quantity" paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same every time — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningName your topic in one general sentenceEvery week my family uses a large amount of paper and time.
2. BodyHow much you use, waste and share. Use general amounts (all / most / some / none), two things (both / either / neither) and small amounts (a few / a little).Most of my crayons still work, but a little paper is still wasted. Both of my parents try to reduce it.
3. ClosingWhat you have learned or want to changeNow none of us wastes as much, and we share any spare toys.

Task (Görev)

Write about how much of different things (toys, snacks, crayons, time...) you and your family use, waste and share. Show general amounts, individuals and small amounts with quantifiers.

WHAT TO INCLUDE

Sample Answer (~165 words) Every week, my family uses a large amount of paper, snacks and time, but we do not always use them well. Most of our crayons and books are still good, and some of the toys come from my cousins. Sadly, a little paper is still wasted, and a few pens dry up before we finish them.

Both of my parents want to reduce this waste. Neither of them likes throwing things away, so each person in the house now has a job. My mother checks the toy box, and every container has a name label on it. None of us keeps more than we need, and if we have plenty of spare books, we donate them to the children next door.

The majority of our rubbish is now much smaller. I have learned that all of these small changes matter. If each family wastes just a little less, the whole class can share more and throw away almost nothing.
