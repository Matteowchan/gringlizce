Grammar: Reported Speech (Statements)

Study the rules, look at the examples, then do the exercises.

Telling someone what another person said

Every day we repeat other people's words: "My boss said the meeting was cancelled," "She told me she was tired." This is called reported speech (or indirect speech). Instead of quoting the exact words with quotation marks, we report the idea inside a longer sentence.

Compare the two ways of saying the same thing. Direct speech gives the exact words: Anna said, "I am busy." Reported speech tells us the message without quotation marks: Anna said that she was busy. Notice that the verb has moved back in time — this backward movement is the key skill of the whole unit.

  • Direct: Tom said, "I work in a bank."His exact words, in quotation marks.
  • Reported: Tom said that he worked in a bank.The present ("work") moves back to the past ("worked").
  • Reported: She told me that she would arrive late."will" becomes "would" when we report it.

1. The tense backshift (one step into the past)

When the reporting verb is in the past (said, told), the verb in the message usually moves one step back in time. Learn this table well — it is the engine of reported speech.

Direct speechReported speechExample
Present Simple (work)Past Simple (worked)"I work here." → He said he worked there.
Present Continuous (am working)Past Continuous (was working)"I am working." → She said she was working.
Past Simple (worked)Past Perfect (had worked)"I worked late." → He said he had worked late.
Present Perfect (have worked)Past Perfect (had worked)"I have finished." → She said she had finished.
Key point
  • The subject pronoun changes to match the new speaker: "I am ready" → She said she was ready.
  • The word that is optional: He said (that) he was tired. Both are correct.

2. Modal verbs also shift (will → would, can → could)

The little helping verbs change too. These three are the most common in exams, so make them automatic.

DirectReportedExample
willwould"I will call." → He said he would call.
cancould"I can swim." → She said she could swim.
maymight"I may be late." → He said he might be late.
musthad to"I must go." → She said she had to go.
Golden rule
  • Never keep will or can after a past reporting verb: She said she would come ✓ — She said she will come ✗.

3. say vs tell (the classic trap)

Both verbs introduce reported speech, but they follow different patterns. This is one of the most common B1 mistakes, so learn the difference by heart.

VerbPatternExample
tellalways needs a person (an object) after itShe told me that she was busy.
sayno person directly after it (or use "say to me")She said that she was busy.
Watch out
  • Never use tell without a person: He told me the news ✓ — He told that the news... ✗.
  • Never put a person straight after say: He said he was ill ✓ — He said me he was ill ✗.

4. Time and place words change (now → then)

Because we usually report words later and in a different place, the little words of time and place must move too.

DirectReportedExample
nowthen"I am busy now." → ... busy then.
todaythat day"I saw it today." → ... that day.
herethere"I live here." → ... lived there.
thisthat"I want this one." → ... that one.
yesterdaythe day before"I came yesterday." → ... the day before.
tomorrowthe next day"I'll go tomorrow." → ... the next day.

5. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B1 students make most often with reported statements. Check your own writing against this list.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
He told that he was late.He told me that he was late."tell" always needs a person.
She said me she was tired.She said she was tired.no person directly after "say".
He said he will help.He said he would help."will" must become "would".
She said she is happy.She said she was happy.the present moves back to the past.
He said he can drive.He said he could drive."can" becomes "could" in reported speech.

6. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Read this short exchange, then read how a third person reports it. Notice every backshift, every say/tell choice, and every time word that changes.

Ece"I am looking for a new job, and I will move to Ankara next month."
Can"I can help you. I know a manager here."
  • Ece said that she was looking for a new job.Present Continuous → Past Continuous.
  • She added that she would move to Ankara the next month."will" → "would"; "next month" → "the next month".
  • Can told her that he could help her."tell" + person; "can" → "could".
  • He said that he knew a manager there.Present → past; "here" → "there".
Test yourself
  • Why "told her" and not "said her"? (See section 3: "tell" always needs a person.)
  • Change "I can help you" into reported speech. (He said he could help me.)
  • What happens to "here" when we report it? (It becomes "there".)

Speaking: Reporting What People Say

Learn the reporting words, then use them to tell your own stories.

report (v)
to tell people about something you saw or heard (bildirmek, aktarmak)
Syn: tell · Ant: hide
"She reported that the road was closed."
mention (v)
to say something briefly, without many details (bahsetmek, değinmek)
Syn: refer to · Ant: omit
"He mentioned that he was moving abroad."
explain (v)
to make something clear so people understand (açıklamak)
Syn: clarify · Ant: confuse
"She explained why she was late."
admit (v)
to agree something is true, often unwillingly (kabul etmek, itiraf etmek)
Syn: confess · Ant: deny
"He admitted that he had made a mistake."
deny (v)
to say that something is not true (inkar etmek)
Syn: reject · Ant: admit
"She denied that she had said it."
complain (v)
to say you are not happy about something (şikayet etmek)
Syn: protest · Ant: praise
"They complained that the food was cold."
promise (v)
to say you will certainly do something (söz vermek)
Syn: guarantee · Ant: refuse
"He promised that he would help me."
suggest (v)
to offer an idea or a plan (önermek)
Syn: propose · Ant: —
"She suggested that we meet on Friday."
announce (v)
to tell people something officially and publicly (duyurmak)
Syn: declare · Ant: conceal
"The boss announced that the office would close early."
claim (v)
to say something is true without proof (iddia etmek)
Syn: assert · Ant: deny
"He claimed that he had finished the work."
reply (v)
to answer what someone has said (yanıtlamak, cevap vermek)
Syn: respond · Ant: ask
"She replied that she was not sure."
remind (v)
to make someone remember something (hatırlatmak)
Syn: prompt · Ant: —
"He reminded me that the exam was on Monday."
rumour (n)
a story that people tell, which may not be true (söylenti, dedikodu)
Syn: gossip · Ant: fact
"There is a rumour that the shop is closing."
according to (phr)
as stated or reported by someone (-e göre)
Syn: as said by · Ant: —
"According to Ali, the train was late."
Direct words become reported speech: "will" becomes "would".

Reading: News Travels Fast

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Have you ever heard a rumour at work or at school that turned out to be false?
  • The text reports what several people said. Which reporting verbs (said, told, explained...) do you expect to see?
  • Look at the title, "News Travels Fast". What do you think the text will say about how information spreads?
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text. Notice every reported statement ("she said that...") and each backshift.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): report · mention · explain · admit · announce · rumour · reply · according to

One message, three people: how a story changes as it is reported.
News Travels Fast: The Office Rumour

I work for a medium-sized company in the city centre, and most of my days follow the same quiet pattern. I arrive around nine o'clock, make a cup of coffee in the small kitchen, and then sit down at my desk near the window. On an ordinary morning, the only sounds are keyboards, printers, and the occasional phone call. That is why I still remember one particular week so clearly: it was the week when a single rumour turned our calm office into a place full of whispers, guesses, and nervous laughter.

Last Monday, something strange happened at our office, and by lunchtime everyone was talking about it. My colleague Selin told me that the company was going to open a new branch in London. She said that the manager had announced it in a private meeting, and she seemed very excited.

The rumour spread faster than I could have imagined. During the morning, I noticed small groups of people standing near the coffee machine, speaking in low voices. Some of them looked worried, while others looked hopeful. A young man from the sales team told his friend that half of the staff would move to another country. A woman from human resources said that the salaries would probably change too. Nobody knew exactly what was true, but everybody had an opinion, and each new version of the story sounded a little more dramatic than the last one.

At first I did not believe her, because Selin often repeats stories without checking them. However, later that day another colleague, Mert, also mentioned the same news. He said that he had heard it from someone in the finance team. He told me that the company would need volunteers to move abroad, so I started to feel curious.

Mert is usually a careful person, so his words made me stop and think. During lunch, he explained more details to me. He said that the finance team had been preparing new budgets for weeks, and that they had discussed the cost of renting an office in another city. He also told me that his friend in that team had seen some documents written in English. To Mert, all of this was strong evidence. I still was not completely sure, but I had to admit that the story now seemed more serious than a simple piece of gossip.

After lunch, I decided that I should not repeat anything until I knew the truth. I have learned that repeating a rumour can be dangerous, because you never know who is listening or how your words will be understood. So, instead of asking my colleagues again, I chose to look for someone who really knew what was happening. I walked slowly through the corridor, past the meeting rooms, hoping that I might see one of the senior managers and ask a direct question.

When I met our manager, Mr. Kaya, in the corridor, I decided to ask him directly. He smiled and said that the rumour was only half true. He explained that the company was indeed planning something new, but he added that no final decision had been made yet. He also asked us not to worry, because nobody would lose their job.

Then he told me something that I found very interesting. He said that managers often hear the same rumours as everyone else, and that they are sometimes surprised by how much the story has changed. He explained that a plan which is still only an idea can suddenly sound like a final decision once it travels from desk to desk. He asked me, in a friendly way, to help him keep people calm, and to remind my colleagues that official news would always come by email, not through whispers in the corridor.

While I was walking back to my desk, I thought about how quickly information travels. One person says something, another person reports it, and soon the whole office believes a story that may not be completely accurate. Although rumours can be exciting, they are often wrong.

I remembered other examples from the past. Once, a colleague was absent for two days, and by the end of the week people said that she had resigned and moved to another company. In fact, she had simply been ill with a bad cold. Another time, someone saw the director carrying boxes out of his office, and a rumour started that the whole department would close. The truth was much less exciting: he was only moving to a bigger room on the same floor. In every case, a small, ordinary event had grown into a big, frightening story, simply because people repeated it without asking questions. Each person who told the story added one more detail, and after a few hours the final version was almost impossible to recognise. I realised that a rumour is a little like a game we used to play as children, where a sentence is whispered from ear to ear until it becomes something completely different at the end of the line.

That evening, on my way home, I promised myself that I would try to be a more careful listener. I decided that when I heard surprising news, I would ask two simple questions: "Who told you this?" and "How do they know?" These questions are short, but they can stop a false story before it spreads. I also thought about how easy it is, especially in a busy office, to enjoy a dramatic rumour instead of waiting patiently for the facts.

The next morning, Mr. Kaya sent an email. He announced that the company had decided to open a small office in London, but he said that the move was still months away. Everyone was relieved, and this time the news was real.

Looking back on that week, I think the whole experience taught the office a useful lesson. Rumours will always exist, because people are naturally curious and they love to share news. Yet a clear message from a reliable source can calm a hundred nervous conversations in a single moment. Since that Monday, whenever someone rushes towards me with an exciting story, I smile, listen politely, and then quietly wait for the official email before I decide what to believe.

From dialogue to report (Model)

In real life we constantly turn conversations into reports: someone speaks, and later we tell a third person what they said. Study this short dialogue and the report below it.

Deniz"That's fine. I will save some cake for you, and I'll bring it tomorrow."

Writing: Reporting a Conversation

Write and submit your paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Connectors turn sentences into a text (Linking Words)

A list of short, separate sentences is not real writing. What connects your ideas is linking words. At A2 you used and, but, so, because. At B1 you add four more powerful connectors: also, when, while, although and however. These are especially useful when you report a conversation, because they join what different people said.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
andadd informationShe said she was tired, and she added that she wanted to rest.
butshow a small contrastHe said he was busy, but he promised to call later.
soshow a resultShe said the shop was closed, so we went home.
becausegive a reasonHe explained he was late because the bus had broken down.
alsoadd an extra pointShe said she was moving. She also mentioned a new job.
whensay at what past timeWhen I asked her, she said she did not know.
whiletwo things at the same timeWhile we were talking, he told me the news.
althoughshow a strong contrastAlthough he denied it, I did not believe him.
howevercontrast between two sentencesShe promised to help. However, she never replied.

See the difference — the same facts, written two ways:

Separate sentences ✗Connected, fluent writing ✓
I met Ali. He told me the news. He said the office was moving. I was surprised. He said nobody would lose their job. When I met Ali, he told me the news. He said that the office was moving, but he added that nobody would lose their job, so I felt relieved.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different connectors in your paragraph. Do not write sentence by sentence; link your ideas.
  • When when / while / although / however start a sentence, they are usually followed by a comma: "However, she never replied."

Paragraph structure (Paragraph Structure)

A "reporting" paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same every time — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningSay who you spoke to and whenLast week I had an interesting conversation with my friend.
2. BodyReport what each person said. Use the backshift, mix say/tell, and link with connectors.She told me that she had changed her job, and she explained that she was much happier.
3. ClosingA final comment about how you felt or what you decidedAfter our talk, I felt more positive about my own plans.

Task (Görev)

Write about a real or imaginary conversation you had recently, or some news you heard. Report what people said using reported speech.

WHAT TO INCLUDE

  • an opening sentence that says who you talked to and when
  • at least four reported statements with a correct backshift (said/told + past)
  • at least two reporting verbs from the Speaking section (explained, admitted, promised, mentioned, announced...)
  • correct use of say and tell (tell + a person)
  • a closing sentence about how you felt or what you decided
  • at least 3 connectors: and, but, so, because, also, when, while, although, however

EXAMPLE OPENERS

"Last week I had an interesting conversation with ..." "Yesterday my friend told me that ..." "According to my colleague, ..."

Sample Answer (~165 words) Last week I had an interesting conversation with my old friend Deniz, and she told me a lot of surprising news. She said that she had changed her job, and she explained that she was much happier now. When I asked her why, she replied that her old company had been very stressful. She also mentioned that she had started learning Spanish, because she wanted to work abroad one day.

Although I was happy for her, I admitted that I felt a little jealous. I told her that I had not made any big changes recently. However, she encouraged me and said that it was never too late to try something new. She promised that she would help me write a better CV.

By the end of the conversation, I felt more positive. Deniz reminded me that everybody moves at a different speed, so I decided to stop worrying. I have not found my dream job yet, but I am ready to start looking.
