Ünite 9 · Nominalisation · Bölüm 1/6

Grammar: Nominalisation

Turn actions into ideas. Learn how academic writing packs whole clauses into a single noun.

From doing to thing: the engine of academic prose

Everyday English tells us what people do: "They decided quickly," "Prices increased," "The ice melted." Academic and formal English prefers to talk about things and ideas: "their quick decision," "the increase in prices," "the melting of the ice." This transformation — turning a verb or adjective into a noun — is called nominalisation, and it is one of the clearest signals that a writer has reached C1.

Why bother? Because a noun can do things a verb cannot: it can become the subject of a new sentence, be counted, measured and compared, and carry a whole action forward without repeating who did it. Linguists call this grammatical metaphor — a process (a verb) is re-dressed as an entity (a noun). Handled well, it makes writing dense, impersonal and objective. Handled badly, it makes writing airless. This unit teaches both the skill and its limits.

  • They decided quickly. → Their quick decision surprised everyone.Verb (decide) → noun (decision); the action becomes a thing you can describe.
  • The population grew. → The growth of the population alarmed planners.Verb (grow) → noun (growth); now it can be the subject.
  • Because prices increased, demand fell. → Due to the increase in prices, demand fell.A whole clause condensed into a noun phrase (mind the preposition: increase IN).
  • The scientists analysed the data. → The analysis of the data took two weeks.The impersonal noun form removes the doer and foregrounds the process.

1. How to nominalise (verbs & adjectives → nouns)

Most nominalisations are formed by adding a derivational suffix to a verb or adjective. There is no single rule, so the correct ending must be learned word by word — a frequent source of C1 errors. Here are the most productive patterns.

Base wordSuffixNominalisation
decide, refuse, propose (verb)-sion / -al / -aldecision, refusal, proposal
implement, treat, develop (verb)-ation / -ment / -mentimplementation, treatment, development
analyse, grow, fail (verb)-sis / -th / (zero)analysis, growth, failure
weak, accurate, able (adjective)-ness / -cy / -ityweakness, accuracy, ability
Watch the ending
  • The suffix is unpredictable: analyse → analysis ✓, not "analysation" ✗.
  • Some verbs nominalise with zero change or an irregular form: fail → failure, lose → loss, choose → choice.

2. Making the noun the subject (subject-making)

The real power of nominalisation appears when the new noun becomes the grammatical subject of a clause. This lets a writer take a process, treat it as a fixed thing, and then say what it caused or led to. The result is the classic cause-and-effect sentence of academic English.

Spoken / verbalAcademic / nominalisedWhat changes
They implemented the policy, so costs fell.The implementation of the policy led to a fall in costs.the action becomes a subject that "leads to" a result
The government intervened, and the market stabilised.Government intervention stabilised the market.two clauses collapse into one dense sentence
Because he refused, the deal collapsed.His refusal caused the deal to collapse.the reason becomes a nameable cause
Useful cause-effect verbs
  • Nominalised subjects pair well with: led to, resulted in, caused, prompted, gave rise to, contributed to.
  • "The reduction in staff gave rise to delays." sounds far more formal than "Because they cut staff, things were delayed."

3. Preposition agreement (increase IN, reduction IN)

When a verb becomes a noun, the grammar around it changes too. A verb takes an object (increase prices); the matching noun usually takes a preposition (an increase in prices). Choosing the wrong preposition is one of the commonest advanced errors, so the noun + preposition pairing must be memorised as a chunk.

NounPrepositionExample
increase, rise, reduction, decline, fallina sharp increase in demand; a reduction in costs
solution, answer, approach, threattoa solution to the problem; a threat to stability
cause, effect, impact, influenceof / onthe cause of the crisis; the impact on prices
C1 subtlety
  • Growth, increase, rise, reduction, decline all take in: "a decline in attendance" ✓, not "a decline of attendance" in this sense.
  • Keep the pairing fixed: "a solution to the problem" ✓, not "a solution of the problem." ✗

4. Abstract noun phrases & register (density & tone)

Nominalisation lets writers build long abstract noun phrases that condense a great deal of meaning: "the gradual deterioration of public trust," "a marked improvement in air quality." Because the doer disappears, the tone becomes impersonal and objective — exactly what reports, essays and science require. Compare the same content across three registers.

RegisterVersionFeel
very informalPeople trust the government less and less, and that's a problem.personal, chatty, verb-driven
neutralPublic trust in the government is falling, which worries analysts.clear but still verbal
formal / academicThe gradual erosion of public trust is a source of concern.dense, impersonal, nominalised
Register & nuance
  • Nominalisation raises the register: it suits essays, reports and abstracts, but sounds stiff in a friendly email.
  • The abstract noun lets you modify it richly: "a gradual, uneven recovery," "a widespread reduction."

5. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

Each row is a genuine C1 error: a wrong suffix, a wrong preposition, or over-nominalisation that harms clarity. Audit your own writing against every one.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
The analysation of the data took hours.The analysis of the data took hours.wrong suffix; the noun of "analyse" is "analysis".
There was a sharp increase of prices.There was a sharp increase in prices."increase" takes the preposition "in", not "of".
We need a solution of this problem.We need a solution to this problem."solution" pairs with "to".
The facilitation of the improvement of communication was the aim.The reform aimed to improve communication.over-nominalisation; too many abstract nouns kill clarity.
The government's intervene stabilised the market.The government's intervention stabilised the market.a subject must be a noun, not a bare verb.

6. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

An editor helps a student tighten a spoken, verb-heavy draft into controlled academic prose. Watch each verb clause become a noun — and notice where the editor warns against going too far.

S:I wrote: "Because the city grew fast, the roads got congested."Spoken style: two verbs, a personal cause.
E:For an essay, nominalise it: "The rapid growth of the city led to congestion on the roads."Verb (grow) → noun (growth); the process becomes the subject.
S:And "prices went up a lot"?Still verbal and informal.
E:Write "a sharp increase in prices" — and mind the preposition, it is "in", never "of".Noun + fixed preposition as a chunk.
S:Should I turn every verb into a noun, then?A tempting over-correction.
E:No — "the facilitation of the reduction of costs" is airless. Nominalise to condense, but keep plain verbs where clarity demands.The limit: over-nominalisation harms readability.
Test yourself
  • Why "the growth of the city" and not "the city grew" in an essay? (The noun can become a subject that "leads to" a result.)
  • What is wrong with "an increase of prices"? ("increase" takes the preposition "in": an increase in prices.)
  • Rewrite "the facilitation of the reduction of costs" more clearly. (e.g. "a plan to cut costs" — fewer nouns, clearer sense.)

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): nominalisation · condense · dense · abstract · impersonal · objective · cumbersome · streamline · concise

Five spoken words become one dense clause: nominalisation trades warmth for compression.
The Hidden Grammar of Serious Prose

Read a scientific report and a chat with a friend side by side, and one difference leaps out. Everyday speech unfolds as a chain of verbs: things happen, people do them, and the doer is usually named. Academic prose, however, works differently. It relies heavily on nominalisation — the conversion of verbs and adjectives into nouns — so that a whole action can be packed into a single phrase. "The ice melted rapidly" becomes "the rapid melting of the ice"; "because prices rose" becomes "owing to the rise in prices". The result is dense, abstract and, to a newcomer, faintly cold.

To grasp why this shift matters, it helps to see nominalisation for what linguists call it: a form of grammatical metaphor. In its literal, congruent form, a happening is expressed by a verb and its participants by nouns — someone does something to something. When a writer nominalises, that natural mapping is deliberately bent: the happening itself is recast as a participant, a noun that can be pointed at, quantified and manipulated. The verb "to invest" describes an act unfolding in time; the noun "investment" freezes that act into an object that can be called "heavy", "sustained" or "ill-judged", and then slotted into a fresh clause. This capacity to package experience is precisely what makes the device indispensable to disciplines that must generalise, since a science cannot advance if it can only ever narrate particular events one after another.

A brief illustration shows the transformation at work. Imagine a researcher who first jots down what happened in plain, sequential terms: the temperature rose, the pressure built, the valve failed, and the technicians evacuated the plant. Recast for a formal report, that chain of events collapses into a chain of things: "a rise in temperature", "a build-up of pressure", "the failure of the valve", "the evacuation of the plant". Each noun can now be dated, measured against a threshold, and cross-referenced with the next, and the sequence as a whole can be made the subject of a further claim — "this sequence of failures prompted a wholesale revision of safety protocols". Nothing has been lost from the account except the reassuring presence of people doing things; what has been gained is a text that can be analysed, compared with other incidents and argued over with precision.

This density is not mere decoration. Since a nominalisation turns a process into a thing, that thing can then become the subject of a new sentence and be measured, compared or explained. "The implementation of the policy led to a sharp reduction in waste" would be cumbersome to express with verbs alone. Moreover, the noun form lets writers stay impersonal and objective: the emphasis falls on the process, not on who performed it. Whereas a storyteller foregrounds people, a scientist foregrounds phenomena.

The mechanics deserve closer attention, because they are where learners most often stumble. Forming the noun is rarely a matter of a single, predictable rule: "decide" yields "decision", "refuse" yields "refusal", "analyse" yields the irregular "analysis", and a handful of verbs change hardly at all. Once the noun exists, it drags a new set of grammatical habits behind it. A verb governs its object directly, whereas the corresponding noun almost always demands a linking preposition, and the choice is fixed by convention rather than logic: one speaks of an "increase in" output, a "solution to" a difficulty, an "impact on" morale. Master these pairings and the prose acquires an effortless authority; misjudge them and even a sophisticated argument betrays its author as an outsider to the register. Different fields, moreover, cultivate their own favoured abstractions — "liability" and "breach" in law, "elasticity" and "contraction" in economics, "oxidation" and "displacement" in chemistry — yet the underlying grammatical move is identical across them all.

Yet the same tool, overused, becomes a vice. Notwithstanding its usefulness, a sentence stuffed with abstract nouns can grow airless and hard to follow. Skilled writers therefore streamline: they nominalise to condense genuine information, but revert to plain verbs when clarity demands it. Consequently, the best academic style is not the most nominalised; it is the most controlled. The reader should feel the prose is concise, not congested. The convention has not been abandoned; nonetheless, it has been tempered by a modern preference for readable, precise writing over needless complexity.

There is, however, a human cost to all this compression that a careful writer never forgets. Every abstract noun asks the reader to unpack an event that the grammar has quietly hidden; when such nouns accumulate, the mind must hold several suspended processes at once before the sentence resolves. A single well-placed nominalisation lightens the prose by summarising what has already been established, but three or four stacked in a row impose exactly the opposite burden, forcing the reader to reconstruct the very actions the writer took such trouble to conceal. This is why editors treat a thicket of abstractions as a warning sign rather than a mark of sophistication, and why the plainest sentence is often the most authoritative one on the page.

The last few decades have only sharpened this awareness. A broad plain-language movement, embraced by governments, courts and scientific journals alike, has pressed writers to justify every abstraction they use, and to prefer a clear verb wherever a noun would merely obscure. The aim is not to banish nominalisation, which remains the natural grammar of generalisation, but to ration it. A modern report is judged less by how learned it sounds than by how quickly a busy reader can extract its meaning. For the learner, then, the practical lesson is twofold: build a reliable stock of noun forms and their prepositions, so that the device is always available; but treat each nominalisation as a decision to be defended, not a reflex to be indulged. Used with judgement, it lends writing the poise of genuine expertise; used without it, it produces only the hollow echo of authority.

Seen in this light, nominalisation is neither a badge of erudition nor a habit to be shunned, but a precision instrument whose value depends entirely on restraint. It allows a writer to lift an argument above the level of anecdote, to name the forces and trends that no single sentence of narrative could capture, and to set them side by side for comparison. The mature stylist wields it the way a good photographer uses focus: sharpening what matters, letting the rest recede, and never mistaking mere density for depth. In the end, the test is simple. If a reader emerges from a passage understanding more, and more quickly, than plain narration would have allowed, the nominalisation has earned its place; if not, it is only ornament, and ornament is the one thing serious prose can least afford.

Writing: An Academic Explanation

Write and submit a dense but readable academic paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound C1 (Linking Words)

Nominalised prose needs strong connectors to hold its dense ideas together. At C1 you gain a sharper, more formal register with connectors such as moreover, consequently, notwithstanding, whereas and nonetheless. Used precisely — and sparingly — they signal a writer in full command of academic tone.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
moreoveradd a stronger, further pointThe reform cut costs. Moreover, it raised morale.
consequentlystate a formal result or effectDemand collapsed; consequently, prices fell.
notwithstandingconcede "in spite of" (very formal)Notwithstanding its density, the report was clear.
whereasdraw a direct contrast between two ideasSpeech names people, whereas reports name processes.
nonethelesssay "even so", after a concessionThe style is dense; nonetheless, it stays readable.
howevercontrast between two sentencesNominalisation condenses. However, it can obscure.

See the difference — the same ideas, written two ways:

Verbal & chatty ✗Controlled C1 academic ✓
The city grew fast, so the roads got jammed. The council spent a lot on a metro, and after that things got better. The rapid growth of the city led to severe congestion. Heavy investment in a metro produced a marked reduction in traffic; consequently, air quality improved.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different C1 connectors from the table. Weave your explanation; don't list sentences.
  • Moreover, Consequently, However, Nonetheless usually open a sentence and take a comma: "Consequently, demand fell."

How to structure an academic explanation (Structure)

A strong C1 explanatory paragraph is dense but controlled. It names the phenomenon, condenses causes into nominalised subjects, states effects, and closes with a balanced judgement. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the shape:

PartWhat to writeGrammar to reach for
1. Framename the trend / phenomenon as an abstract nounnominalised subject (The growth of ...)
2. Causecondense the reasons into nominalised subjectsnoun + led to / resulted in + noun
3. Effectstate measurable resultsnoun + preposition (a reduction in ...)
4. Judgementweigh it up, staying impersonal and objectiveconcession connector + balanced claim

Sample Answer (~205 words) The rapid growth of remote work has produced a profound transformation in how organisations operate. Its adoption, accelerated by the pandemic, led to a sharp reduction in office attendance and a marked increase in reliance on digital tools. The dispersal of teams across cities and time zones brought clear benefits: a fall in commuting, a widening of the talent pool, and, for many, a genuine improvement in work-life balance.

Notwithstanding these gains, the shift is not without cost. The weakening of face-to-face contact has, for some, meant a decline in collaboration and a creeping sense of isolation. Whereas an office concentrates people and ideas in one place, remote work disperses them; consequently, the informal exchange of knowledge becomes harder to sustain.

The impact on management has been equally significant. Supervision by presence has given way to supervision by output, an adjustment many managers still resist.

Overall, the evidence remains mixed. Remote work has delivered flexibility and cut costs; nonetheless, its long-term effect on innovation and belonging is far from settled. The most sensible response, therefore, is not wholesale adoption but a careful, deliberate balance between presence and distance.
