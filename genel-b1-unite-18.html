Grammar: Phrasal Verbs

Study the rules, look at the examples, then do the exercises.

What is a phrasal verb?

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a small word called a particle (a preposition or an adverb, such as on, off, up, down, in, out, after, with). Together they often make a new meaning that you cannot always guess from the two words alone. For example, look means to use your eyes, but look after means "take care of", and look up means "search for information".

Phrasal verbs are everywhere in natural English, especially at work and at home. The hard part for B1 students is not the meaning but the grammar: does the verb take an object? Can you put a word between the verb and the particle? This unit answers exactly those questions.

  • Please turn on the computer.turn on = start a machine → new meaning.
  • She looks after her little brother after school.look after = take care of.
  • My car broke down on the motorway.break down = stop working (no object needed).

1. Transitive or intransitive? (with or without an object)

Some phrasal verbs need an object (the thing the action happens to) — these are transitive. Others stand alone with no object — these are intransitive. Using them the wrong way is a very common mistake.

TypeRuleExample
transitiveneeds an object (a thing/person)Can you turn off the TV?
intransitiveno object — the verb is completeThe engine broke down.
intransitiveno objectPlease sit down and carry on.
Key point
  • You cannot add an object to an intransitive phrasal verb: "The car broke down the road." ✗ means something totally different from "The car broke down on the road." ✓
  • A dictionary shows this with "(sth/sb)" for transitive verbs. Check when you learn a new one.

2. Separable phrasal verbs (you can split them)

Many transitive phrasal verbs are separable: the object can go after the particle, OR between the verb and the particle. Both are correct when the object is a full noun. This is the most flexible group.

Both correctStructureMeaning
turn on the light / turn the light onverb + particle + noun, OR verb + noun + particlestart the light
fill in the form / fill the form inboth orders are fine with a nouncomplete the form
put off the meeting / put the meeting offboth orders are fine with a nounpostpone the meeting
Key point
  • With a noun, you have a free choice of order: "Take off your shoes." = "Take your shoes off."
  • But with a pronoun (it, them, him...), the rule is strict — see the next card.

3. Where does the pronoun go? (turn it on, NOT turn on it)

This is the single most important rule in the unit. With a separable phrasal verb, a pronoun object (it, them, him, her, us) must go between the verb and the particle. It can never go after the particle.

Right ✓Wrong ✗Rule
Turn it on.Turn on it.pronoun goes in the middle.
Fill it in.Fill in it.pronoun goes in the middle.
Throw them away.Throw away them.pronoun goes in the middle.
The one rule to remember
  • Noun: free choice → "Turn on the radio" or "Turn the radio on." ✓
  • Pronoun: middle only → "Turn it on." ✓ — "Turn on it." ✗

4. Inseparable phrasal verbs (you cannot split them)

Some transitive phrasal verbs are inseparable: the object — a noun OR a pronoun — always stays after the particle. You can never put it in the middle. Many of these have two words after the verb (verb + preposition).

Phrasal verbRight ✓Wrong ✗
look after (= take care of)She looks after them.She looks them after.
deal with (= handle)I'll deal with it.I'll deal it with.
get on with (= have a good relationship)He gets on with her.He gets her on with.
How to tell them apart
  • There is no easy trick — you learn separable vs inseparable when you learn the verb. A good dictionary marks them.
  • As a rough guide, three-word phrasal verbs (get on with, look forward to, run out of) are almost always inseparable.

5. Everyday phrasal verbs (work & home)

Here is a core set of phrasal verbs you will hear every day. Learn each one with its meaning and whether it is separable (S) or inseparable (I).

Phrasal verbMeaningExample
set up (S)arrange / prepare / startThey set up a new office in Ankara.
sort out (S)solve or organiseCan you sort out this problem?
hand in (S)give to a person in chargePlease hand in your report today.
back up (S)make a safety copyAlways back up your files.
run out of (I)have none leftWe ran out of paper.
carry on (I)continueHe carried on working late.
Watch out
  • Some verbs change meaning with a different particle: turn on (start) vs turn down (refuse / reduce) vs turn up (arrive / increase).
  • Learn phrasal verbs in short example sentences, not as single words.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B1 students make most often with phrasal verbs. Almost all of them come from the wrong word order or from adding/dropping an object.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
Please turn on it.Please turn it on.a pronoun must go in the middle of a separable verb.
She looks them after every day.She looks after them every day."look after" is inseparable → object stays after it.
The bus broke down the trip.The bus broke down."break down" is intransitive → no object.
I will deal it with tomorrow.I will deal with it tomorrow."deal with" is inseparable → object stays after it.
We ran out paper.We ran out of paper."run out" needs "of" before the object.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two colleagues talk about a busy morning. Read it twice and notice how each phrasal verb works — where the pronoun goes, which verbs take an object, and which ones cannot be split.

  • — Morning! Can you turn on the printer? Actually, turn it on and print my report, please.turn on + noun; with a pronoun → turn it on (middle).
  • — Sure. Oh, we've run out of paper again.run out of = have none left (inseparable).
  • — Don't worry, I'll deal with it. I'll also sort out the email problem.deal with it (inseparable); sort out + noun.
  • — Great. Did you back up your files last night?back up + noun (separable).
  • — Yes, I backed them up before I left.pronoun in the middle → backed them up.
  • — Perfect. Let's carry on — we still have to hand in the plan by noon.carry on (intransitive); hand in + noun.
Test yourself
  • Why is it "turn it on" and not "turn on it"? (A pronoun must go between the verb and the particle.)
  • Rewrite "I backed them up" with a noun instead of "them". (I backed up my files / I backed my files up.)
  • Can you split "deal with"? (No — it is inseparable: "deal with it", never "deal it with".)

Speaking: Getting Things Done at Work & Home

Learn the phrasal verbs, then use them — with the right word order — to talk about your day.

turn on (phr v, S)
to start a machine, light or device (açmak, çalıştırmak)
Syn: switch on · Ant: turn off
"Turn on the laptop." / "Turn it on."
turn off (phr v, S)
to stop a machine, light or device (kapatmak)
Syn: switch off · Ant: turn on
"Please turn the TV off before bed."
set up (phr v, S)
to arrange, prepare or start something (kurmak, hazırlamak)
Syn: arrange · Ant: shut down
"We set up a new office last year."
fill in (phr v, S)

Syn: fill out · Ant: leave blank
"Fill in this form and sign it."
deal with (phr v, I)
to take action to solve or manage something (ilgilenmek, halletmek)
Syn: handle · Ant: ignore

look after (phr v, I)
to take care of a person or thing (bakmak, ilgilenmek)
Syn: take care of · Ant: neglect
"She looks after her little sister."
put off (phr v, S)
to move something to a later time (ertelemek)
Syn: postpone · Ant: bring forward
"They put off the meeting until Friday."
give up (phr v, S)
to stop trying to do something (pes etmek, bırakmak)
Syn: quit · Ant: carry on
"It was hard, but I did not give up."
carry on (phr v, I)
to continue doing something (devam etmek)
Syn: keep going · Ant: stop
"Carry on working; I'll be back soon."
sort out (phr v, S)
to solve a problem or organise something (çözmek, düzene sokmak)

hand in (phr v, S)

get on with (phr v, I)

"I get on with my new colleagues."
run out of (phr v, I)

"We ran out of paper this morning."

Reading: Leo's First Week at Work

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think about your first day in a new job or school. What did you have to set up or sort out?
  • The title is "Leo's First Week at Work". What problems do you expect a new worker to deal with?
  • This unit is about phrasal verbs. As you read, underline every phrasal verb you find, such as turn on, sort out, deal with and carry on.
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text for the answers. Notice how each phrasal verb is used — where its object goes.

Phrasal verbs to spot (from the Speaking section): turn on · set up · deal with · sort out · look after · back up · hand in · carry on

A first week at work: from a nervous first day to a confident new colleague.
Leo's First Week at Work

Leo was very nervous when he turned up for his first day at the marketing office. His new manager, Nadia, met him at the door and helped him settle in. "Don't worry," she said with a smile. "Everyone here will help you pick things up quickly, so just carry on and ask questions."

Leo had left college the year before, and he had been looking for a proper job for several months. He had sent off dozens of applications, and most companies had never even written back. When the marketing office finally rang him and offered him the job, he could hardly believe it. He had spent the whole weekend getting ready, laying out his clothes the night before and going over the bus times again and again. He did not want anything to go wrong on his very first morning.

On the day itself, Leo woke up early and set off long before he needed to. He got to the office half an hour ahead of time, so he waited in a small café across the road until it felt right to go in. The building was bigger than he had expected, with wide glass doors and a busy reception area. His heart was beating fast as he walked up to the desk and gave his name, but the friendly receptionist soon put him at ease and rang Nadia to come down.

Nadia took him on a short tour of the office and pointed out the meeting rooms, the kitchen and the fire exits. She introduced him to the rest of the team, who all looked up from their desks and said hello. There were about ten people in the department, and Leo tried hard to take in everyone's name at once. A cheerful colleague called Sam showed him where to hang up his coat and where the coffee machine was, and told him not to worry if he mixed up a few names during the first week.

Leo's first job was to set up his computer. He turned it on, but he could not log in, because he had forgotten his password. Nadia sorted it out in a minute and then showed him how to back up his files every evening, so that he would never lose his work again.

She also spent some time going through the main programs he would need. There was a system for keeping track of customers, another one for planning social media posts, and a shared calendar where the whole team wrote down their meetings. It was a lot to take in, and Leo made careful notes in a small notebook so that he could look things up later. Whenever he did not understand something, he remembered Nadia's advice and simply asked, and nobody seemed to mind at all.

In the afternoon, Leo had to fill in some forms and hand them in to the HR office. He also had to deal with a few emails from customers. One customer was angry because his order had not turned up on time, but Leo stayed calm, carried on politely, and promised to look into the problem straight away.

He read the message twice before he replied, so that he would not miss anything important. Then he went to find Sam, who explained how to check where the order was and what to say to the customer. Together they worked out that the parcel had been sent to the wrong address by mistake. Leo wrote back, said sorry for the delay, and arranged for a new order to be sent out the next day. A few hours later the customer replied with a short, friendly message to thank him, and Leo felt proud that he had turned a difficult moment into a good one.

The rest of the week carried on in much the same way. On Tuesday and Wednesday, Leo helped to write short posts for the company's social media pages and checked them carefully for mistakes. He was slow at first, but he was getting faster, and Nadia told him that his writing was clear and polite. On Thursday, he sat in on his first team meeting. He did not say very much, but he listened closely and wrote down the plans for the coming month, and by the end he felt like a real part of the group.

Each evening, before he switched off his computer, Leo went over what he had learned during the day. He wrote a short list of the things he wanted to remember and the questions he still wanted to ask. At home, he told his family about the office, the friendly team and the customer he had helped. They could all see how much happier he was than he had been during the long months when he was still looking for work. Little by little, the job that had once seemed so frightening was starting to feel like a place where he really belonged.

By Friday, Leo felt much better. He got on well with his new colleagues, and he no longer wanted to give up. When the office printer broke down, he did not panic. He simply asked a colleague for help and carried on with his other tasks. He even offered to look after the office plants while someone was away on holiday.

At the end of the afternoon, Nadia came over to his desk and told him he had made a really good start. She said the team had noticed how calm and polite he had been, especially with the angry customer. Sam invited him to join everyone for a quick coffee before they all went home, and Leo happily agreed. For the first time all week, he did not feel like the new person who did not belong; he felt like someone who could do the job well.

On the way home, Leo thought about his week. He had almost put off applying for this job because he was afraid of failing. Now, though, he was really glad he had not. "If you don't give up," he told himself quietly, "things usually pick up in the end."

Writing: A Typical Day at Work or Home

Write and submit your paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Phrasal verbs make writing natural (Phrasal Verbs)

Native speakers use phrasal verbs all the time, especially when they describe daily actions. Writing that uses only "big" single verbs (establish, complete, postpone) can sound stiff. This unit gives you a set of everyday phrasal verbs — use them, and remember the golden rule about where the object goes.

Phrasal verbUse it to...Example
turn on / turn offstart / stop a device (separable)I turn on my laptop and check my email.
set uparrange or prepare (separable)First I set up the meeting room.
deal withhandle a task or problem (inseparable)Then I deal with the phone calls.
sort outsolve or organise (separable)I sort out any problems quickly.
back upmake a safety copy (separable)Every night I back up my files.
carry oncontinue (intransitive)After lunch I carry on with my work.
run out ofhave none left (inseparable)Sometimes we run out of paper.

See the difference — the same day, written two ways:

Stiff, plain verbs ✗Natural, with phrasal verbs ✓
I start my computer. I complete the forms. I resolve the problems. I copy my files. I continue until five. I turn on my computer and fill in the forms. I sort out any problems, then I back up my files and carry on until five.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different phrasal verbs from this unit. Do not use only single verbs.
  • Remember: with a pronoun, split a separable verb → "turn it on", not "turn on it". Never split an inseparable one → "deal with it".

Paragraph structure (Paragraph Structure)

A "typical day" paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same every time — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningSay what your day is like in one general sentenceMy working day is busy, but I have a routine that keeps me organised.
2. BodyThe main things you do and any problems, using phrasal verbs (turn on, set up, deal with, sort out, back up).First I turn on my computer and set up my desk. Then I deal with emails and sort out any problems.
3. ClosingHow the day ends, or how you feel about itBefore I leave, I back up my files and carry on until everything is done.

Task (Görev)

Write about a typical day at work or at home and how you get things done. Describe your routine and any problems, using phrasal verbs.

WHAT TO INCLUDE

  • an opening sentence about what your day is generally like
  • the main things you do (set up, turn on, fill in, hand in)
  • a problem and how you handle it (deal with, sort out, run out of)
  • at least one separable verb with a pronoun in the middle (turn it on, back them up)
  • a closing sentence about how the day ends or how you feel
  • at least 3 phrasal verbs: turn on, turn off, set up, fill in, deal with, look after, sort out, hand in, back up, carry on, get on with, run out of
  • at least 1 inseparable phrasal verb used correctly (deal with it, look after them, get on with him)

EXAMPLE OPENERS

Sample Answer (~165 words) My working day is always busy, but I have a routine that keeps me organised. As soon as I get to the office, I turn on my computer and set up my desk for the day. Then I log in and deal with the emails that arrived overnight.

After that, I have to fill in a few reports and hand them in to my manager. If a customer has a problem, I try to sort it out straight away instead of putting it off. Some days are difficult; last week the printer broke down and we also ran out of paper, so I asked a colleague to help me.

I get on well with my colleagues, which makes everything easier. Before I leave, I always back up my files so that I never lose my work. Even when I am tired, I carry on until the important tasks are done. It is hard work, but I really enjoy it.
