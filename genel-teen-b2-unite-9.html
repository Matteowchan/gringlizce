Ünite 9 · Reporting Structures · Bölüm 1/6

Grammar: Reporting Structures

Report what "people say" without ever naming them — the language of news and rumour.

How to report what "everybody says"

Newspapers, gossip and formal writing all need a way to say "people say / people believe / people think..." without pointing at a single person. English does this with reporting structures — a special passive that lets you stay neutral and sound objective. Compare:

  • People say that the new teacher is really strict.Active, but "people" is vague and clumsy.
  • It is said that the new teacher is really strict.Impersonal report — no subject needed.
  • The new teacher is said to be really strict.Personal report — the same idea, more elegant.

Master both patterns and your English instantly sounds like a journalist: careful, formal and impossible to blame, because you never say who is doing the saying.

1. The two structures at a glance (quick review)

There are two ways to build a report from a verb like say, believe, think, report, know, expect, consider. Choose whichever fits your sentence.

StructurePatternExample
ImpersonalIt + is/was + V3 + that + clauseIt is believed that he is very talented.
PersonalSubject + is/was + V3 + to-infinitiveHe is believed to be very talented.
Key point
  • The impersonal form always starts with the empty subject it and keeps the word that: It is thought that...
  • The personal form takes the real subject and a to-infinitive — and it never uses "that": She is thought to be... ✓ / She is thought that she is... ✗

2. The impersonal report (It is said that...)

Start with It, add a passive reporting verb, then that + a normal clause. This is the safest, easiest pattern — the clause after "that" keeps its own tense.

Reporting verbImpersonal reportMeaning
sayIt is said that she has left.people say so
believeIt is believed that the exam will be harder.the general belief
reportIt is reported that ten people were hurt.news language
know / thinkIt is known that the site is ancient.accepted as fact
Golden rule
  • The verb inside the "that" clause does not change: It is said that he lives alone / It was said that he lived alone.
  • This pattern works with almost any reporting verb, which is why the news loves it.

3. The personal report (He is said to be...)

Take the real subject out of the "that" clause and put it at the front. The reporting verb stays passive, and the second verb becomes a to-infinitive.

  • It is said that he is very talented. → He is said to be very talented.The clause "is very talented" becomes "to be very talented".
  • It is believed that she speaks five languages. → She is believed to speak five languages."speaks" → "to speak".
  • It is thought that the plan works. → The plan is thought to work.A present fact stays as a simple to-infinitive.
Watch out — the classic B2 error
  • The personal structure has no "that": He is said to be rich. ✓ — He is said that he is rich. ✗
  • Do not forget the passive: He is said to be... ✓ — He said to be... ✗

4. Reporting the ongoing and the past (to be + -ing / to have + V3)

The little word after the subject can carry different times. Match the infinitive to the time of the original action.

Original ideaInfinitive formPersonal report
a present factto + verbHe is said to live in Paris.
happening now / around nowto be + -ingHe is thought to be living abroad.
a finished, earlier actionto have + V3He is said to have won a gold medal.
B2 subtlety — the time test
  • If the original is Present Simple ("he lives"), use the simple infinitive: is said to live.
  • If the original is continuous ("he is living"), use to be + -ing: is thought to be living.
  • If the original is past / present perfect ("he won / has won"), use to have + V3: is said to have won.

5. Present report vs past report (is said / was said)

The reporting verb itself can be present or past. It tells us when the report was made, not when the action happened.

Report timeExampleMeaning
now (current rumour)She is said to have left the country.people say so today
then (report in the past)She was said to have left the country.people said so at the time
past report, past actionThe ship was believed to have sunk.at that time, people believed it
Golden rule
  • Change the reporting verb (is → was) to move the whole report into the past; the to-infinitive after it keeps showing the earlier or ongoing action.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B2 students still make with reporting structures. Check your own writing against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
He is said that he is famous.He is said to be famous.the personal form never uses "that".
It is said him to be famous.It is said that he is famous.the impersonal form needs "that" + a clause.
She is believed to won the prize.She is believed to have won the prize.a past action needs "to have + V3".
They said to be very kind.They are said to be very kind.the reporting verb must be passive (are said).
He is thought to living abroad.He is thought to be living abroad.an ongoing action needs "to be + -ing".

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two friends share the latest school gossip. Read it twice and watch how the reporting structures let them repeat rumours without taking responsibility for a single one.

A:Have you heard? The head teacher is said to be leaving at the end of the year.Ongoing plan reported now → is said + to be + -ing.
B:Really? It is believed that she has a job at another school, isn't it?Impersonal report → It is believed that + clause.
A:Apparently. She is thought to have accepted it already.Earlier, finished action → to have + V3.
B:Last term the same thing was said about our maths teacher, and it was false.Past report → was said.
A:True. It is known that half of these stories never come true.Accepted fact → It is known that.
B:Exactly. Until it's official, she is only said to be thinking about it.A cautious present report → is said to be + -ing.
Test yourself
  • Rewrite "It is believed that she has a job at another school" as a personal report. (She is believed to have a job at another school.)
  • Why is "to have accepted", not "to accept", correct in line 3? (The accepting is already finished → to have + V3.)
  • Turn "was said about our maths teacher" into a present rumour. (is said about our maths teacher.)

Speaking: News, Rumours & Reputation

Learn the words of news and gossip, then report what "people say" in your own sentences.

rumour (n)
a story that people repeat although it is not confirmed (söylenti)
Syn: gossip · Ant: fact
"It is rumoured that our school trip will be cancelled, but nobody knows for sure."
allegedly (adv)
according to what people say, without proof (iddiaya göre)
Syn: reportedly · Ant: provably
"He allegedly changed schools, though he is thought to be still in our town."
reputation (n)
the opinion that people generally have of someone (itibar, ün)
Syn: standing · Ant: disgrace
"She is said to have an excellent reputation among her classmates."
credible (adj)
believable and able to be trusted (inandırıcı)
Syn: believable · Ant: unlikely
"His excuse for being late is not considered to be very credible."
sceptical (adj)
doubtful; not easily convinced that something is true (kuşkucu, şüpheci)
Syn: doubtful · Ant: convinced
"Many people are sceptical about what is reported online."
exaggerate (v)
to make something seem bigger or worse than it really is (abartmak)
Syn: overstate · Ant: understate
"The story is believed to have been exaggerated on social media."
speculation (n)
guessing about something without real evidence (tahmin, spekülasyon)
Syn: guesswork · Ant: certainty
"There is speculation that the two most popular students are said to be dating."
verify (v)
to check that something is really true (doğrulamak)
Syn: confirm · Ant: dismiss
"Journalists must verify a claim before it is reported as fact."
anonymous (adj)
with a name that is not known or not given (isimsiz, anonim)
Syn: nameless · Ant: named
"The information is thought to have come from an anonymous source."
controversy (n)
public disagreement or a heated argument (tartışma, ihtilaf)
Syn: dispute · Ant: agreement
"The new phone ban is said to have caused huge controversy at school."
eyewitness (n)
a person who personally saw an event happen (görgü tanığı)
Syn: observer · Ant: hearsay
"An eyewitness is reported to have seen the whole accident."
coverage (n)
the reporting of an event by newspapers, TV or online media (haber, kapsam)
Syn: reporting · Ant: silence
"The final match is said to have received worldwide coverage."
spokesperson (n)
someone who speaks officially on behalf of a group (sözcü)
Syn: representative · Ant: —
"A spokesperson is believed to have denied the rumours."

Reading: The Legend of Elias Ward

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Do you know a famous person whose life is full of rumours? What do people say about them?
  • The text is about a man surrounded by stories. Which reporting structures do you expect to see a lot?
  • The title is "The Legend of Elias Ward". Do you think the text will tell us the truth about him, or leave it a mystery? Guess!
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text. Notice each report — an impersonal one ("It is said that..."), a personal one ("he is believed to be...") — and ask why the writer never states it as plain fact.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): rumour · reputation · sceptical · anonymous · verify · speculation · eyewitness · controversy

A man who says nothing is soon surrounded by stories.
The Legend of Elias Ward

Few names spark as much curiosity as that of Elias Ward, the inventor who disappeared from public life more than twenty years ago. He is said to have built his first machine at the age of twelve; moreover, by thirty he was believed to be one of the most brilliant inventors in the country. Then, quite suddenly, he vanished.

For teenagers today, Ward is less a figure from a history lesson than a shared obsession. In classrooms and group chats across the country his name is passed around like a puzzle that nobody can quite solve. Teachers report that whenever a lesson touches on famous inventors, at least one student will raise a hand and ask what really happened to him. Short videos retelling his story are said to gather millions of views, and each new version adds a detail that the last one lacked. Although none of these accounts can be checked, they are repeated with total confidence, as if the person filming had once met Ward in person.

It is widely reported that Ward now lives alone in a house high in the mountains. Some claim he is still working; indeed, he is thought to be developing a device that could change the way we store energy. Other people, however, insist that the stories are pure invention.

Much of what young people believe about Ward has been shaped online rather than in libraries. It is often claimed on social media that he still sends coded messages to former students; moreover, some accounts insist that a blurry figure filmed near a mountain village last winter was the inventor himself. None of this has ever been proven. Nevertheless, the theories spread from one phone to the next within hours, and a rumour that begins as a joke on a Monday can be treated as established fact by Friday. Teachers who try to calm the excitement often find that a careful correction travels far more slowly than a dramatic guess.

What is known for certain is very little. Although it is said that he refuses all interviews, it is believed that only three people have seen him in the last decade. His former best friend was once thought to have quarrelled with him; nevertheless, she now says almost nothing. Since that time, the rumours have only grown.

At one secondary school the mystery even became the subject of a formal debate. Members of the debate club were asked to argue whether a person like Ward has the right to disappear completely, or whether the public is owed some kind of explanation. The team defending his silence argued that curiosity does not create an obligation; the opposing side replied that a figure so widely admired cannot simply switch off the world's interest. Since neither team could rely on confirmed facts, both were forced to reason from the little that is genuinely known. The judges later said that the contest had taught the students more about evidence and rumour than any textbook chapter could. Interest did not stop at the school gates, either: the recording of the debate was uploaded that evening and, within a week, students from other towns had left hundreds of comments, most of them adding fresh theories of their own rather than answering the actual question.

Journalists have not given up. Last year a reporter was said to have tracked him down; therefore, the whole country waited for proof. Yet no photograph ever appeared. The article was later shown to be false, whereas Ward's silence remained as complete as ever.

A group of students once decided to investigate the case for a class project rather than simply repeat what they had heard. They collected every article they could find, sorted the claims into 'proven', 'possible' and 'invented', and were surprised by how small the first column turned out to be. Whereas the internet offered them dozens of confident stories, the project left them with only a handful of facts they could actually verify. The exercise, their teacher noted, was really a lesson in how a legend is built: not from what a person does, but from what other people are willing to imagine on their behalf. Ward, it seemed, had become famous less for his inventions than for his absence. When the students presented their findings to the class, several classmates admitted that they had shared stories about him without ever pausing to ask where those stories had come from in the first place.

What makes the story so appealing to young people is perhaps that it mirrors their own experience of being online. They know how quickly a single post can be shared, exaggerated and misunderstood; they have watched a harmless photo turn into a scandal overnight, and a small mistake swell into a headline. In that sense Ward's legend is a warning as much as an entertainment. If an inventor with a genuine record of work can be buried under invented stories, then anyone with a phone can be reshaped by a crowd that has never actually met them. Learning to ask "who really confirmed this?" therefore matters far beyond the case of one missing engineer.

Ward himself has never confirmed or denied a single rumour. Perhaps that is the point. A man who says nothing is soon surrounded by stories, and each new tale is repeated until it is taken for fact. Whether Elias Ward is a genius or simply an ordinary man who wanted to be left alone, nobody can say for sure.

Writing: What People Say

Write and submit your paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound B2 (Linking Words)

At B1 you linked ideas with and, but, so, because. At B2 you sound noticeably more advanced when you use formal, precise connectors. Four of them are essential this unit — and two familiar ones, although and however, become sharper tools.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
moreoveradd a stronger, extra pointIt is rumoured he has left our school. Moreover, his locker is empty.
thereforeshow a logical resultNo one could verify it; therefore, the story was dropped.
whereascontrast two ideas directlyOne paper is sceptical, whereas another prints every rumour.
neverthelesssay "in spite of that"The claim was false. Nevertheless, people believed it.
althoughconcede a point inside one sentenceAlthough it is only speculation, the coverage is huge.
howevercontrast between two sentencesHe is said to be guilty. However, there is no proof.

See the difference — the same facts, written two ways:

Basic linking ✗Confident B2 writing ✓
There was a rumour. It was not true. People believed it. The news showed it. Then it stopped. A strong rumour appeared, although nobody could verify it. Nevertheless, it is said to have spread within hours; moreover, it received huge coverage. However, no eyewitness was ever found; therefore, the story quietly died.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different B2 connectors from the table. Do not write sentence by sentence; connect your ideas.
  • Moreover, Therefore, However, Nevertheless usually start a sentence and take a comma: "However, there is no proof."

Paragraph structure (Paragraph Structure)

A "what people say" paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningA topic sentence with an impersonal reportIt is widely said that our striker will leave this summer.
2. BodyReport the rumours. Use personal reports (is believed to be...), "to have + V3" for finished actions, and past reports (was said) for older stories. Link with B2 connectors.He is believed to be unhappy; moreover, a rival club is reported to have made an offer.
3. ClosingA final comment giving your own opinionNevertheless, until it is verified, I treat it as pure gossip.

Task (Görev)

Write about a famous person, event or piece of news that is surrounded by rumours or different opinions. Report what people say, believe and think — do not state everything as fact.

WHAT TO INCLUDE

  • an opening sentence with an impersonal report (It is said/reported that...)
  • at least two personal reports (He/She/They is/are believed to be...)
  • at least one "to have + V3" for a finished action (is said to have signed / left / won)
  • at least one past report (was said, was believed) for an older rumour
  • a closing sentence giving your own opinion (sceptical or convinced?)
  • at least 3 B2 connectors: moreover, therefore, whereas, nevertheless, although, however
  • at least 4 words from the Speaking section (rumour, sceptical, anonymous, verify, speculation, controversy, coverage, spokesperson)

EXAMPLE OPENERS

"It is widely said that ..." "Recently, a rumour has appeared that ..." "Although it is only speculation, ..."

Sample Answer (~190 words) Every few months, a new rumour is said to appear about my favourite football club, and this season has been no different. It is widely reported that our best striker will leave in the summer; moreover, several newspapers claim that a foreign club has already made an offer. He is believed to be unhappy with his contract, although the club has said almost nothing.

Personally, I am rather sceptical. The story first came from an anonymous source, and nobody has been able to verify it. A spokesperson is said to have denied everything; however, the speculation has only grown. Some fans exaggerate every detail, whereas others refuse to believe a single word.

I think the truth is simpler. The striker is thought to have asked for better wages, and the club is reported to be discussing a new deal. Therefore, the 'transfer' may never happen at all.

Nevertheless, the coverage continues, and each day a fresh headline appears. Until someone provides real evidence, I will treat the whole affair as nothing more than gossip.
