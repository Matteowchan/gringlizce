Grammar: Advanced Concession & Contrast

Move beyond "although". Concede a point with precision, and let the contrast do the persuading.

Concede first, then win the point

At B2 you handled contrast with although, but, however and despite. At C1 the goal is finer control: choosing the exact concessive form that fits the register, the emphasis and the structure of your sentence. Concession admits that something is true and then pushes against it; contrast sets two ideas side by side. A skilled writer uses these forms to sound measured, fair and in command — conceding a smaller point to strengthen a larger one.

This unit gathers the advanced machinery of concession into one toolkit: emphatic much as and for all; the -ever clauses (however, whatever, whoever, wherever); the formal markers granted, albeit, notwithstanding; and the fine distinctions of even if versus even though and despite the fact that. Each carries its own register and its own grammatical trap, and mastering them is a clear signature of C1 writing.

  • Much as I admire her energy, I cannot support the plan.Emphatic concession → "although I very much admire".
  • However carefully we plan, something always goes wrong.However + adverb + clause → "no matter how carefully".
  • For all its ambition, the reform changed very little.For all + noun → "despite all its ambition".
  • The results were encouraging, albeit preliminary.Albeit + short phrase (not a full clause).

1. Concessive clauses (even though / even if / while / whilst)

These conjunctions all introduce a full clause, but they mean different things. Even though concedes a real fact; even if concedes a hypothetical possibility. While / whilst can mean either "although" (concession) or "whereas" (contrast). Mixing them up changes the meaning, not just the tone.

FormWhat it signalsExample
even thougha real, accepted fact + clauseEven though he was exhausted, he finished the race.
even ifa hypothetical or unlikely conditionEven if it rains, the ceremony will go ahead.
while / whilstconcession ("although") or contrast ("whereas")Whilst I accept the cost, I still favour the plan.
Register & nuance
  • even though = it happened. even if = suppose it happens: "Even though it rained" (it did) vs "Even if it rains" (it may not). ✓
  • whilst is a more formal British variant of while; both fit academic prose, but avoid switching between them in one text.

2. Emphatic concession (much as & for all)

Two structures front the concession for emphasis. Much as + clause means "although I very much..." and works with verbs of feeling (admire, respect, sympathise, like). For all + noun phrase means "despite all", often with a note of irony that the thing counted for little. Both open a sentence and sound distinctly literary.

FormNuanceExample
much as + clausestrong concession with a feeling verbMuch as I'd love to help, my hands are tied.
for all + noun"despite all", often faintly ironicFor all his promises, nothing actually changed.
for all + nounconcedes a quality that still falls shortFor all her wealth, she was a lonely woman.
C1 subtlety
  • Much as takes a clause: "Much as I respect him..." ✓ — not "Much as his talent..." ✗ (use for all with a noun).
  • For all takes a noun phrase, never a clause: "For all his effort..." ✓ — not "For all he tried..." ✗

3. The -ever clauses (however / whatever / whoever / wherever)

These universal concessives mean "no matter how / what / who / where". The trickiest is however: when it means "no matter how", it is immediately followed by an adjective or adverb, then the subject and verb — with no inversion. Getting that word order right is a classic C1 marker.

FormPatternExample
however + adj/advhowever + adjective/adverb + subject + verbHowever hard he tried, he could not persuade them.
whatever / whichever"no matter what" + clauseWhatever you decide, we will respect it.
whoever / wherever"no matter who / where" + clauseWherever you go, the same rules apply.
C1 subtlety — word order
  • Keep the adjective/adverb next to however: "However difficult it is..." ✓ — not "However it is difficult..." ✗
  • No question inversion: "However hard he tried" ✓ — not "However hard did he try" ✗

4. Granting a point (granted (that) / no matter how / what)

Granted (that) concedes a point before you counter it — a favourite move in argument and debate. No matter how / what / who / where is a slightly more conversational equivalent of the -ever clauses. Both let you acknowledge the other side fairly before pressing your own case.

FormUse it to...Example
granted (that)concede a point you will then answerGranted, the cost is high; even so, the benefit is greater.
no matter how= however + adjective/adverbNo matter how loudly he argued, no one agreed.
no matter what= whatever + clauseNo matter what happens, I will finish the project.
Register & nuance
  • Granted can stand alone as a discourse marker: "Granted, it's risky. Nonetheless, it's worth trying."
  • No matter how follows the same word order as however: adjective/adverb next, no inversion.

5. Formal markers (albeit & notwithstanding)

These two are the most formal in the set. Albeit means "although it be" and attaches a short phrase (an adjective, adverb or reduced element), never a full clause. Notwithstanding means "in spite of" and takes a noun phrase; it can even follow the noun it governs. Both signal careful, academic writing.

FormPatternExample
albeit + phraseadds a short qualifying phrase, not a clauseThe offer was generous, albeit conditional.
albeit + adverbconcedes a manner brieflyShe agreed, albeit reluctantly.
notwithstanding + noun"in spite of" + noun phrase (very formal)Notwithstanding the risks, they pressed on.
C1 subtlety — the classic albeit trap
  • albeit takes a phrase, not a clause: "...albeit modest." ✓ — not "...albeit it was modest." ✗
  • notwithstanding takes a noun, not a clause: "Notwithstanding the delay..." ✓ — not "Notwithstanding he was late..." ✗

6. Despite & in spite of (+ the fact that)

Despite and in spite of mean the same thing and take a noun phrase or an -ing form — never a bare clause. To follow them with a clause, you must add the fact that. Two persistent errors here separate C1 from B2: writing "despite of" and following "despite" with a subject and verb.

FormPatternExample
despite / in spite of + noun+ noun phrase or -ingDespite the warning, they set off. / In spite of being tired...
despite the fact thatthe only way to add a full clauseDespite the fact that he was warned, he set off.
in spite of the fact thatformal, full-clause equivalentIn spite of the fact that the questions grew harder, our scores held firm.
C1 subtlety
  • There is no "despite of": it is either despite or in spite of. ✓
  • "Despite he was tired" ✗ → "Despite the fact that he was tired" or "Despite being tired". ✓

7. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the concession errors that still surface in otherwise strong C1 writing. Each is a structural slip — the wrong form after a connector. Audit your own writing against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
However it is difficult, we must try.However difficult it is, we must try.the adjective/adverb must follow "however" directly.
The plan worked, albeit it was slow.The plan worked, albeit slowly."albeit" takes a phrase, never a full clause.
Despite of the rain, we walked on.Despite the rain, we walked on.it is "despite" or "in spite of" — never "despite of".
For all he tried, he failed.For all his efforts, he failed."for all" takes a noun phrase, not a clause.
Much as his intelligence, he was unwise.Much as I respected him, I disagreed."much as" takes a clause with a feeling verb, not a noun.

8. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two classmates debate a science-fair project idea. Read it twice and watch the concessions work as a system: an emphatic much as, a however + adjective clause, a formal albeit, a granted that concedes before countering, and a notwithstanding that overrides the objection.

A:Much as I like your idea, I worry it will take too long.Emphatic concession before the objection.
B:Granted, our deadline is tight; even so, the results would be worth it.Grant the point, then push back.
A:But however carefully we plan, experiments always run late.However + adverb + subject + verb (no inversion).
B:True — the schedule is optimistic, albeit achievable if we focus.Albeit + short phrase, not a clause.
A:For all its risks, I suppose the project could work.For all + noun, conceding a quality.
B:Notwithstanding the doubts, then, let's pitch it to our teacher.Notwithstanding + noun overrides the objection.
Test yourself
  • Why "however carefully we plan" and not "however we plan carefully"? (The adverb must sit next to "however"; the subject and verb follow.)
  • Rewrite "albeit achievable" as a full clause. Which is more C1? ("albeit it is achievable" is wrong; the reduced phrase is the correct, more elegant form.)
  • Could "For all its risks" become "For all it is risky"? (No — "for all" needs a noun; use "Much as it is risky" for a clause.)

Speaking: Arguing Fairly

Learn the language of debate and concession, then use the unit's structures to argue a balanced case.

concede (v)
to admit something is true, often reluctantly (kabul etmek, teslim etmek)
Syn: acknowledge · Ant: deny
"I concede that the costs are real, but the gains outweigh them."
refute (v)
to prove that a claim is wrong (çürütmek)
Syn: disprove · Ant: confirm
"She calmly refuted every objection with hard evidence."
reconcile (v)
to make two opposing ideas fit together (bağdaştırmak, uzlaştırmak)
Syn: harmonise · Ant: oppose
"It is hard to reconcile his words with his actions."
qualify (v)
to limit or soften a statement (kaydını koymak, sınırlandırmak)
Syn: temper · Ant: assert
"He qualified his praise by noting a few weaknesses."
counter (v)
to respond against an argument (karşı çıkmak, cevap vermek)
Syn: rebut · Ant: concur
"Critics countered that the data was out of date."
caveat (n)
a warning or condition attached to a claim (uyarı, ihtirazi kayıt)
Syn: proviso · Ant: guarantee
"I support the plan, with one important caveat."
reservation (n)
a doubt that stops full agreement (çekince)
Syn: misgiving · Ant: endorsement
"Despite my reservations, I voted in favour."
paradox (n)
a statement that seems contradictory yet may be true (paradoks, çelişki)
Syn: contradiction · Ant: truism
"It is a paradox that to lead you must first listen."
nuance (n)
a subtle difference in meaning or feeling (ince ayrım, nüans)
Syn: subtlety · Ant: crudeness
"A good debater is alert to every nuance of the question."
ambivalent (adj)
having mixed or conflicting feelings (kararsız, iki arada)
Syn: conflicted · Ant: certain
"She felt ambivalent, drawn to and wary of the offer at once."
compelling (adj)
convincing and hard to resist (inandırıcı, karşı konulmaz)
Syn: persuasive · Ant: flimsy
"He made a compelling case for reform."
tenuous (adj)
weak, thin or poorly supported (zayıf, çürük)
Syn: flimsy · Ant: robust
"The link between the two events is tenuous at best."
disparate (adj)
very different and hard to compare (birbirinden farklı, ayrışık)
Syn: divergent · Ant: uniform
"She wove disparate viewpoints into one clear argument."
A fair argument weighs what to concede against what to hold: the concession you make lends weight to the point you keep.

Reading: The Quiet Power of Conceding a Point

Read the feature, then work through the five tasks.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Skilled readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think of an argument you lost. Did the other person concede any of your points — and did that make them more or less convincing?
  • The text argues that admitting a weakness can be a strength. What kind of concession words might a writer use to signal fairness?
  • Watch for the "paradox" the writer builds towards. Can you predict what it will be before you reach the last line?
How to read
  • First read: read quickly for the whole argument — why conceding can help, and where its limits lie. Don't stop for every word.
  • Second read: track the concession markers — much as, granted, albeit, notwithstanding, however, for all — and ask what each one concedes and what it defends.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): concede · refute · caveat · reservation · paradox · compelling · nuance · disparate

The Quiet Power of Conceding a Point

In debate, the instinct is to defend every inch of ground. Skilled arguers, however, do the opposite. Much as it feels like weakness, conceding a minor point often strengthens the case that follows. Granted, an opponent may seize on the admission; yet a well-placed concession signals confidence and fairness, and it disarms hostility before it hardens into a compelling counter-attack.

Anyone who has sat through a heated classroom debate will recognise the pattern. Two students, each convinced of being right, talk past one another, and the more they dig in, the further apart they drift. Teachers who coach debate teams notice this early: the novice treats every objection as an attack to be repelled, whereas the seasoned member of the club learns to listen for the grain of truth in a rival's claim. That grain, once acknowledged, becomes a bridge rather than a battlefield, and the tone of the whole exchange begins to shift.

Take the sort of motion that surfaces in a school debating society, whether phones ought to be banned during lessons or whether homework does more harm than good. A student determined merely to score points will dismiss every statistic the opposing bench raises. Her rival, by contrast, might begin by admitting that constant notifications genuinely fragment concentration, and only then argue that a blanket ban ignores the legitimate uses of technology in learning. By granting the obvious, she earns the right to be heard on the contested. The audience, most of them classmates, warms to the speaker who seems to be reasoning rather than merely reciting a script.

Consider a debater who admits, albeit reluctantly, that the other side has a fair point. Whereas a stubborn rival invites resistance, the debater who concedes wins trust. Moreover, by granting the smaller claim, she clears the path to the larger one. Notwithstanding the short-term cost, the long-term gain is considerable; a single reservation, honestly voiced, can carry more weight than a dozen denials.

The same dynamic plays out far from the debating hall. In a group project, where four or five teenagers must agree on a single plan, the member who insists that only her idea has merit quickly finds herself isolated. The one who says that a classmate's approach to the presentation is stronger, and that they should use it while her own research shapes the argument, tends to steer the group without ever raising her voice. Conceding the layout costs her nothing; holding firm on the substance costs her everything, and she has learned to tell the two apart.

Even in written work the principle holds. English teachers routinely reward the essay that anticipates the counter-argument and answers it, rather than the one that pretends no opposing view exists. A paragraph that opens by acknowledging a reasonable objection, then explains why the main thesis survives it, reads as far more mature than a wall of unqualified assertions. Examiners, like audiences, can tell the difference between a writer who has genuinely weighed the evidence and one who is simply shouting on paper.

For all its usefulness, concession has limits. However tempting it is to agree, one must never surrender the central argument. A speaker who concedes everything persuades no one, and a concession built on tenuous reasoning is quickly refuted. The art lies in the balance: yield the trivial, hold the essential. Since the audience senses honesty, a measured concession lands as strength, not surrender.

None of this means a student should fold at the first sign of pressure. There is a world of difference between a thoughtful concession and a nervous retreat. A debater who abandons her case the moment a confident opponent frowns has not conceded wisely; she has merely lost her nerve. The skill worth practising, and it does take genuine practice, is knowing in advance which points are negotiable and which lie at the very heart of the argument. Draw that map before the debate begins, and each concession becomes a deliberate move rather than an accident of panic.

Consider, too, the student who stands for a place on the school council. The candidate who promises everything to everyone convinces no one, while the candidate who openly admits that the budget cannot stretch to new lockers, longer breaks and a revamped canteen all at once sounds refreshingly honest. Voters, even young ones, have a keen ear for the difference between a realistic plan and an empty wish list. A single frank admission about limits can win more support than a stack of glossy promises.

Why does conceding feel so uncomfortable in the first place? Partly because, as teenagers especially, we tend to tie our arguments to our sense of self, so that losing a point can feel like losing face in front of people whose opinion we care about. Yet the discomfort is misleading. The classmate who can say, without flinching, that a rival is right about something usually commands more respect, not less. Confidence, it turns out, is not the refusal ever to be wrong, but the willingness to admit a small mistake while calmly defending the larger truth.

Learning this takes time, and the debating hall is a forgiving place to practise. Lose a round because you conceded too much and you will remember it; win a round because a single fair admission disarmed a hostile audience and the lesson sticks even faster. Over a term or two, the reflex to defend every syllable gives way to something more supple, and the student begins to sense, almost instinctively, when to yield and when to stand.

Consider how differently two speakers handle the same challenge. The first denies every objection and, consequently, sounds defensive and brittle. The second weaves disparate points into one fair case; nonetheless, no one mistakes her flexibility for weakness. She has understood the paradox at the heart of persuasion: to win the whole argument, you must sometimes lose a part of it.

Writing: A Balanced Discursive Essay

Write and submit a fair, two-sided argument; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound C1 (Linking Words)

At B2 you linked ideas with however, although, but, despite. At C1 you gain a sharper, more formal register with connectors such as notwithstanding, whereas, nonetheless, moreover and consequently. Used precisely — and sparingly — they signal a writer in full command of a balanced argument.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
notwithstandingconcede "in spite of" (very formal) + nounNotwithstanding the risks, the reform proceeded.
whereasdraw a direct contrast between two ideasCritics saw danger, whereas supporters saw progress.
nonethelesssay "even so", after a concessionThe cost was real; nonetheless, the plan went ahead.
moreoveradd a stronger, further pointThe idea was bold. Moreover, it was affordable.
consequentlystate a formal result or effectSupport collapsed; consequently, the bill failed.
howevercontrast between two sentencesThe plan seemed sound. However, doubts remained.

See the difference — the same idea, argued two ways:

Flat, one-sided ✗Balanced C1 concession ✓
Social media is bad. It wastes time. It makes people unhappy. We should all delete it. Much as social media connects us, its costs are real. Granted, it can waste time; nonetheless, used wisely it informs and unites. Notwithstanding the risks, then, the answer is discipline, not deletion.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different C1 connectors from the table. Weave a balanced argument; don't list opinions.
  • Moreover, Consequently, However, Nonetheless usually open a sentence and take a comma: "Nonetheless, the plan succeeded."

How to structure a discursive essay (Essay Structure)

A strong C1 discursive essay does not merely list one side. It concedes the opposing view fairly, then argues past it. The skeleton stays the same — you change the topic, not the shape:

PartWhat to writeStructures to reach for
1. Introductionframe the debate and state your positionwhereas / while + contrast of the two views
2. Concessiongrant the opposing side's strongest pointmuch as / granted (that) / for all + noun
3. Counter-argumentpush back and defend your casehowever + adj/adv / nonetheless / albeit
4. Conclusionweigh both and restate your viewnotwithstanding + noun / on balance

Sample Answer (~205 words) Whereas some celebrate technology as a liberating force, others fear it is quietly dulling our capacity to think. The truth, I would argue, lies in a careful balance rather than either extreme.

Much as I value the convenience of instant answers, I concede that constant reliance can erode real reasoning. Granted, few of us now memorise facts or navigate without a screen; consequently, a genuine skill may be fading. This is a compelling caveat, and it would be dishonest to refute it outright.

However persuasive that fear is, it tells only half the story. Notwithstanding our dependence, technology also hands us tools that sharpen thought: it exposes us to disparate viewpoints and lets us test ideas at speed. Even though the machine can calculate, it is we who must ask the questions that matter, albeit with more discipline than before.

The danger, then, is not the tool but the passive habit. On balance, and despite my reservations, I do not believe technology has made us unable to think; rather, it has made independent thought a choice. The paradox is plain: the very devices that could weaken our minds can, used deliberately, strengthen them instead.
