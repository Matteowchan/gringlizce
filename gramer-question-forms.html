Question FormsSoru Yapıları

İngilizcede soru, yardımcı fiil (do/does/did, be, have, modal) ile özne yer değiştirerek kurulur. Wh- soruları (what, where, when, why, how) bilgi ister. Türkçede soru eki (-mi) ile yapıldığından, İngilizcedeki yardımcı-fiil devrikliği Türk öğrenciyi zorlar. Özne sorusu (Who came?) istisnası kritiktir.

In English a question is formed by swapping the subject with an auxiliary verb (do/does/did, be, have, modal). Wh- questions (what, where, when, why, how) ask for information. Because Turkish forms questions with a question particle (-mi), the auxiliary-verb inversion of English challenges Turkish learners. The subject question (Who came?) exception is critical.

İngilizcede soru kurmanın kalbi tek bir mekanizmadır: yardımcı fiili öznenin önüne al. Türkçede cümlenin sonuna bir -mi eki koyarak soru yaparız; İngilizcede ise kelime sırasını değiştiririz. Bu bölümde iki büyük soru ailesini (Evet/Hayır soruları ve Wh- soruları), her zamandaki yardımcı fiil seçimini, kısa cevapları ve Türk öğrencinin en sık düştüğü tuzakları baştan sona işliyoruz. Özne sorusu istisnası (Who came?) ile soru kelimesinin doğrudan özne olduğu özel durumu da netleştiriyoruz. Konuyu tamamladıktan sonra Soru Ekleri (soru ekleri) ve Geniş Zaman konularıyla bağ kurabilirsin.

The heart of forming questions in English is a single mechanism: move the auxiliary in front of the subject. In Turkish we make a question by adding a -mi particle to the end of the sentence; in English we change the word order. In this section we cover, from start to finish, the two big question families (Yes/No questions and Wh- questions), the choice of auxiliary in each tense, short answers, and the traps Turkish learners fall into most. We also clarify the subject-question exception (Who came?) where the question word is directly the subject. Asking questions is one of the most basic and most frequently used functions of a language. Learning an address, asking someone's name, wondering about a price, having an interview, understanding a text in an exam — all of these rely on question structures. The logic of forming questions in English follows a path completely different from Turkish, so this topic is both very necessary and, at first, confusing for the Turkish learner.

Making a question in Turkish is very easy: you build the sentence, add a question particle (-mı/-mi/-mu/-mü) to the end, and raise your intonation. Sen çay seversin. becomes Sen çay sever misin? . The positions of the words barely change; only a small particle is added. In English, however, there is no suchquestion particle. Instead, we change the order of the words.

TURKISH

A question particle (-mı/-mi/-mu/-mü) is added to the end of the sentence and the intonation rises. The word order barely changes: Sen çay seversin → Sen çay sever misin?

ENGLISH

There is no question particle; instead the auxiliary verb is moved in front of the subject and the word order changes: You like tea → Do you like tea?

English's solution: bring the auxiliary to the front

When forming a question, English moves the auxiliary verb in the sentence in front of the subject. An auxiliary is a 'functional' verb that accompanies the main verb of the sentence: and the like. In linguistics this swap is called inversion (inversion) .

The Turkish learner's first big difference
In Turkish, question = sentence + a small particle. In English, question = changing the word order. That's why You are coming tomorrow? — trying to make a plain sentence like this into a question with intonation alone is not considered formal or correct in English; in a real question the auxiliary must come to the front: Are you coming tomorrow?

The two big question families

  • Yes/No questions: Questions whose answer is 'yes' or 'no'. Do you speak English? — Yes, I do. / No, I don't.
  • Wh- questions: Questions that ask for information; they begin with what, where, when, why, who, which, whose, how. Where do you live? — In Ankara.

This topic teaches us a basic truth about English sentence structure: English usually does its grammatical 'work' through auxiliary verbs . Making questions, making negatives (don't), giving emphasis (I DO like it) and giving short answers (Yes, I do) all rely on the same auxiliary-verb system.

"In other words, understanding this topic well means understanding the entire skeleton of English.
Why is this topic hard for the Turkish learner?
Three reasons: (1) Turkish has no auxiliary-verb inversion, so this mechanism is entirely new. (2) When there is no visible auxiliary, 'do/does/did' must be invented out of nothing — there is nothing like this in Turkish. (3) In subject questions like 'Who came?' inversion, contrary to this rule, is not done; this exception is counterintuitive.

  • In English a question is formed by moving the auxiliary in front of the subject (inversion). There is no equivalent of the Turkish -mi particle.
  • If the sentence already has an auxiliary (be, modal, have), you bring it to the front: Is she here? Can you help? Have you finished?
  • If the sentence has no visible auxiliary (Present/Past Simple), you add the do/does/did auxiliary: Do you know? Does he work? Did they leave?
  • does or did when you use it, the main verb stays in the base form : Does she like (not likes), Did you go (not went).
  • Yes/No questions begin with an auxiliary; the answer is yes/no. Short answer: Yes, I do. / No, I don't.
  • Wh- questions begin with a question word (what, where, when, why, who, how) and ask for information.
  • The pattern of a Wh- question: Wh- + auxiliary + subject + main verb? — Where do you live?
  • Subject-question exception: If the question word is the subject of the sentence (Who? What?), do/does/did are NOT used and there is no inversion: Who called you?
  • In spoken language the preposition often goes to the end : What are you looking at? Where are you from?
  • In polite/indirect questions the pattern changes and inversion disappears: Could you tell me where the station is? (not ...where is the station).

There is a single golden rule for forming questions, and everything rests on it: move the auxiliary in front of the subject. The only remaining question is: 'Does the sentence already have an auxiliary, or do I need to bring in do/does/did?' Let's look at the three basic cases below.

Case 1 — There is an auxiliary

If the sentence already has be, a modal or have , bring it in front of the subject. She is ready → Is she ready?

Case 2 — No auxiliary

If there is no visible auxiliary, do/does/did steps in, and the main verb stays in the base form. You work → Do you work?

Case 3 — Wh- question

Put the question word at the very front, then apply the same inversion rule. Where do you live?

Case 1: The sentence already has an auxiliary verb (be / modal / have)

If the sentence already has am/is/are/was/were, a modal (can, will, should, must...) or have/has (perfect), there is no need to add anything extra. You bring the existing auxiliary to the front. Formula: Auxiliary + subject + (main verb) + ...?

  • Turkish: The question word (who, what, where) can stand in different places in the sentence. Sen dün kimi gördün?

Although the general rule is 'bring the auxiliary to the front', there are a few important special cases. Knowing them makes the difference between natural and merely correct English.

CAUTION

When the question word is the subject of the sentence, do/does/did are NOT used and there is no inversion: Who came? we say, Who did come? not. This is exactly the trap Turkish learners most often fall into.

1. The subject question: the most critical exception

When the question word (Who, What, Which) is the subject of the sentence, do/does/did are NOT used and there is no inversion. The sentence stays in plain order; only the subject is replaced by the question word. This is the most important question exception in English and is completely counterintuitive for the Turkish learner.

Who broke it? → the person who broke it (subject) → no do. What did you break? → the thing that was broken (object) → did present. Rule: 'If the question word is the doer, drop the do.'

When the main verb is 'be' (am/is/are/was/were), no extra auxiliary is needed for the question. 'be' acts as both the main verb and its own auxiliary, and comes to the front directly.

In academic and formal writing questions are used less, because academic text 'presents' information, it doesn't 'ask for' it. However, questions appear in academic writing with special functions: stating a research question, asking a rhetorical question, and setting a polite/objective tone with the indirect-question structure.

A rhetorical question is one asked to make the reader think, with no answer expected. In academic writing it should be used sparingly and appropriately; too much sounds informal.

The questions below check not rote rules but the meaning and the logic of the structure. Think about each yourself first, then reveal the answer.

concept check Think about the meaning and structure; explain briefly.
  1. What is the difference in meaning between 'Who called you?' and 'Who did you call?'
    In the first, 'Who' is the subject: it asks about the person who CALLED you (that person is the doer, not you). In the second, 'Who' is the object: it asks about the person you CALLED (you are the doer). That's why the first has no 'did' and the second does.
  2. Why is 'Does she likes it?' wrong?
    'does' already carries the 3rd person singular -s information. Marking the -s twice (both does and likes) is wrong; the main verb must stay base: 'Does she like it?'
  3. Why is 'Do you can help?' wrong? What is special about modal verbs?
    'can' is already an auxiliary verb (a modal). It comes to the front itself; a second auxiliary (do) does not come before it. The correct form is: 'Can you help?'
  4. Why is 'Could you tell me where is the station?' wrong?
    In a polite/indirect question inversion disappears; the embedded part stays in plain-sentence order: 'where the station is.' The inverted 'where is' is wrong here.
  5. Does 'Why don't we go out tonight?' really ask for a reason?
    No. It is a suggestion pattern: it means 'Let's go out tonight'. Context makes it clear that this is a suggestion, not a reason question.

  11. ____ did you go with?
    Doğru: Who
    Günlük İngilizcede edat (with) sona gider: 'Who did you go with?' Doğal ve doğru olan budur.

  1. ____ you speak French?
    Correct: Do
    Present Simple, subject 'you' → auxiliary 'do'. The main verb 'speak' stays base.
  2. ____ she live near here?
    Correct: Does
    Present Simple, 3rd person singular subject 'she' → 'does'. The verb 'live' is base (not lives).
  3. ____ you go to the party last night?
    Correct: Did
    'last night' is past tense → 'did'. The verb 'go' is base (not went).
  4. Which sentence is correct?
    Correct: Where are you going?
    A Present Continuous Wh- question; be (are) comes before the subject, -ing is preserved.
  5. ____ broke the window? (we are asking who broke the window)
    Correct: Who
    Subject question: because 'Who' is the subject, do/did are NOT used; the verb 'broke' is directly conjugated.
  6. ____ you help me, please? (most polite request)
    Correct: Could
    'Could' is more polite than 'can' for a request; being a modal, it comes to the front directly.
  7. Choose the correct question: 'Bu kelime ne demek?'
    Correct: What does this word mean?
    A Present Simple question: 'does' + subject + base verb (mean). Correct order: What does this word mean?
  8. ____ she can dance?
    Correct: Can
    With the modal 'can', no other auxiliary is added; 'can' comes to the front directly: Can she dance?
  9. ____ have you known each other?
    Correct: How long
    'How long' asks about duration; the right pattern for how long you've known each other. Used with the Present Perfect.
  10. Which is the correct indirect question?
    Correct: Do you know where the bank is?
    In an indirect question inversion disappears: 'where the bank is' (plain order), not 'where is'.
  11. ____ did you go with?
    Correct: Who
    In everyday English the preposition (with) goes to the end: 'Who did you go with?' This is the natural and correct one.
  12. ____ the children playing in the garden?
    Correct: Are
    Present Continuous ('playing' -ing) + plural subject 'children' → the auxiliary be 'are'.

Nereden başlamalı?
Bu konuya be fiiliyle başla (Are you...?), çünkü tek adımlıdır: sadece be'yi öne al, ekstra yardımcı yok. Öğrenci bu mekanizmayı (devriklik) be üzerinde otomatikleştirdikten sonra, 'görünür yardımcı yoksa do/does/did getir' adımını ekle. do/does/did'i baştan verirsen öğrenci ezber yapar ama mantığı kavramaz.

En büyük direnç noktası, Türkçedeki 'soru = küçük ek' refleksinin İngilizcedeki 'soru = yapısal devriklik' gerçeğiyle çatışmasıdır. Öğrenciler sürekli düz cümleyi soru tonuyla söylemeye kayar (You like it?). Bunu düzeltmenin en etkili yolu, hataya işaret etmeden 'Yardımcı fiilin nerede?' diye sormaktır; öğrenci çoğu zaman kendi kendini düzeltir.

Kavram kontrol soruları (kural sormadan)
'Does she likes it?' cümlesini tahtaya yaz ve sor: '-s işareti bu cümlede kaç yerde var? Kaç yerde OLMALI?' Öğrencinin '-s bilgisini does zaten taşıyor' fikrini kendi bulmasını sağla. Özne sorusu için: 'Who broke it?' ve 'What did you break?' yan yana yaz, 'Hangisinde kıran kişiyi, hangisinde kırılan şeyi soruyoruz?' diye sor.
Etkinlik önerileri
(1) Soru zinciri: Her öğrenci bir öncekine bir soru sorar, sıra halka boyunca döner; yardımcı fiil hatası yapan elenir. (2) Röportaj: Öğrenciler ünlü/hayali bir kişiyle mülakat için 8 soru hazırlar, sonra rol yapar. (3) Özne/nesne ayrımı kartları: 'Who...?' başlayan kartları öğrenciler 'özne sorusu' ve 'nesne sorusu' diye ikiye ayırır; hızlı ve etkili.
Akılda Tutma: 'ASI' formülü
Soru kurarken sırayı hatırlamak için: A-S-I → Auxiliary (yardımcı fiil) + Subject (özne) + Infinitive/main verb (ana fiil). 'ASI' zincirini kur, başına gerekirse Wh- kelimesini ekle: (Wh) + A + S + I = Where + do + you + live. Bu dört kutulu şablon neredeyse tüm soruları üretir.
Where to start?
Start this topic with the verb be (Are you...?), because it is one step: just move be to the front, no extra auxiliary. After the student automates this mechanism (inversion) on be, add the step 'if there's no visible auxiliary, bring in do/does/did'. If you give do/does/did from the start, the student memorises but doesn't grasp the logic.

The biggest point of resistance is the clash between the Turkish 'question = a small particle' reflex and the English reality of 'question = structural inversion'. Students constantly slip into saying the plain sentence with question intonation (You like it?). The most effective way to fix this is to ask, without pointing out the error, 'Where is your auxiliary?'; the student usually self-corrects.

Concept-check questions (without asking for the rule)
Write 'Does she likes it?' on the board and ask: 'How many places is the -s in this sentence? How many places SHOULD it be?' Let the student discover for themselves the idea that 'does already carries the -s information'. For the subject question: write 'Who broke it?' and 'What did you break?' side by side and ask, 'In which are we asking about the person who broke it, and in which about the thing that was broken?'
Activity suggestions
(1) Question chain: Each student asks the previous one a question, and the turn goes around the circle; whoever makes an auxiliary-verb mistake is eliminated. (2) Interview: Students prepare 8 questions to interview a famous/imaginary person, then role-play. (3) Subject/object distinction cards: Students sort cards beginning with 'Who...?' into 'subject question' and 'object question'; quick and effective.
Memory Aid: the 'ASI' formula
To remember the order when forming a question: A-S-I → Auxiliary + Subject + Infinitive/main verb. Build the 'ASI' chain, and add a Wh- word to the front if needed: (Wh) + A + S + I = Where + do + you + live. This four-box template produces almost all questions.

  • In English, question = moving the auxiliary in front of the subject (inversion). There is no equivalent of the Turkish '-mi' particle.
  • If the sentence already has an auxiliary (be, modal, have), bring it to the front: Is she..? Can you..? Have you..?
  • If there is no visible auxiliary (Present/Past Simple), bring in do/does/did : Do you..? Does she..? Did they..?
  • does/did when you use them, the main verb in the base form : Does she like (not likes), Did you go (not went).
  • The two big families: Yes/No questions (begins with an auxiliary) and Wh- questions (begins with a question word, asks for information).
  • Wh- pattern: Wh- + auxiliary + subject + main verb (the ASI formula). The question word is almost always at the very front.
  • Subject-question exception: If the question word is itself the subject (Who? What?), do/does/did are NOT used: Who broke it?
  • For a polite request use could/would; in everyday speech the preposition often goes to the end shifts: What are you looking at?
  • In polite/indirect questions (Do you know where..?) inversion disappears, the sentence returns to plain order.
  • Short answers repeat the auxiliary from the question: Yes, I do. / No, she isn't.
