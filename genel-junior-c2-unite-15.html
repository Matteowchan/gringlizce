Grammar: Rhetoric & Persuasion

At C2, syntax stops being merely correct and starts being moving. This unit hands you the ancient machinery of persuasion — and teaches you to run it without burning it out.

Rhetoric is structure with a purpose

Rhetoric is not a bag of tricks; it is the deliberate arrangement of language to persuade. Every device in this unit is really a pattern of structure — a way of repeating, balancing or ordering clauses so that meaning lands with force. You have met most of the raw grammar before; what is new at C2 is bending that grammar consciously toward an effect on a listener.

The great persuasive figures all share one root: parallelism — matching grammatical shapes so the ear hears a pattern. From parallelism grow the tricolon (three matched parts), anaphora (repeated openings), antithesis (balanced opposites) and climax (a rising order). Add the rhetorical question, the honest concession-refutation and the unifying inclusive "we", and you hold the whole toolkit. The C2 skill is not merely using these — it is weaving them into a real argument, in the right register, without tipping into empty noise.

  • We came, we saw, we conquered.Tricolon — three matched clauses, rising in force. The rhythm persuades before the sense does.
  • Ask not what your country can do for you; ask what you can do for your country.Antithesis — the same words rebalanced into a mirror; the contrast carves a line you cannot unsee.
  • We shall not falter, we shall not yield, we shall not fail.Anaphora + tricolon — the repeated opening hammers the promise home.
  • Is this really the future we want to leave our children?Rhetorical question + inclusive "we" — no answer expected; it pulls the reader onto your side.

1. Parallelism & the tricolon (matched structure, in threes)

Parallelism means giving coordinated ideas the same grammatical form: verb matches verb, noun matches noun, clause matches clause. When three parallel elements are grouped, you have a tricolon — the single most persuasive rhythm in English, because the ear finds three both complete and memorable.

DeviceHow it is builtExample
parallelismrepeat the same grammatical shape across itemsa government of the people, by the people, for the people
tricolonthree parallel elements, often risingYour freedom, your future, your choice.
tricolon crescenseach of the three longer than the lastto live, to work, to build a life worth living
C2 subtlety
  • The parts must match at the joints: "reading, swimming and to cycle" breaks the pattern — make it "reading, swimming and cycling".
  • Three is a sweet spot: two feels thin, four feels like a list. Reach for the tricolon at moments you want to land.

2. Antithesis (not X, but Y)

Antithesis sets two opposing ideas in a balanced, parallel frame so the contrast rings out. The classic shape is "not X, but Y", but any mirrored pair works. Because the structure is symmetrical, the opposition feels almost inevitable — the reader accepts your line before questioning it.

ShapeExampleWhy it persuades
not X, but YNot the size of the dog in the fight, but the size of the fight in the dog.the mirror makes the second half feel like the truth.
A, whereas BThe rich were pardoned, whereas the poor were punished.balanced clauses expose the injustice sharply.
we do X, they do YWhere they see a cost, we see a promise.contrast plus inclusive "we" draws a clean battle line.
C2 subtlety
  • Keep both halves grammatically parallel, or the contrast blurs: "not the money, but I want respect" should be "not the money, but the respect".
  • Antithesis is concentrated: one sharp pair lands; ten in a row exhaust the reader.

3. Anaphora, climax & the rhetorical question (repetition, rise, and the unanswered ask)

Three more engines of persuasion. Anaphora repeats the opening words of successive clauses, building momentum like a drumbeat. Climax orders items so each is stronger than the last, driving to a peak. The rhetorical question asks something whose answer is already obvious, pulling the reader into agreement without a single fact.

DeviceFormal / bare versionRhetorical version
anaphoraWe must invest in schools, hospitals and roads.We must fund our schools, we must save our hospitals, we must mend our roads.
climaxThey took our land, freedom and lives.They took our land, then our freedom, and finally our very lives.
rhetorical questionThis situation is clearly unacceptable.How much longer must we tolerate this?
C2 subtlety
  • A rhetorical question must have an obvious answer that favours you; if the reader could sincerely answer "no", the device backfires.
  • Climax only rises: end on the biggest idea. "our lives, our jobs and our pens" collapses into unintended comedy.

4. Concession-refutation & inclusive "we" (admit, then counter; and unite)

Persuasion is not only attack. Concession-refutation first grants the other side a point (Admittedly… True… Of course…), then overturns it (yet… nonetheless… even so…). It makes you sound fair, so your rebuttal lands harder. The inclusive "we" folds the reader into your camp, turning an argument into a shared cause.

MoveSignal wordsExample
concessionadmittedly, granted, of course, trueAdmittedly, the cost is high…
refutationyet, nonetheless, even so, still…yet the cost of doing nothing is higher.
inclusive "we"we, us, our, togetherTogether, we can change what once seemed fixed.
C2 subtlety
  • Concede something real, not a straw man; a hollow concession fools no one and weakens your credibility.
  • Inclusive "we" persuades only if the reader feels genuinely included; used to command ("we" meaning "you") it turns patronising.

5. Rhetoric in register (matching device to tone)

A device must suit the register around it (Unit 1) and hold the text's cohesion (Units 8–9). Full-throated anaphora belongs in a rousing speech, not a quiet report; a rhetorical question that fits a rally can read as aggressive in a formal letter. C2 mastery is choosing not just which device but whether the moment can bear one at all.

ContextSuitsAvoid
school election speech, appealanaphora, tricolon, climaxflat, deviceless prose
formal report, class rulesrestrained parallelism, quiet antithesisrhetorical questions, chant-like repetition
persuasive essayconcession-refutation, one clean tricolondevice on every line
C2 subtlety
  • Rhetoric and cohesion work together: a tricolon that ignores the paragraph's flow feels bolted on, not built in.
  • The most persuasive prose often hides its devices; the reader feels the force without spotting the machinery.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

A student drafts the close of a school-council speech; an older friend tightens the rhetoric. Read it twice and watch how each note turns a flat line into a persuasive one — and where the friend says stop.

J:I wrote: "We should protect the library because it is useful for people."True, but flat — a claim with no structure and no rhythm.
C:Give it a tricolon. "A library is a door for the curious, a refuge for the lonely, a ladder for the poor."Three parallel images, rising — the ear is persuaded before the mind agrees.
J:Then: "Shall we not, dear friends, defend this hallowed, timeless temple of the mind?"Now over-corrected: empty, purple, over-formal padding.
C:That's the opposite error. Cut the incense. Try a clean rhetorical question: "What is a town without a place to learn?"Restraint — one honest question does the work of ten flourishes.
J:And the very last line?The close is where anaphora and climax earn their keep.
C:Anaphora into a climax: "Let us not falter, let us not flinch, let us not fail." Then stop.Rising repetition, then silence — the discipline is knowing when to end.
Test yourself
  • Why is the tricolon "a door…, a refuge…, a ladder…" more persuasive than "it is useful for people"? (Three parallel, rising images give rhythm and vividness; the bare claim gives neither.)
  • What made the second draft ("hallowed, timeless temple") fail? (Empty, overblown rhetoric: ornament with no argument, and a register far too purple.)
  • Rewrite the close for a sober written report instead. Which devices would you drop? (Drop the anaphora and rhetorical question; keep at most one restrained parallel pair — the register cannot bear a chant.)

The Architecture of Persuasion

Long before the microphone, there was the sentence — and the sentence, shaped with care, could move a crowd, topple a government, or mend a private grief. This is the province of rhetoric: the ancient, exacting craft of arranging words so that they persuade. Its tools are older than any parliament and younger than this morning's advertisement, for wherever one person hopes to sway another, rhetoric is quietly at work. It is not trickery, and it is not mere decoration; it is the deliberate engineering of language toward an end.

To understand why words arranged with care should hold such power, it helps to return to the dusty marketplaces of the ancient world, where rhetoric was first named and taught. In the crowded agora of Athens, and later in the forums of Rome, a citizen's fortune could hinge on a single afternoon's speech: a lawsuit won or lost, an army raised or disbanded, a reputation burnished or ruined. Aristotle, cataloguing the craft with a naturalist's patience, divided its persuasive force into three appeals — ethos, the speaker's character; pathos, the listener's emotion; logos, the argument's reason. The devices that fill these pages are the visible machinery through which those three appeals travel; they are the grooves cut into language so that character, feeling and reason may run swiftly from one mind into another. Far from being a modern gloss upon plain speaking, rhetoric is among the oldest of the disciplined arts, refined over centuries by orators who staked everything they had upon the single question of whether they would be believed.

Consider the tricolon, that irresistible group of three. "I came, I saw, I conquered." Three beats, each stronger than the last, and the ear is satisfied — persuaded by cadence before reason has even spoken. We remember in threes; we are convinced in threes; we are moved in threes.

Why should three, of all numbers, sit so comfortably in the ear? Part of the answer is arithmetic and part is almost musical. Two items offer a contrast but no pattern; the mind registers a pair and then waits, uncertain whether anything more will follow. Four or five items dissolve into mere inventory, a list to be endured rather than felt. Three, however, is the smallest quantity that can establish a rhythm and then complete it: the first beat opens, the second confirms, and the third resolves, the way a melody returns home to its final chord. Orators have exploited this instinct for millennia, and advertisers have inherited the habit wholesale — stop, look, listen; blood, sweat and tears; location, location, location. The tricolon persuades not because the audience consciously counts to three, but because the shape satisfies an appetite the listener never knew was there, and satisfaction, once given, is easily mistaken for assent.

Or take antithesis, the sharp collision of opposites: "not the size of the dog in the fight, but the size of the fight in the dog." By setting one idea against its mirror, the speaker carves a clean line the listener cannot unsee, and the contrast lingers long after the argument that carried it has faded. Meanwhile, anaphora — the drumbeat of a repeated opening — hammers a phrase home: we shall not falter, we shall not yield, we shall not fail.

Not every persuasive move, however, is a blow struck; some are hands extended. The inclusive "we" is the gentlest and most cunning of these, for it quietly enrols the listener in the speaker's cause before a single claim has been tested. When a leader says "we must decide what kind of country we wish to become", the pronoun performs a small act of enlistment: the audience, folded into that "we", finds it strangely awkward to dissent from a purpose it now appears to share. The concession works by a related generosity. By granting the opposing side a genuine point — the objection is a fair one, the cost is real and I shall not pretend otherwise — the speaker purchases a priceless commodity, which is the appearance of fairness, and, having bought it, spends it upon the refutation that follows. A speaker who concedes nothing sounds like a zealot; a speaker who concedes wisely sounds like a judge, and audiences have always found it easier to trust the judge.

The record of the spoken word is, in large part, a record of these devices deployed at moments of crisis. Lincoln, standing amid the fresh graves at Gettysburg, entrusted the survival of a nation to a single tricolon — a government "of the people, by the people, for the people" — and to a balanced antithesis between a world that would "little note, nor long remember" and the dead who could never be forgotten. Kennedy inverted one clause and minted a sentence that outlived him. Churchill, with an island's fate hanging in the balance, reached again and again for anaphora, vowing to fight on the beaches, on the landing grounds, in the fields and in the streets, until the sheer accumulation of repeated openings became a wall raised against despair. What unites these moments is not ornament but necessity: at the very hour when plain prose would have failed, it was structure that carried the weight meaning alone could not bear.

Nor does rhetoric fall silent when the crowd disperses and the page replaces the podium. The finest prose is quietly cadenced too, its clauses weighed and balanced so that the reading eye hears a rhythm the writer never spoke aloud. A well-built sentence, like a well-built arch, distributes its weight: subordinate clauses lean inward, the emphasis falls where the author intends, and the full stop arrives, ideally, upon the word that matters most. Essayists and novelists have long understood that a paragraph can climb toward its own quiet climax, that a short sentence dropped after several long ones lands like a gavel, and that the deliberate return of a single phrase can bind a chapter together as surely as any chant binds a rally. Persuasion, in short, is not confined to the platform; it lives wherever one mind sets out, patiently and by design, to alter another.

And yet, precisely because these tools are so effective, they carry a moral charge the honest speaker cannot ignore. The same tricolon that lends dignity to a plea for justice can lend a false grandeur to a lie; the same antithesis that clarifies a real choice can manufacture a phantom one, pressing a listener to choose between two options where a dozen quietly exist. Rhetoric, in this sense, resembles any powerful instrument — a scalpel, a lever, a flame — indifferent to the hand that wields it and to the end toward which it is turned. The craft can be taught; the conscience cannot. This is why the study of persuasion has always been shadowed by suspicion, from Plato's distrust of the sophists who sold eloquence to the highest bidder, to our own unease at slogans engineered in focus groups and tested upon strangers. The device itself is never innocent and never guilty; the whole of the responsibility rests with the one who chooses to speak.

Yet rhetoric can betray its user. For what is a speech, after all, but an argument set to music? Admittedly, a rhetorical question is a fine instrument; nonetheless, asked at the wrong moment, it rings hollow, even smug. Empty parallelism, unmatched at the joints, jars the very ear it meant to please. The finest persuaders therefore practise restraint. They concede before they refute; they build before they strike; they know that the grandest flourish still answers, in the end, to a single, honest, well-turned line.

How to structure the speech (Persuasive Structure)

A persuasive speech has a shape: a hook that grips, a body that argues and concedes, and a close that rouses. The devices belong at the joints — the opening question, the tricolon in the body, the anaphora at the end.

Sample Answer (~220 words) Friends, neighbours, fellow readers — tonight our library is under threat, and tonight we must decide what kind of town we wish to be.

Ask yourselves this: what is a town without a place to learn? A library is not merely a building of shelves and silence. It is a door for the curious, a refuge for the lonely, and a ladder for the poor. This tricolon is no accident: three plain images, rising, carry more persuasion than any statistic. Admittedly, the budget is tight; nonetheless, some things are too precious to price. Close these doors, and we do not save money — we spend our future.

Some will say the age of print is over. I say the age of ideas has only begun. Where others see old paper, I see young minds; where they see a cost, I see a promise. That antithesis is the whole argument in a single breath.

So let us not falter, let us not flinch, let us not fail. Sign the petition, raise your voice, and stand with your neighbours tonight. This is not a plea for a building, but a call for who we are. Save our library — and, in saving it, save the best of ourselves. The rousing close earns its cadence because the case beneath it is true.
