Ünite 1 · Perfect Tenses Review · Bölüm 1/6

Grammar: Perfect Tenses Review

Bring the four "past" tenses together and learn exactly when to choose each one.

One family, four different jobs

You already know these tenses one by one. The real B2 skill is choosing between them under pressure, in a real sentence, in a fraction of a second. This unit puts four tenses side by side — the Past Simple, the Present Perfect, the Present Perfect Continuous and the Past Perfect — and shows you the single question that decides each one.

Master this and your English immediately sounds more precise and more mature. Speakers who control these tenses can show time, sequence and consequence in one clean sentence, without ever explaining themselves twice.

  • I finished my science project at 6 p.m.Finished time (6 p.m.) → Past Simple.
  • I have finished my science project, so it is ready to hand in.Past action, result now → Present Perfect.
  • I have been working on my science project all day.Ongoing activity up to now → Present Perfect Continuous.
  • By the time she called, I had finished my science project.Earlier of two past events → Past Perfect.

1. The forms at a glance (quick review)

Before the meaning, fix the shape of each tense in your mind. Notice that every "perfect" tense uses a form of have, and the two continuous ideas add been + -ing.

TenseStructureExample
Past SimpleV2 (verb + -ed / irregular)She led the chess club in 2023.
Present Perfecthave / has + V3She has led three clubs.
Present Perfect Continuoushave / has + been + V-ingShe has been leading this club since May.
Past Perfecthad + V3She had led a club before she joined our school.
Key point
  • Only have / has / had changes for the subject — the participle (V3) never does: I have seen, she has seen, they had seen.
  • Never combine did with a perfect tense: "Have you seen it?" ✓ — "Did you have seen it?" ✗

2. Present Perfect vs Past Simple (finished vs unfinished time)

This contrast is the foundation. The Past Simple lives in a finished time (yesterday, in 2019, last week); the Present Perfect connects the past to now — a life experience, or an action whose result we still feel today.

IdeaPresent PerfectPast Simple
Life experience (when? not important)I have won big matches.I won that match in March.
Finished, specific time✗ not usedThe film ended at four.
Period still open / result nowWe have lost two players this term (the term is not over).We lost two players last term.
Golden rule
  • If the sentence names (or asks) exactly when in a finished time, use the Past Simple.
  • If the time is your whole life so far, the period is still open, or the result matters now, use the Present Perfect.

3. Present Perfect Simple vs Continuous (result vs process)

Both link the past to now, but they highlight different things. The simple form points at a completed result or a quantity; the continuous form points at the ongoing activity itself, often still unfinished.

FocusFormExample
Result / finished amountPresent Perfect SimpleI have written ten pages. (How many? A result.)
Ongoing activity / durationPresent Perfect ContinuousI have been writing all morning. (For how long? A process.)
Visible present effectPresent Perfect ContinuousI'm exhausted — I have been running.
B2 subtlety — stative verbs
  • Stative verbs (know, believe, own, love, understand, be) describe states, not actions, so they avoid the continuous: "I have known her for years." ✓ — "I have been knowing her..." ✗
  • For a finished number or amount, prefer the simple: "She has read three books." (not "has been reading three books").

4. The Past Perfect (narrative sequencing)

When you tell a story in the past, sometimes you need to jump even further back. The Past Perfect (had + V3) marks the action that happened first, so your listener never gets lost in the timeline.

  • When I arrived, the class had already started.The class started first (Past Perfect); I arrived second (Past Simple).
  • He couldn't play because he had forgotten his football boots.Forgetting the boots came before not being able to play.
  • By the time we performed, we had practised everything twice.Practising was complete before the performance.
B2 subtlety — do not overuse it
  • You only need the Past Perfect to show that one event came before another. For two actions in a clear sequence, the Past Simple is enough: "I opened the door and walked in." ✓ — not "I had opened the door and had walked in."
  • Words like before, after, by the time already show the order, so use the Past Perfect where the "earlier" action truly matters.

5. Time signals (the fastest clue)

The time words in a sentence usually decide the tense before you even think. Group them and let them work for you.

SignalPoints toExample
yesterday, ago, in 2019, last weeka finished time → Past SimpleWe finished the project two weeks ago.
just, already, yet, ever, so farup to now → Present PerfectHave they replied yet? / I've just texted them.
for, since (+ how long?)duration up to now → Present Perfect (often Continuous)I've been waiting since nine / for an hour.
by the time, before, after, already (past)earlier past event → Past PerfectBy the time the teacher came, the news had spread.
Watch out
  • ago always means a finished time, so it takes the Past Simple: "I joined the club five years ago." ✓ — "I have joined ... ago" ✗
  • for = a length of time (for two years); since = a starting point (since 2015). Never say "since two years".

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B2 students still make with the perfect tenses. Check your own writing against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
I have seen him yesterday.I saw him yesterday."yesterday" is a finished time → Past Simple.
I have been knowing her since 2019.I have known her since 2019."know" is stative → no continuous.
She has read since two hours.She has been reading for two hours.a duration takes "for", not "since".
I had opened the door and had gone out.I opened the door and went out.a clear sequence needs no Past Perfect.
When did you have finished it?When did you finish it?"when" about a finished event → Past Simple.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two classmates catch up after a hard week at school. Read it twice and watch how all four tenses cooperate: the Present Perfect opens a topic, the Past Simple adds finished detail, the Continuous shows ongoing effort, and the Past Perfect reaches back in time.

A:You look worn out. Have you been practising late again?Ongoing activity with a present effect → Present Perfect Continuous.
B:Every evening this week. I have already finished the science project, though.Completed result up to now → Present Perfect Simple.
A:Impressive. When did you actually start it?"When" about a finished event → Past Simple.
B:Last Monday. By the time I opened my notebook, someone had borrowed half my colour pens!Earlier past event → Past Perfect.
A:Typical. Have you told the teacher yet?"yet" → Present Perfect.
B:Not yet. I have been waiting for the poster paper since this morning.Duration up to now → Present Perfect Continuous.
Test yourself
  • Find the Past Perfect. Which event happened first? (Someone borrowed the pens, before B opened the notebook.)
  • Why is "did you start" in the Past Simple, not the Present Perfect? (A "when" question about a finished event.)
  • Rewrite "I have already finished the science project" as a question with yet. (Have you finished the science project yet?)

Speaking: Journeys, Setbacks & Growth

Learn the words, then use them in your own sentences about your progress.

setback (n)
a problem that delays or reverses progress (aksilik)
Syn: drawback · Ant: advance
"We have had a few setbacks, but the project is alive."
resilience (n)
the ability to recover quickly from difficulty (dayanıklılık, direnç)
Syn: toughness · Ant: fragility
"Hard years have taught me real resilience."
persevere (v)
to keep trying despite difficulty (azimle sürdürmek)
Syn: persist · Ant: quit
"She has persevered even when others gave up."
overcome (v)
to deal with a problem successfully (üstesinden gelmek)
Syn: conquer · Ant: surrender
"I have overcome my fear of public speaking."
underestimate (v)
to think something is smaller or easier than it is (hafife almak)
Syn: undervalue · Ant: overestimate
"I had underestimated how hard the exam would be."
thrive (v)
to grow or do very well (gelişip serpilmek)
Syn: flourish · Ant: struggle
"The team has thrived since we changed our training."
milestone (n)
an important stage or event in progress (dönüm noktası)
Syn: landmark · Ant: —
"Winning my first medal was a real milestone."
breakthrough (n)
an important, sudden success or discovery (atılım, çığır)
Syn: advance · Ant: setback
"After months of work, we have finally had a breakthrough."
cope (v)
to deal with something difficult (başa çıkmak)
Syn: manage · Ant: crumble
"I have learned to cope with hard homework."
momentum (n)
the force of continuing progress (ivme)
Syn: drive · Ant: stagnation
"Since spring, our team has gained momentum."
gain (v)
to get or obtain something useful (elde etmek, kazanmak)
Syn: acquire · Ant: lose
"I have gained a lot of experience this year."
steady (adj)
regular and continuous, without sudden change (istikrarlı, düzenli)
Syn: stable · Ant: erratic
"We have made steady progress every month."
venture (n)
a new project or plan that involves some risk (girişim)
Syn: enterprise · Ant: —
"Our new club has grown faster than anyone expected."
Talking about progress: mix all four tenses to show experience, result and effort.

Production Task (Üretim Görevi)

Write 8 sentences about a journey of growth — your studies, a sport, a hobby or a school project. Use at least 8 of the words above.

Grammar requirement — apply this unit's topic
  • Use the Present Perfect for experiences and results (I have overcome..., I have gained...).
  • Use the Present Perfect Continuous for ongoing effort (I have been working on... for months).
  • Use the Past Simple for finished details and the Past Perfect for an earlier event (By the time I started, I had already...).
  • Write at least 2 contrast pairs: a result in the Present Perfect, then a finished detail in the Past Simple. (Örn: "I have overcome my fear of exams. I failed my first one in 2021.")

Sentence starters (you can begin with these):

"I have been working on ... for ..." "By the time I started, I had already ..." "My biggest setback was ..." "Since then, I have ..." "Have you ever overcome ...?"

A cake stall rebuilt step by step: from a hard start to today.
Second Chances: How Leyla Rebuilt Her Cake Stall

Leyla runs one of the most popular cake stalls at her school, but her success has not come easily. She has been baking since she was five years old, and she has survived setbacks that would have discouraged most children.

Her love of baking began long before she ever thought of selling a single cake. As a small child, she had spent countless weekends in her grandmother's kitchen, watching flour turn into dough and dough turn into warm, golden buns. By the time she was nine, she could already shape a loaf without a recipe. Even so, nobody in her family expected her to sell her baking. In those days, baking was simply something the household did together on cold mornings, and it would be a while before Leyla realised that this childhood habit had quietly been preparing her for everything that followed.

Before she set up her own cake stall, Leyla had helped at three different school fairs. She had learned a lot about baking; however, she had never run a stall of her own. Therefore, when the school offered her a table at the spring fair, she hesitated. Nevertheless, she took the risk and said yes.

Each of those fairs had taught her something she would later depend on. At the first, a busy sports-day stall, she had learned how to stay calm when dozens of orders arrived at once. At the second, run by an older neighbour famous for her pastries, she had been shown that good bread cannot be rushed and cannot be faked either. At the third, she had watched, with growing frustration, how quickly a stall could fall apart when nobody planned ahead. By the time it closed, she had promised herself that if she ever ran a stall of her own, she would do things differently.

The first few weeks were brutal. She had underestimated the costs, and by the end of the first week she had almost run out of money for ingredients. Although she had made many mistakes, she refused to give up. Other stalls often used cheap mixes, whereas Leyla focused on quality. Since that hard week, she has spent every weekend improving her recipes and listening to her customers.

There was one morning during that first month that she still remembers vividly. A friend had forgotten to bring the flour, her cake tin had cracked overnight, and only a handful of classmates had stopped by her table all day. Standing behind the empty stall, she seriously considered giving it up. Then a quiet boy from another class, who bought the same little bun every morning, noticed how tired she looked and told her that her buns were the best he had ever tasted. That small comment, she says, was worth more than any poster. Instead of stopping, she borrowed a little money from her sister, fixed her tin, and started again the following week.

Slowly, she rebuilt the stall around the things she could control. She changed where she bought her ingredients until she found a shop she could trust, and she rewrote her recipes so that nothing left the table unless she would happily eat it herself. Rather than competing on price, she competed on flavour and on honesty, telling her classmates exactly what went into each cake. Word of mouth did the rest. Children began recommending the stall to their friends, and the queue on fair days grew a little longer with each passing month.

Her patience has paid off. Over the last three years, her stall has won two prizes at the school fair, and Leyla has taught more than a dozen younger children how to bake. Moreover, these days she has been selling her cakes online to families in her street, and demand has grown steadily.

The prizes, she is quick to point out, belong to the whole club rather than to her alone. The younger children she has taught come from very different classes; some had never baked anything before, while others had almost given up on it. She teaches them not only how to shape a bun but also how to talk to customers, manage their time, and take pride in their work. Two of the children she once helped have already started little stalls of their own at other schools, and Leyla speaks about them as if it were her greatest prize.

The move online was not something she had planned. When school closed for a few weeks, she began taking orders by phone and delivering cakes by bicycle to neighbours who could not go out. What started as a favour gradually turned into a proper part of the stall. Today, families from across the street order her buns and seasonal cakes through a simple page, and she has had to ask her little brother to help just to keep up with the demand. Yet she still insists on baking the first batch herself, exactly as she always has.

Success has not changed her routine as much as you might expect. She still refuses to use cheap ingredients, and she still tastes every new batch before it reaches the table. When friends ask her for the secret behind the two prizes, she usually laughs and says there is no secret at all, only patience and attention. Because she remembers how close she once came to failing, she treats every customer as if the whole stall depended on that single cake. She has also started giving free buns to a younger class each week, explaining that a stall which forgets its friends will not last. Many other stalls have opened and closed in the time since she started, yet Leyla's stall has kept growing, precisely because she has never taken her customers for granted.

These days Leyla still gets up early to bake. She has already achieved more than she once imagined, yet she insists that the best is still to come. 'I have been doing this for years,' she says, 'and I have learned that a good stall is never truly finished — you keep building it, loaf by loaf.'

Writing: A Turning Point

Write and submit your paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound B2 (Linking Words)

At B1 you linked ideas with and, but, so, because. At B2 you sound noticeably more advanced when you use formal, precise connectors. Four of them are essential this unit — and two familiar ones, although and however, become sharper tools.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
moreoveradd a stronger, extra pointThe bake sale went wrong. Moreover, it cost us two hours of work.
thereforeshow a logical resultTicket sales had fallen; therefore, we changed the show.
whereascontrast two ideas directlyMy first year was hard, whereas this year has been steady.
neverthelesssay "in spite of that"I had failed twice. Nevertheless, I tried again.
althoughconcede a point inside one sentenceAlthough I had made mistakes, I have learned a lot.
howevercontrast between two sentencesI lost the first match. However, I won three more.

See the difference — the same facts, written two ways:

Basic linking ✗Confident B2 writing ✓
I started a baking club. It was hard. I did not give up. I have learned a lot. I still have goals. I started a baking club, and the first month was really hard. Nevertheless, I persevered. Although I had underestimated the work, I have learned a great deal since then; therefore, I feel ready for the next stage.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different B2 connectors from the table. Do not write sentence by sentence; connect your ideas.
  • Moreover, Therefore, However, Nevertheless usually start a sentence and take a comma: "However, I tried again."

Paragraph structure (Paragraph Structure)

A "turning point" paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningA topic sentence in the Present PerfectI have faced several turning points in my life.
2. BodyTell the story. Use the Past Simple for finished events, the Past Perfect for an earlier event, and the Present Perfect (Continuous) for the result and ongoing effort. Link with B2 connectors.By the time I applied, I had already failed once. Nevertheless, I have kept improving since then.
3. ClosingA final comment about now or the futureI have not reached my main goal yet, therefore I keep working hard.

Sample Answer (~185 words) I have faced several turning points in my life, but the hardest one taught me the most. Three years ago, I decided to try out for the school football team. By the time the trials started, I had already practised all summer; nevertheless, I had underestimated how good the other players were.
