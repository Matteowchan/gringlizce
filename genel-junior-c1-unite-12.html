Ünite 12 · Advanced Relative Structures · Bölüm 1/7

Grammar: Advanced Relative Structures

Stop chaining short clauses. Start embedding whole ideas the way academic writers do.

Relative clauses at C1: precision and register

By C1 you can already build defining and non-defining relative clauses with who, which, that and whose. The advanced skill is a small family of structures that let you fold a whole argument into a single, controlled sentence and sound distinctly academic. Four moves matter most: a which that refers back to an entire clause (a sentential relative), a quantifier or noun + of which / of whom, the formal relative adverbs whereby, wherein and whereupon, and a fronted preposition + which/whom such as the extent to which.

These are not decoration. Each one lets you show a relationship between ideas — consequence, quantity, method, degree — without starting a new sentence. Used precisely, they raise the density and formality of your writing to the level examiners reward; used carelessly, they misfire badly, so this unit is as much about control as about range.

  • He was chosen as captain over his friends, which caused a little jealousy.Sentential relative → "which" refers to the whole first clause, not to one noun.
  • The survey asked 900 pupils, most of whom loved reading.Quantifier + of whom → a fact about part of a group, in one sentence.
  • They proposed a system whereby chores are shared equally.whereby = by which / by means of which → a formal method.
  • The report examines the extent to which the plan failed.Fronted preposition + which → the hallmark of academic register.

1. The sentential relative (which for a whole clause)

Normally which refers back to a noun. In a sentential relative it refers back to the entire preceding clause — the whole event or idea. It always follows a comma, and it lets you add a comment, a consequence or an evaluation without a fresh sentence. This is one of the most useful C1 structures — and the source of one of the most common errors, because learners reach for what instead.

StructureRefers toExample
, which + verbthe whole previous clauseShe quit the team without warning, which shocked the coach.
, all/none of whicha whole idea, quantifiedHe made three excuses, none of which convinced us.
, at which pointa moment within the clauseThe lights failed, at which point the talk ended.
Register & nuance — never use "what"
  • A sentential relative uses which, not what: "He lied to me, which hurt." ✓ — "He lied to me, what hurt." ✗
  • Always keep the comma before it; without one, the reader expects which to point at a single noun.

2. Quantifier / noun + of which / of whom

To say something about part of a group you have just mentioned, C1 writers use a quantifier or a noun followed by of which (things) or of whom (people). This packs two facts — the group and the sub-fact — into one elegant sentence. The word order is fixed: the quantifier or noun comes first, then of which / of whom.

PatternUseExample
many / several / most of whoma portion of a group of peopleTen new members joined, several of whom stayed on.
some / none / all of whicha portion of a set of thingsShe owns rare maps, some of which are priceless.
noun + of which / of whoma specific attribute of the groupA club, the leader of whom had left, met late.
C1 subtlety — order and choice
  • Keep the order: "...students, many of whom failed." ✓ — not "...students, which of them failed." ✗
  • Use of whom for people and of which for things — even the possessive idea (the cause of which, the leader of whom).

3. Formal relative adverbs (whereby / wherein / whereupon)

These single-word relatives replace a clumsier preposition + which and sound crisp and formal. whereby = by which / by means of which (a method or system). wherein = in which (a situation, text or place, very formal). whereupon = immediately after which (a swift consequence in a sequence). They belong to academic, legal and literary prose — powerful, but easy to overuse.

WordMeansExample
wherebyby which / by means of whichA scheme whereby members earn points on purchases.
whereinin which (a state or text)The novel depicts a world wherein emotion is banned.
whereuponright after whichHe signed the deed, whereupon the keys changed hands.
Register & nuance
  • Reserve these for formal writing; in speech, "a system whereby..." can sound stiff, so "a system where / that lets you..." is more natural.
  • Don't confuse them: whereby is method, wherein is location/state, whereupon is time/sequence. They are not interchangeable.

4. Preposition + which/whom & reduced relatives (the extent to which)

Formal English often fronts the preposition before the relative pronoun instead of leaving it stranded at the end. Compare informal "the house I live in" with formal "the house in which I live". This produces the classic academic phrases the extent to which, the manner in which, the degree to which and a friend with whom I worked. Separately, a reduced relative drops the pronoun and auxiliary to tighten a phrase.

Full / informalAdvanced / formalWhy?
how far the plan workedthe extent to which the plan workednominal, academic register
the person I spoke tothe person to whom I spokefronted preposition + whom (formal)
questions that were raisedquestions raised in the lessonreduced relative — tighter, faster
C1 subtlety
  • After a fronted preposition, use whom (not who) for people and which for things: "the field in which she works", "the author to whom he wrote".
  • Reduced relatives work with -ing (active) or -ed (passive): "the man waiting outside", "the data collected last year".

5. A crucial warning: whereas is NOT a relative

Because it begins with where-, learners often slip whereas into a relative slot. It is not a relative at all: it is a contrastive conjunction meaning "while, by contrast". It links two opposing ideas; it never introduces a relative clause about a noun. Confusing it with where / wherein / whereby is a signature C1 error.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
This is the classroom whereas I study.This is the classroom where / in which I study.place → relative, not whereas.
We found a door whereas led out.We found a door which led out.a noun → use which/that.
He is calm whereas.He is calm, whereas she is tense.whereas needs two contrasted ideas.
Rule of thumb
  • If you can replace the word with "by contrast", it is whereas. If you mean in / by / after which, it is a relative adverb.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the slips that still surface in otherwise strong C1 writing. Each one is a structure error, not a spelling slip. Audit your own sentences against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
He was late, what annoyed everyone.He was late, which annoyed everyone.a sentential relative uses "which", never "what".
200 guests, which of them left early.200 guests, many of whom left early.quantifier + of whom; keep the order and use "whom" for people.
a method whereas costs fall.a method whereby costs fall.method = whereby; "whereas" is contrast, not a relative.
the degree which prices rose.the degree to which prices rose.the fronted preposition "to" is required before "which".
The result, the cause of whom is unknown.The result, the cause of which is unknown.a cause is a thing → "of which", not "of whom".

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

A student and a teacher discuss a class project. Read it twice and watch the relative structures work as a system: a sentential which for a whole idea, a quantifier + of whom for part of a group, whereby for a method, the extent to which for degree, and whereupon for a swift consequence.

S:I surveyed forty classmates, most of whom welcomed the new club.Quantifier + of whom → a fact about part of the group.
T:Good. But your report ignores the extent to which the weather shaped their answers.Fronted preposition + which → academic "degree".
S:True. I suggested a plan whereby each class records its own results.whereby = by which → a method/system.
T:You handed it in late, which I had to note.Sentential which → refers to the whole previous clause.
S:I know. I fixed it overnight, whereupon the site finally accepted the file.whereupon = right after which → swift consequence.
T:Then we are ready — a report wherein every fact is now checked.wherein = in which → a formal "text/state".
Test yourself
  • Why is it "most of whom" and not "most of which"? (Classmates are people, so "of whom".)
  • What does the sentential "which" in "you handed it in late, which..." refer to? (The whole act of handing it in late, not a single noun.)
  • Rewrite "a plan whereby each class records" less formally. ("a plan where / in which each class records" — softer register.)

Speaking: Describing How Things Connect

Learn the words of systems and structure, then use the unit's relatives to explain how something works.

stipulate (v)

Syn: specify · Ant: imply
"The club rules stipulate a deadline no one may extend."

Reading: The Charter That Redefined a River

Read the feature, then work through the five tasks.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Skilled readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think of a shared resource — a park, a road, a river — that several groups must manage together. What usually goes wrong when nobody is clearly responsible?
  • The text is an academic-style feature about a legal agreement. Which formal relatives might such writing use to sound precise — whereby, wherein, or of which?
  • Watch for a comma followed by which that comments on a whole idea, not a single noun. Ask what each one adds.
How to read
  • First read: read quickly for the whole arc — what the charter changed and why it worked. Don't stop for every word.
  • Second read: track the relative structures — a method (whereby), a state (wherein), a sequence (whereupon), a portion of a group (the majority of whom), and a degree (the extent to which). Ask what each one does to the argument.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): framework · stipulate · encompass · contingent · interdependent · pertinent · underpin · intricate

One agreement binds every town on the river — a structure wherein no signatory can hide.
The Charter That Redefined a River

For over fifty years, the Valdera River had been governed by a patchwork of local rules, none of which addressed the pollution steadily creeping downstream. In 2019, however, twelve municipalities signed a charter whereby every town along the river became jointly liable for its health — a framework wherein no single authority could shift the blame onto another.

The Valdera itself was hardly a dramatic river; for most of its course it wound unremarkably through farmland and small industrial towns, the prosperity of which had long depended on the very factories now fouling the water. It was precisely this quiet ordinariness, some observers later argued, that had allowed the problem to fester, for there was no single spectacular disaster around which public outrage might crystallise. Instead the decline had been gradual, almost imperceptible, the kind of slow ruin against which conventional politics is notoriously ill-equipped to act. By the time the first serious proposals emerged, the river had reached a condition in which incremental fixes were plainly no longer enough, and the towns that shared its banks faced a stark choice between coordinated action and years of mutual recrimination.

The document, the drafting of which took almost three years, established a shared monitoring fund. Each town contributed according to its size; consequently, an independent panel — the majority of whom were scientists rather than politicians — began publishing regular data. Moreover, the extent to which this openness changed public behaviour surprised even the charter's authors, which suggests that information alone can be a powerful regulator.

The mechanics of the fund were deliberately unglamorous. Each contribution was calculated by a transparent formula, the details of which were published alongside the results, so that no town could later claim to have been overcharged. Samples were drawn at fixed points along the river, the readings from which fed directly into an online dashboard that any resident could consult at will. The panel took particular care to standardise its methods, since inconsistent measurement would have handed sceptics an easy line of attack. Consequently, when a factory's discharge exceeded the agreed limits, the evidence arrived not as an accusation from a rival town but as a dry, verifiable number, the provenance of which was almost impossible to dispute. This, more than any single clause of the agreement, gave the charter its quiet authority.

Critics had predicted failure. Since the scheme was voluntary, they argued, the wealthier towns would simply opt out. Yet the opposite occurred. Factories, many of which had resisted regulation for decades, found that being named in an open report was far more damaging than any fine. Whereas earlier laws had punished pollution only after the fact, the charter created a mechanism whereby reputation itself enforced compliance.

What unfolded next was a slow shift in how the towns behaved. Factory owners who had once brushed off complaints about the water began to dread the published figures, for a poor result could appear in the local paper within hours. Families, shopkeepers and visitors alike could now read information that had once been seen only by officials, and a good name built over decades could be dented in a single report. Some factories argued with the data at first, disputing the readings; others, recognising which way the wind was blowing, chose instead to compete on cleanliness, proudly advertising their improving numbers. In this way openness did more than expose wrongdoing — it quietly changed the reasons behind every choice each town made.

By 2024, fish had returned to stretches of water wherein nothing had survived for a generation. The recovery, the pace of which astonished ecologists, was not total; several tributaries remained badly contaminated, the cleanup of which will take decades. Notwithstanding these gaps, the charter has become a model that other regions now study closely; nonetheless, its architects warn against copying it blindly.

The charter's growing fame brought complications of its own. Delegations from other river basins arrived to study the scheme, many of whom hoped to transplant it wholesale into circumstances that bore little resemblance to the Valdera. The architects were careful, in every briefing, to stress how much of the success had depended on local factors: a manageable number of towns, a shared sense of grievance, and a press willing to report the figures fairly. A mechanism that harnesses reputation, they cautioned, only works where reputation is visible and where an audience actually cares. Where those conditions were absent, the same open reports might simply be ignored, and the elegant structure that had disciplined the Valdera would achieve nothing at all. The lesson they pressed on their visitors was therefore not a template but a habit of mind: adapt the principle, they insisted, rather than copy the paperwork.

People who later studied the case were struck by how little it relied on the usual tools for protecting rivers. There were no new taxes, no army of inspectors, and no fresh punishments beyond those that already existed and had so plainly failed. What the charter supplied instead was a trustworthy flow of information and a structure in which that information genuinely mattered — an arrangement whereby the cost of pollution was paid, at least in part, in lost pride and public respect. Whether such an approach can be scaled to larger rivers, longer coastlines or whole regions remains genuinely uncertain; the Valdera, after all, is small, and the trust on which its charter depended cannot simply be ordered into existence. It is one thing to publish figures that a few thousand neighbours will actually read, and quite another to hold the attention of a distant crowd for whom a polluted shore is merely a word on a map. Even so, the experiment has forced a wider question into the open: how much of what we routinely ask the law to do might be achieved, more cheaply and more lastingly, by simply telling the truth in plain view.

Its central lesson, which is easily overlooked, is quietly radical: cooperation was secured not by a stronger law but by a cleverer structure — one wherein every signatory held a stake, and from which none could quietly withdraw.

Writing: An Analytical Description

Write and submit a controlled, formal description; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound C1 (Linking Words)

At B2 you linked ideas with however, therefore, although, whereas. At C1 you gain a sharper, more formal register with connectors such as moreover, consequently, notwithstanding, whereas and nonetheless. Used precisely — and sparingly — they signal a writer in full command of tone, and they pair naturally with the relative structures of this unit.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
moreoveradd a stronger, further pointThe fund grew. Moreover, transparency improved.
consequentlystate a formal result or effectCosts were shared; consequently, no town could opt out.
notwithstandingconcede "in spite of" (very formal)Notwithstanding the gaps, the model spread.
whereasdraw a direct contrast (not a relative!)Old laws punished; whereas the charter prevented.
nonethelesssay "even so", after a concessionIt was voluntary; nonetheless, it worked.
howevercontrast between two sentencesSuccess seemed unlikely. However, it came.

See the difference — the same facts, written two ways:

Choppy narration ✗Controlled C1 narration ✓
The towns signed a charter. It made them all responsible. Some factories resisted. They were named in reports. This damaged them. The towns signed a charter whereby all became jointly responsible. Some factories, many of which had resisted for years, were named in open reports, which damaged them far more than any fine.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different C1 connectors from the table. Weave your description; don't list sentences.
  • Remember: whereas is contrast, never a relative. For method use whereby; for a state use wherein.

How to structure an analytical description (Structure)

A strong C1 description of a system or institution is not a flat list of features. It manages relationships deliberately: it defines the thing, explains how it works, quantifies its parts, and evaluates it. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the shape:

PartWhat to writeStructure to reach for
1. Definitionwhat it is and why it mattersdefining relative + the extent to which
2. Mechanismhow it actually workswhereby (method) + wherein (state)
3. Compositionwho or what it is made ofquantifier + of which / of whom
4. Evaluationjudge its strengths and limitssentential which + C1 connectors

Sample Answer (~205 words) My neighbourhood runs a food cooperative, the popularity of which surprises everyone who visits. It is a simple framework whereby residents buy fresh fruit and vegetables straight from local farms, so the food arrives fresh and cheap. Membership encompasses about three hundred households, most of whom volunteer a few hours each month.

The scheme underpins a quiet little world wherein trust matters more than money. Prices are contingent on the harvest, which means members share both good seasons and bad ones. The rules, drawn up by an elected committee, stipulate that every family must take a turn on the rota; consequently, no one is merely a shopper, and the tasks, many of which are dull, are shared fairly.

What impresses me most is the extent to which this intricate little system has bound the street together. Neighbours who barely spoke are now interdependent, which has changed the whole atmosphere. Whereas the old shop treated us as strangers, the cooperative treats us as partners.

Notwithstanding the occasional dispute, whereupon the committee steps in to mediate, the model has held firm for six years. It is not perfect; nonetheless, it delineates a pertinent lesson: a cleverer structure, rather than a bigger budget, can transform a community.
