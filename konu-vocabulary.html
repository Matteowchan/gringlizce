Vocabulary & UsageKelime ve Kullanım — Sözcükleri Doğru Birleştirmek

İleri seviyenin sırrı daha fazla kelime ezberlemek değil, kelimeleri doğru birleştirmektir. Anadili konuşan biri make a decision der, do a decision demez — bu bir kural değil, bir alışkanlık örüntüsüdür. Bu bölüm kelime bilgisinin katmanlarını ve doğru kalıpları öğrenmenin yollarını gösterir.

The secret to an advanced level is not memorizing more words but combining words correctly. A native speaker says make a decision, not do a decision — this is not a rule but a habitual pattern. This section shows the layers of vocabulary knowledge and the ways to learn the right patterns.

Neden bu bölüm bu kadar önemli? Çünkü sınavların ve gerçek hayatın ayırt ettiği şey, kaç kelime tanıdığın değil, kaç kelimeyi doğru kullanabildiğindir. Pasif kelime dağarcığın (okuyunca anladığın sözcükler) her zaman aktif dağarcığından (kendin üretebildiklerin) çok daha geniştir. Bu bölümün amacı, tanıdığın kelimeleri aktif hâle getirmen için gereken katmanları — birlikte kullanıldıkları sözcükler, aldıkları edatlar, türedikleri ekler — tek çatı altında toplamaktır. Bir kelimeyi gerçekten "bilmek" onu telaffuz edebilmek ve anlamını söyleyebilmekten fazlasıdır: hangi cümlelerde doğal durduğunu, hangi register'da (resmî/gündelik) kullanıldığını ve yanına hangi sözcükleri çektiğini de bilmektir.

Türkçe konuşan öğrenciler için en büyük tuzak, kelimeleri kafada Türkçe karşılıklarıyla eşleştirip cümleyi Türkçe mantığıyla kurmaktır. make ve do Türkçede tek bir "yapmak" fiiline karşılık geldiği için öğrenci ikisini karıştırır; oysa İngilizce bunları alışkanlık hâline gelmiş kalıplarla dağıtır: make a mistake ama do your homework. Bu kalıpların ardında çoğu zaman mantıklı bir kural yoktur — bunlar dilin "parmak izidir" ve ancak bol maruz kalma ve tekrar ile içselleşir. İyi haber: bir kez öğrenilen doğal eşleşme, cümlelerini anında daha akıcı ve anadili sesine yakın hâle getirir.

When you note a new word, also write the word it goes with: not decision but make a decision. That makes the word usable.

Why does this section matter so much? Because what exams and real life single out is not how many words you recognize but how many words you can use correctly. Your passive vocabulary (the words you understand when you read them) is always much larger than your active vocabulary (the ones you can produce yourself). The aim of this section is to gather, under one roof, the layers you need to turn the words you recognize into active ones — the words they are used with, the prepositions they take, the affixes they derive from. Truly "knowing" a word is more than being able to pronounce it and state its meaning: it is knowing which sentences it sits naturally in, which register (formal/informal) it is used in, and which words it draws to its side.

For Turkish-speaking learners, the biggest trap is matching words with their Turkish equivalents in the head and building the sentence with Turkish logic. Because make and do both correspond to the single Turkish verb "yapmak," learners confuse the two; yet English distributes them through habitual patterns: make a mistake but do your homework. There is often no logical rule behind these patterns — they are the language's "fingerprint" and are internalized only through plenty of exposure and repetition. The good news: a natural match once learned instantly makes your sentences more fluent and closer to a native speaker's ear.

Types of Patterns — Their Differences

Vocabulary is not a single pile; it consists of several different "types of patterns," and each type has its own logic. Telling these types apart matters because each is studied differently: you learn collocations through exposure and repetition, idioms by memorizing them as wholes, phrasal verbs by pairing them with their particle, and derivations by working out the logic of affixes. The table below compares the four main types at a glance; the section that follows deepens each one in turn.

Her Türü Tek Tek — Kural, Örnek, Nüans

Şimdi her kalıp türünü sırasıyla açalım. Her paragraf aynı düzeni izler: önce türün ne olduğunu tanımlar, sonra tipik bir İngilizce örnek verir, en sonda Türkçe konuşan öğrenci için kritik nüansı belirtir. Amaç ezber listesi vermek değil, her türü kendi kendine keşfetmeni sağlayacak bakış açısını kazandırmaktır — böylece karşına çıkan yeni ifadeleri doğru rafına yerleştirir ve doğru yöntemle öğrenirsin.

Collocations (sözcük eşleşmeleri). Collocation, belirli sözcüklerin alışkanlık hâline gelmiş şekilde birlikte kullanılmasıdır. Anlamları tektir ama birleşimleri sabittir: heavy rain (yoğun yağmur) doğaldır, strong rain değil; ama çay için strong tea deriz, heavy tea demeyiz. En sık kalıplar fiil + isim (take a photo, make an effort, pay attention), sıfat + isim (a heavy smoker, a narrow escape) ve zarf + sıfat (deeply concerned, fully aware) örüntülerinde görülür. Türkçe nüans: bu eşleşmeler dilden dile taşınmaz, bu yüzden çeviri yaparken sözcüğü değil kalıbın tamamını düşün.

do

Çoğunlukla bir eylem/görev ya da genel iş fikri taşır. do your homework, do the housework, do business, do research, do a favour.

Idioms (deyimler). Deyim, sözcüklerin tek tek anlamından bağımsız, bütün olarak mecazi bir anlam taşıyan sabit ifadedir. break the ice buzu kırmakla değil, ortamdaki gerginliği dağıtmakla ilgilidir; once in a blue moon "çok nadiren" demektir. Deyimlerin sözcükleri genellikle değiştirilemez: break the frost yanlıştır. Türkçe nüans: Türkçede de deyim boldur, ama birebir çeviri neredeyse hiç tutmaz — it's raining cats and dogs "bardaktan boşanırcasına yağıyor" demektir, "kedi köpek yağıyor" değil. Deyimleri günlük konuşmada ve gayrıresmî yazıda kullan; akademik metinlerde ölçülü ol.

Phrasal verbs (öbek fiiller). Fiil + edat/zarf birleşimidir ve çoğu zaman fiilin sözlük anlamıyla ilgisi olmayan yeni bir anlam doğurur: give up (vazgeçmek), look after (bakmak, ilgilenmek), put off (ertelemek). Bazıları ayrılabilir (turn the light off / turn off the light), bazıları ayrılamaz (look after the baby, look the baby after değil). Türkçe nüans: phrasal verb'ler konuşma dilinde çok yaygındır; anadili konuşanlar tolerate yerine put up with der. Resmî yazıda ise çoğu zaman tek sözcüklü Latince kökenli karşılığı (postpone, tolerate, investigate) tercih edilir.

Word formation (kelime türetme). Aynı kökten ek yardımıyla farklı sözcük türleri üretilir: use → useful → useless → usefully → misuse. Bu mekanizmayı bilmek, tek bir kökten dört-beş kelime kazanmanı sağlar ve boşluk doldurma sorularında (özellikle YDS/YÖKDİL ve Cambridge sınavlarında) hayat kurtarır. Ekler sözcüğün türünü belirler: -tion/-ment/-ness isim, -ful/-ous/-al sıfat, -ly zarf, -ise/-ify/-en fiil yapar. Türkçe nüans: cümledeki boşluğa hangi türden sözcük gerektiğini (isim mi, sıfat mı) önce belirle, sonra doğru eki seç.

Confusable words (karıştırılan sözcükler). Yazılışı veya sesi birbirine benzeyen ama anlamı tamamen farklı çiftler, en sinsi hatalardır çünkü yazım denetimi bunları yakalamaz: affect (etkilemek, fiil) / effect (etki, isim); its (onun) / it's (it is); lose (kaybetmek) / loose (gevşek); advice (tavsiye, isim) / advise (tavsiye etmek, fiil). Türkçe nüans: bu çiftleri birbirinden ayıran küçük bir bağlam ipucu ezberle (örneğin "advice = isim, advise = fiil") ve her yazımdan sonra bilinçli olarak kontrol et.

Word families & ekler (prefix/suffix). Ön ekler (prefix) çoğunlukla anlamı değiştirir: un-, in-, im-, dis-, mis-, re-, over- (happy → unhappy, do → redo, cook → overcook). Son ekler (suffix) ise türü değiştirir. Bir kökün ailesini birlikte öğrenmek — economy, economic, economical, economist, economize — kelime dağarcığını katlanarak büyütür ve okurken tanımadığın bir sözcüğün anlamını ve türünü tahmin etme gücü kazandırır.

Aşağıdaki tablo, tek bir kökten kelime ailesinin nasıl doğduğunu gösterir. Bu mantığı kavradığında, okuma parçasında tanımadığın bir sözcüğün türünü ve yaklaşık anlamını, kökünü ve ekini ayırarak tahmin edebilirsin. Bu, hem okuma hızını hem de boşluk doldurma başarını doğrudan artırır.

Each Type One by One — Rule, Example, Nuance

Now let's unpack each type of pattern in turn. Each paragraph follows the same layout: first it defines what the type is, then gives a typical English example, and finally notes the critical nuance for the Turkish-speaking learner. The aim is not to give a list to memorize but to give you the perspective to discover each type for yourself — so that you place the new expressions you meet on the right shelf and learn them with the right method.

Collocations. A collocation is the habitual co-occurrence of certain words. Their meanings are literal, but their combinations are fixed: heavy rain is natural, not strong rain; but for tea we say strong tea, not heavy tea. The most common patterns are verb + noun (take a photo, make an effort, pay attention), adjective + noun (a heavy smoker, a narrow escape), and adverb + adjective (deeply concerned, fully aware). Turkish nuance: these pairings do not carry over from language to language, so when you translate, think of the whole pattern, not the single word.

make

Mostly carries the idea of creating/producing something. make a decision, make a mistake, make an effort, make progress, make a difference.

↔
do

Mostly carries the idea of an action/task or general work. do your homework, do the housework, do business, do research, do a favour.

Idioms. An idiom is a fixed expression that carries a figurative meaning as a whole, independent of the individual meanings of its words. break the ice is not about breaking ice but about easing the tension in a setting; once in a blue moon means "very rarely." The words of an idiom usually cannot be changed: break the frost is wrong. Turkish nuance: Turkish is also rich in idioms, but a literal translation almost never works — it's raining cats and dogs means "it's pouring," not "cats and dogs are raining." Use idioms in everyday speech and informal writing; be moderate with them in academic texts.

Phrasal verbs. A phrasal verb is a combination of a verb + particle/adverb, and it often produces a new meaning unrelated to the verb's literal meaning: give up (to quit), look after (to take care of), put off (to postpone). Some are separable (turn the light off / turn off the light), some inseparable (look after the baby, not look the baby after). Turkish nuance: phrasal verbs are very common in spoken language; native speakers say put up with rather than tolerate. In formal writing, however, the single-word Latinate equivalent (postpone, tolerate, investigate) is usually preferred.

Word formation. Different word classes are produced from the same root with the help of affixes: use → useful → useless → usefully → misuse. Knowing this mechanism lets you gain four or five words from a single root and is a lifesaver in gap-fill questions (especially in YDS/YÖKDİL and Cambridge exams). Affixes set the word's class: -tion/-ment/-ness make nouns, -ful/-ous/-al adjectives, -ly adverbs, and -ise/-ify/-en verbs. Turkish nuance: first decide which class of word the gap needs (a noun or an adjective), then choose the right affix.

Confusable words. Pairs that look or sound alike but mean completely different things are the sneakiest mistakes because a spell-checker won't catch them: affect (to influence, verb) / effect (an influence, noun); its (possessive) / it's (it is); lose (to misplace) / loose (not tight); advice (a recommendation, noun) / advise (to recommend, verb). Turkish nuance: memorize a small context cue that tells each pair apart (for example, "advice = noun, advise = verb") and check consciously after every piece of writing.

Word families & affixes (prefix/suffix). Prefixes mostly change the meaning: un-, in-, im-, dis-, mis-, re-, over- (happy → unhappy, do → redo, cook → overcook). Suffixes, on the other hand, change the class. Learning a root's family together — economy, economic, economical, economist, economize — grows your vocabulary exponentially and gives you the power to guess the meaning and class of an unfamiliar word while reading.

Once you grasp this logic, you can guess the class and approximate meaning of an unfamiliar word in a reading passage by separating its root and its affix. This directly improves both your reading speed and your success in gap-fill questions.

One last warning about phrasal verbs and idioms: because they look "cool," learners try to force them into sentences, but a phrasal verb used with the wrong particle changes the meaning entirely. look after (to take care of), look for (to search for), and look into (to investigate) all spring from the same verb but are completely different. So learn each phrasal verb as a single unit together with its particle and anchor it with an example sentence. The same goes for idioms: a half-remembered idiom (a piece of cake walk) looks worse than the correct one — if you are not sure, use a plain, correct expression.

Build your own sentence

The most efficient way to study vocabulary is not memorizing isolated lists but a cycle of repetition and production. The first time you see a word, your brain places it in short-term memory; for it to stick, you need to meet it again at increasing intervals (the same day, the next day, three days later, a week later) — this is called spaced repetition. A flashcard app like Anki calculates these intervals for you. But recognition is not enough: use each new word in a sentence relevant to your own life. If you have learned "reluctant," build a sentence of your own like "I was reluctant to speak in front of the class" — personal context strengthens memory.

How to study

Set a weekly target (for example, 20 new collocations), write each word in your notebook with an example sentence, and three days later close the notebook and try to produce the English from the Turkish equivalent. What you can produce is your real active vocabulary; put what you can't back into the repetition cycle.

The most natural way to grow your vocabulary is plenty of reading and listening. When you read texts at your level on topics that interest you, you meet the same word again and again in different contexts and internalize its meaning without a dictionary. While reading, don't look up every unfamiliar word right away; first try to guess it from context, because that is exactly what you have to do in an exam too. If a word keeps coming up and still blocks you, then add it to your notebook. This "extensive reading" habit gives a naturalness and a feel for usage that memorized lists can never provide.

Common Mistakes

Literal translation

Turkish collocations don't carry over to English: do a mistake → make a mistake; drink a soup → have soup.

The common source of these mistakes is building the sentence in Turkish first and then translating it word by word. The solution is to try to think in English directly, with English patterns: when expressing an idea, instead of asking "how would I say this in Turkish?" ask "what would a native speaker say in this situation?" The correct example sentences you record feed exactly this inner model. It may seem slower in the short term, but in the long run it is the only way to escape the literal-translation trap.

Confusable words

affect/effect, its/it's, lose/loose, advice/advise distort the meaning. Details: Confusable Words.

The wrong preposition

Adjectives and verbs come with specific prepositions; choosing them by Turkish logic produces errors: good at (not in), interested in (not to), depend on (not to), married to (not with). Learn these pairs as wholes, like collocations.

Mind the register

Idioms and phrasal verbs are natural in everyday language; in academic writing, more formal equivalents are preferred (find out → discover). See Register & Tone.

Another common mistake is fitting a memorized word into the wrong grammatical pattern. Not every verb takes the same structure: some require -ing (enjoy doing), some require to (want to do), and some depend on a specific preposition (depend on, good at, interested in). When you learn a word, note this "grammatical nest" too. Finally, trying to use overly rich vocabulary is also a trap: rather than cramming three fancy words you're unsure about into one sentence, a plain sentence used correctly is always more effective. Accuracy first, variety second.
