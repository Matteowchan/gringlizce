Grammar: Advanced Linking & Concession

Say "yes, but" like an educated speaker: concede a point, then contrast it with precision.

Concession: the mark of a mature writer

At B2 you no longer join ideas with a string of but and and. You learn to concede a point — to admit that something is true — and then turn it against a stronger idea. This is the language of essays, reports and serious discussion, and it makes you sound balanced and in control.

This unit gathers the whole family of concession and contrast links: the prepositions despite / in spite of, the conjunctions although / even though / though, the comparison words whereas / while, and the formal linking adverbs however, nevertheless, nonetheless and on the other hand. The single skill that ties them together is knowing what follows each one — a noun, an -ing form, or a full clause.

  • Despite the rain, the ceremony went ahead.despite + noun (a preposition, no verb after it).
  • Although it was raining, the ceremony went ahead.although + a full clause (subject + verb).
  • The morning was bright, whereas the afternoon turned grey.whereas compares two contrasting situations.
  • The plan was risky. Nevertheless, the board approved it.a formal linking adverb across two sentences.

1. despite / in spite of (preposition + noun / -ing / the fact that)

These two mean exactly the same thing ("in spite of that"). They are prepositions, so they are never followed by a full clause on their own. Three things can follow them: a noun, an -ing form, or the phrase the fact that + clause.

What followsPatternExample
a noundespite / in spite of + nounDespite the delay, we caught the train.
an -ing formdespite / in spite of + V-ingIn spite of feeling ill, she finished the race.
a full clausedespite / in spite of + the fact that + clauseDespite the fact that he was tired, he kept going.
Key point — the two classic errors
  • Never write despite that + clause: "Despite it was late..." ✗ — you need "Despite the fact that it was late..." ✓ or simply "Although it was late..." ✓
  • There is no of after despite: "despite of the rain" ✗ — "despite the rain" ✓. Only "in spite of" carries the of.

2. although / even though / though (conjunction + full clause)

These are conjunctions, so each one introduces a full clause (subject + verb). They mean the same as despite, but the grammar after them is different — a clause, not a noun.

ConnectorForce / positionExample
althoughneutral; most common in writingAlthough the film was long, nobody left.
even thoughstronger, more emphaticShe stayed calm even though she was furious.
thoughinformal; can also end a sentenceIt was expensive. I bought it, though.
B2 subtlety
  • The clause can come first or second: "Although we lost, we played well." = "We played well, although we lost." Use a comma when the clause comes first.
  • Do not add but as well: "Although it was cold, but we swam." ✗ — one connector is enough.

3. whereas / while (comparing two situations)

Use whereas and while to set two facts side by side and highlight the difference between them. Both parts of the sentence are usually true; you are not conceding a point, you are comparing.

  • Our exports rose sharply, whereas our imports fell.Two contrasting facts, weighed against each other.
  • While the north of the country is wet, the south is dry.while = whereas here (a direct comparison).
  • He prefers a fixed routine, whereas I like variety.Two people, two opposite preferences.
Watch out — whereas is NOT "because"
  • whereas shows a contrast, never a reason: "I stayed home whereas it was raining." ✗ — use "because it was raining" ✓ for a reason.
  • while has two meanings: "at the same time as" ("while I cooked") and "whereas" ("while some agree, others do not"). The context makes it clear.

4. however / nevertheless / nonetheless / on the other hand (linking adverbs + punctuation)

These are linking adverbs, not conjunctions. They connect two separate sentences, and they usually start the second sentence, followed by a comma. They belong to formal, written English.

AdverbMeaningExample
however"but" (formal contrast)The offer was generous. However, we declined it.
nevertheless / nonetheless"in spite of that"The risks were high. Nevertheless, she invested.
on the other handintroduces the opposite sideCars are convenient. On the other hand, they pollute.
Punctuation rule
  • Use a full stop or a semicolon before the adverb, then a comma after it: "It was late; however, we continued." ✓
  • A comma alone is wrong: "It was late, however we continued." ✗ (this is a run-on sentence).

5. no matter how / however + adjective (concession with intensity)

To say that something stays true whatever the degree, use no matter how or however in front of an adjective or adverb. They mean "it does not matter how much".

  • No matter how hard I try, I cannot please everyone.no matter how + adjective/adverb + clause.
  • However expensive it is, they will buy it.however + adjective + clause (formal, same meaning).
  • No matter how carefully we plan, surprises happen.Concedes that effort does not remove the problem.
Key point
  • Here however is NOT the linking adverb from card 4. It joins tightly to the adjective: "However tired you are, keep going." (no comma after "however").
  • The related family: no matter what / who / where / when — "No matter what happens, I trust you."

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B2 students still make with concession links. Check your own writing against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
Despite it was raining, we walked.Although it was raining, we walked.despite cannot take a clause; use although, or "despite the fact that".
Despite of the noise, I slept.Despite the noise, I slept.no "of" after despite (only after "in spite").
Although the traffic, we were late.Despite the traffic, we were late.although needs a clause; before a noun use despite.
I like tea whereas I don't like coffee, because it's bitter.I like tea, whereas I dislike coffee.whereas is contrast, not a reason ("because").
It was hard, however we finished.It was hard; however, we finished.a linking adverb needs a full stop or semicolon before it.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two colleagues debate whether to accept a new project. Read it twice and watch how each concession link connects to what follows: a noun after despite, a clause after even though, a comparison with whereas, and a linking adverb with however.

A:Should we take the contract? Despite the tight deadline, it pays very well.despite + noun ("the tight deadline").
B:I'm tempted, even though our team is already overloaded.even though + full clause.
A:Their last client was patient, whereas this one wants everything yesterday.whereas compares two situations.
B:True. However, turning down good money now would be foolish.linking adverb after a full stop, then a comma.
A:No matter how hard it gets, we always deliver.no matter how + adverb + clause.
B:Agreed. In spite of the pressure, let's say yes.in spite of + noun ("the pressure").
Test yourself
  • Why is it "despite the tight deadline" and not "although the tight deadline"? (despite takes a noun; although needs a clause.)
  • Rewrite "even though our team is overloaded" using despite. (Despite being overloaded, / Despite the fact that our team is overloaded.)
  • Fix the punctuation: "It's risky, however we'll try." (It's risky; however, we'll try.)

Speaking: Weighing Both Sides

Learn the words, then use them to argue a case fairly — concede a point, then make yours.

drawback (n)
a disadvantage or negative side of something (dezavantaj, sakınca)
Syn: downside · Ant: benefit
"Despite its drawbacks, the plan is worth trying."
counterargument (n)
an argument used against another argument (karşı görüş)
Syn: rebuttal · Ant: agreement
"There is a strong counterargument, however, that we cannot ignore."
trade-off (n)
a balance between two things you cannot both fully have (ödünleşim, denge)
Syn: compromise · Ant: —
"Speed and quality involve a trade-off; you rarely get both."
offset (v)
to balance one effect against an opposite one (dengelemek, telafi etmek)
Syn: counterbalance · Ant: worsen
"The higher cost is offset by lower repair bills."
concede (v)
to admit that something is true, often reluctantly (kabul etmek, teslim etmek)
Syn: admit · Ant: deny
"I concede that the cost is high, but the value is higher."
nonetheless (adv)
in spite of what has just been said (yine de, buna rağmen)
Syn: nevertheless · Ant: therefore
"The idea is unusual; nonetheless, it might work."
compromise (n/v)
an agreement where each side gives up something (uzlaşma, ödün)
Syn: middle ground · Ant: stalemate
"After a long debate, we reached a fair compromise."
disparity (n)
a large or unfair difference between things (eşitsizlik, uçurum)
Syn: gap · Ant: equality
"There is a wide disparity between the two regions."
paradox (n)
something that seems to contradict itself but may be true (paradoks, çelişki)
Syn: contradiction · Ant: —
"It is a paradox that the more we hurry, the slower we get."
mitigate (v)
to make something bad less serious (hafifletmek, azaltmak)
Syn: ease · Ant: intensify
"Good planning can mitigate the risks considerably."
contradict (v)
to say or show the opposite of something (çelişmek, yalanlamak)
Syn: oppose · Ant: confirm
"The new data seem to contradict the earlier report."
weigh up (phr v)
to consider the good and bad points before deciding (tartmak, değerlendirmek)
Syn: assess · Ant: ignore
"Let's weigh up the options before we commit."
on balance (phr)
after considering everything (genel olarak, her şey düşünülünce)
Syn: all in all · Ant: —
"On balance, the benefits outweigh the drawbacks."
albeit (conj)
although; even if (formal, before a short phrase) (her ne kadar ... olsa da)
Syn: though · Ant: —
"The results were positive, albeit modest."
Weighing both sides: concede a point, then contrast it with a concession link.

Reading: The Two Faces of Ecotourism

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Have you ever visited a national park or a protected natural area? What was it like?
  • The text weighs the good and bad sides of "green" travel. Which concession links do you expect to see?
  • The title says "Two Faces". Do you think the writer will fully support ecotourism, or stay balanced?
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text. Underline every concession link — despite, although, even though, whereas, however, nevertheless, no matter how — and ask which side of the argument it introduces.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): drawback · compromise · trade-off · offset · mitigate · concede · disparity · on balance

Ecotourism has two faces: it can protect nature, yet the travel itself has a cost.
The Two Faces of Ecotourism

Ecotourism promises to protect nature while letting travellers enjoy it. Despite this green image, however, the reality is more complicated. Although the idea sounds simple, balancing profit and protection is anything but easy.

On the positive side, ecotourism can fund conservation. In many rainforests, the money that tourists pay protects trees that would otherwise be cut down. Even though logging is often more profitable in the short term, some communities now choose tourism, whereas twenty years ago they saw the forest only as timber. Moreover, visitors who watch wildlife closely tend to care about it, and that concern often lasts long after they fly home.

On the other hand, the drawbacks are real. No matter how careful a company claims to be, large numbers of visitors disturb fragile habitats. Despite strict rules, some tourists still leave rubbish, feed wild animals or frighten them. In spite of good intentions, the flights that carry eco-tourists across the world release enormous amounts of carbon. Thus a holiday designed to celebrate nature may quietly harm it.

So is ecotourism worth it? Although the concept is far from perfect, most experts argue that responsible tourism still beats the alternative. Nevertheless, they warn that it works only when visitor numbers stay small and the profits genuinely reach local people.

On balance, ecotourism is neither a miracle nor a disaster. It is a fragile compromise that must be managed with real care — loaf by loaf, tree by tree.

Writing: For and Against

Write and submit a balanced paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound B2 (Concession & Contrast)

At B1 you linked ideas with and, but, so, because. At B2, a balanced argument needs the concession family from this unit. The trick is choosing the right one for what comes next — a noun, a clause, or a whole new sentence.

ConnectorFollowed by...Example
despite / in spite ofa noun or -ing formDespite the cost, the plan succeeded.
although / even thougha full clauseEven though it was risky, we tried.
whereas / whilea contrasting clauseCities are busy, whereas villages are calm.
howevera new sentence (; however,)It is cheap; however, it is slow.
neverthelessa new sentence ("in spite of that")She lost. Nevertheless, she smiled.
on the other handthe opposite side of an argumentIt saves time. On the other hand, it costs more.

See the difference — the same facts, written two ways:

Basic linking ✗Confident B2 writing ✓
Online learning is cheap. It is flexible. It can feel lonely. Classrooms are social. They cost more. I like online learning. Online learning is cheap and flexible. Even though it can feel lonely, most students adapt. Despite the higher cost, classrooms offer real contact, whereas online courses rely on a screen. On balance, I still prefer studying online.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different concession connectors from the table, and match each to the right grammar (noun vs clause).
  • Punctuate linking adverbs correctly: "It is popular; however, it is expensive."

Paragraph structure (For-and-Against)

A balanced "for-and-against" paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningName the topic and say it has two sides.Working from home has clear advantages, although it is not for everyone.
2. BodyGive the advantages, then concede the drawbacks with concession links (despite, even though, whereas, however).It saves commuting time; however, it can blur work and rest. Whereas an office keeps you focused, home is full of distractions.
3. ClosingWeigh both sides and give your view with On balance, ...On balance, the freedom outweighs the drawbacks for me.

Task (Görev)

Write a balanced paragraph that argues for and against a topic you know well — for example: working from home, social media, living in a big city, or banning phones in schools. Present both sides fairly, then give your own view.

Sample Answer (~185 words) There are strong arguments on both sides of the debate about working from home, and it is worth weighing them up carefully before deciding.

On the positive side, remote work saves hours of commuting and offers real flexibility. Despite the isolation it can cause, many people concentrate far better at home, whereas a busy office is full of interruptions. Moreover, the money saved on travel can offset the cost of a good home office.

On the other hand, the drawbacks are genuine. Even though the freedom is appealing, the line between work and rest often disappears. Despite good intentions, some workers struggle with loneliness, and there is a growing disparity between those with a quiet space and those without one. However, most of these problems can be mitigated with clear routines.

On balance, I believe the advantages outweigh the drawbacks. Although working from home is not perfect, it is a fair compromise: with a little discipline, the flexibility it offers is, for me, well worth the trade-off.
