Koşul cümleleri iki parçadan oluşur: şart (if-clause) ve sonuç (main clause). Hangi tipin kullanılacağını tek bir soru belirler: bu durum gerçek/olası mı, yoksa hayalî/imkânsız mı? Gerçeklikten uzaklaştıkça fiil zamanı bir adım "geriye" kayar. Aşağıdaki tablo tüm tipleri tek bakışta gösterir.

Conditional sentences have two parts: the condition (if-clause) and the result (main clause). A single question decides which type to use: is the situation real/likely, or imaginary/impossible? The further you move from reality, the more the verb tense shifts one step "back". The table below shows every type at a glance.

Koşul Cümlesi Nedir, Neden Önemli?

Koşul cümleleri (conditionals), bir olayın başka bir koşula bağlı olduğunu anlatır: "eğer şu olursa, bu olur." Türkçede bunu büyük ölçüde "-se/-sa" ekiyle çözeriz; İngilizce ise koşulun gerçeklik derecesini fiil zamanlarıyla kodlar. İşte bu yüzden İngilizce öğrenen Türk öğrenciler için conditionals hem çok mantıklı hem de sık hata kaynağıdır: mesele "if" kelimesi değil, iki cümledeki fiillerin doğru eşleşmesidir.

Her koşul cümlesi iki parçadan oluşur: şart cümlesi (if-clause) ve sonuç cümlesi (main clause). Doğru tipi seçmek için tek bir soru yeter: bu durum gerçek/olası mı, yoksa hayalî/imkânsız mı? Gerçeğe yakınsa fiiller normal zamanında kalır; gerçekten uzaklaştıkça fiil bir ya da iki adım "geriye" kayar. Bu geriye kayma (backshift), aslında bir zaman değil bir mesafe işaretidir — "bu gerçek değil, kurguluyorum" demenin İngilizcedeki yoludur. Bu bölümde beş temel tipi tek tek tanıyacak, mantığını kavrayacak ve en sık hataları göreceksin.

What Is a Conditional, and Why Does It Matter?

Conditionals describe how one event depends on another condition: "if this happens, that happens." In Turkish we mostly handle this with the "-se/-sa" suffix; English, on the other hand, encodes the degree of reality of the condition through verb tenses. That is exactly why, for Turkish learners of English, conditionals are both highly logical and a frequent source of error: the point is not the word "if", but the correct matching of the verbs in the two clauses.

Every conditional has two parts: the if-clause (if-clause) and the result clause (main clause). To choose the right type, one question is enough: is the situation real/likely, or imaginary/impossible? If it is close to reality, the verbs stay in their normal tense; the further it moves from reality, the more the verb shifts one or two steps "back". This backshift is really a marker not of time but of distance — it is English's way of saying "this isn't real, I'm imagining it". In this section you'll get to know the five core types one by one, grasp the logic behind them, and see the most common mistakes.

The table below shows at a glance which tense pairs with which across the two clauses of each conditional type. This is the most critical point in conditionals: the wrong match distorts the meaning of the sentence. Use the table like a "recipe" — first choose the meaning (real, imaginary, or past), then apply the tense pair in the relevant row.

Order is flexible

The two clauses can swap places. If if comes first, a comma goes between them; if it comes last, no comma is needed: I will stay home if it rains.

Here's the subtlety to watch for: the verb form in the result clause can differ from the form you'd use for the same event in a plain sentence. In the First conditional, for example, the result refers to the future but the if-clause stays present; in the Second conditional, even though both clauses "look past", they actually talk about the present or the future. This apparent contradiction comes from English encoding unreality with the past tense — we unpack the logic of this in the sections that follow.

Her Tipi Yakından

Zero Conditional — genel gerçekler, bilimsel kanunlar ve her zaman geçerli neden-sonuç ilişkileri. Hem şart hem sonuç Present Simple'dır ve burada if, çoğu zaman when ile değiştirilebilir çünkü sonuç kesindir. If you mix blue and yellow, you get green. (Maviyle sarıyı karıştırırsan yeşil elde edersin.) Bu tip bir "ihtimal" değil, bir "kural" anlatır.

First Conditional — gerçek, olası bir gelecek. Şart makul biçimde gerçekleşebilir ve sonuç da buna bağlıdır. Şart cümlesi Present Simple, sonuç will + V1. If it rains tomorrow, we will cancel the picnic. (Yarın yağmur yağarsa pikniği iptal ederiz.) Sonuçta will yerine may, might, can, should ya da bir emir de gelebilir: If you see her, tell her to call me.

Second Conditional — şimdiki ya da geleceğe dair hayalî, gerçek dışı durumlar; dilek ve varsayımlar. Şart Past Simple, sonuç would + V1. Geçmiş biçim burada gerçek geçmiş değil, "gerçek değil" işaretidir. If I won the lottery, I would travel the world. (Piyangoyu kazansam dünyayı gezerdim — ama kazanmam pek olası değil.) Kalıp öneri: If I were you, I'd apologise.

Third Conditional — geçmişte gerçekleşmemiş, artık değiştirilemez durumlar; genellikle pişmanlık ve eleştiri taşır. Şart Past Perfect, sonuç would have + V3. If I had studied harder, I would have passed. (Daha çok çalışsaydım geçerdim — ama çalışmadım, geçmedim.) Geçmiş bitti; bu tip "keşke şöyle olsaydı" duygusunu kurgular.

Second · hayalî şimdi

Şu ana ya da geleceğe dair, gerçek olmayan bir durum. Şart Past Simple, sonuç would + V1. If I had a car, I would drive. (Arabam yok ama olsaydı…)

↔
Third · hayalî geçmiş

Geçmişte olmamış, artık değiştirilemez bir durum; pişmanlık. Şart Past Perfect, sonuç would have + V3. If I had had a car, I would have driven.

Mixed Conditional — iki zamanı köprüler: genellikle geçmişteki bir şartın bugüne etkisi. Şart Past Perfect, sonuç would + V1 (şimdiki sonuç). If I had saved money, I would be rich now. (Para biriktirseydim şimdi zengin olurdum.) Tersi de mümkündür: şimdiki bir durumun geçmişteki sonucu.

Wishes (wish / if only) — teknik olarak koşul cümlesi olmasa da aynı "gerçek dışı" mantığı kullanır ve pişmanlık/özlem anlatır. Şimdiki dilek için wish + Past, geçmiş pişmanlık için wish + Past Perfect. I wish I knew the answer. (Keşke cevabı bilsem.) · I wish I had called earlier. (Keşke daha erken arasaydım.)

Örüntüyü fark et

Gerçeklikten uzaklaştıkça fiil geriye kayar: gerçek → Present, hayalî şimdi → Past, hayalî geçmiş → Past Perfect. Tipleri ezberlemek yerine bu tek örüntüyü içselleştirmek, beş tipi de otomatik doğru kurmanı sağlar.

Each Type Up Close

Zero Conditional — general truths, scientific laws and cause-and-effect relationships that always hold. Both the condition and the result are in the Present Simple, and here if can often be replaced by when because the result is certain. If you mix blue and yellow, you get green. (Mix blue and yellow, and you get green.) This type describes not a "possibility" but a "rule".

First Conditional — a real, likely future. The condition can plausibly come true, and the result depends on it. The if-clause is Present Simple, the result will + V1. If it rains tomorrow, we will cancel the picnic. (If it rains tomorrow, we'll call off the picnic.) In the result, instead of will you can also use may, might, can, should or even an imperative: If you see her, tell her to call me.

Second Conditional — imaginary, unreal situations about the present or future; wishes and hypotheses. The condition is Past Simple, the result would + V1. The past form here is not a real past but a marker for "not real". If I won the lottery, I would travel the world. (I would travel the world if I won the lottery — but I'm not really likely to win.) A set phrase for advice: If I were you, I'd apologise.

Third Conditional — situations that didn't happen in the past and can no longer be changed; it usually carries regret and criticism. The condition is Past Perfect, the result would have + V3. If I had studied harder, I would have passed. (I would have passed if I had studied harder — but I didn't study, so I didn't pass.) The past is over; this type frames the feeling of "if only things had been different".

Second · imaginary present

A situation about the present or future that isn't real. The condition is Past Simple, the result would + V1. If I had a car, I would drive. (I don't have a car, but if I did…)

↔
Third · imaginary past

A situation that didn't happen in the past and can no longer be changed; regret. The condition is Past Perfect, the result would have + V3. If I had had a car, I would have driven.

Mixed Conditional — bridges two time frames: usually the effect of a past condition on today. The condition is Past Perfect, the result would + V1 (a present result). If I had saved money, I would be rich now. (If I had saved money, I would be rich now.) The reverse is also possible: the past consequence of a present situation.

Wishes (wish / if only) — although technically not conditionals, they use the same "unreal" logic and express regret/longing. For a present wish, wish + Past; for past regret, wish + Past Perfect. I wish I knew the answer. (I wish I knew the answer.) · I wish I had called earlier. (I wish I had called earlier.)

Notice the pattern

The further you move from reality, the further the verb shifts back: real → Present, imaginary present → Past, imaginary past → Past Perfect. Instead of memorising the types, internalising this single pattern lets you build all five correctly and automatically.

From Reality to Imagination — The Distance Axis

Instead of memorising the conditional types, think of them as a degree of reality. The further away it is, the further the verb shifts "back" (this is the principle of "backshift" in English). The four boxes below show this axis from the most certain to the most impossible: Zero on the left represents an absolute truth, and Third on the right an unchangeable past. Where you place a sentence on this axis directly determines which verb tense you choose.

Zero · Certain
Law of nature, routine — always the case
First · Likely
A future that can happen — reasonable probability
Second · Imaginary
Not real right now — hypothesis/wish
Third · Impossible
The past is over, unchangeable — regret

The Logic: Why Does the Tense Shift?

Many learners get stuck on "why am I using the Past Simple for the future?" The answer is this: English uses the past tense not only for "what happened earlier" but also for "what isn't real / is distant". So the past in the Second conditional is a marker not of time but of unreality. Once you grasp this single idea, the four cases below become parts of one and the same logic.

Real = normal tense

If the situation is real/likely (Zero, First), the verbs stay in their ordinary tense.

If she calls, I answer.

Imaginary = one step back

To imagine the present, the past is used (the unreal past). It's a marker of "distance", not "time".

If I were you… (were, with all subjects)

Imaginary past = two steps back

To imagine the past, the past perfect. The result is would have + V3.

If I had known, I would have come.

Mixed = time bridge

The effect of a past condition on today (or the reverse) is built with the Mixed conditional.

If I had slept, I wouldn't be tired now.

Resmî ve İleri Kullanımlar

Temel beş tipi kavradıktan sonra, akademik ve resmî İngilizcede karşına çıkacak birkaç ileri yapı seni bir üst seviyeye taşır. Bunlar aynı mantığı taşır ama daha zarif bir biçim sunar.

Inversion (if düşürme). Resmî üslupta if atılıp yardımcı fiil öne alınabilir. Anlam aynı kalır, ton yükselir. If I had known… → Had I known… · If you should need help… → Should you need help… · If it were true… → Were it true… Bu yapı özellikle yazılı, resmî metinlerde görülür.

should ile ihtimal düşürme. First conditional'a should eklemek şartı biraz daha "uzak ihtimal" yapar: If you should see him, give him my regards. (Şayet onu görürsen…) were to kalıbı ise hayalî bir geleceği dramatize eder: If the sun were to disappear, life would end.

Noktalama ve sıra. İki cümlenin yeri serbesttir. if baştaysa iki cümle arasına virgül konur; sonda ise virgül gerekmez. If it rains, we'll stay in. ↔ We'll stay in if it rains. Bu küçük ama sınavlarda ve yazıda fark yaratan bir kuraldır.

Formal and Advanced Uses

Once you've grasped the five core types, a few advanced structures you'll meet in academic and formal English will take you to the next level. They carry the same logic but offer a more elegant form.

Inversion (dropping if). In a formal style, if can be dropped and the auxiliary moved to the front. The meaning stays the same, the tone rises. If I had known… → Had I known… · If you should need help… → Should you need help… · If it were true… → Were it true… This structure is seen especially in written, formal texts.

Lowering the probability with should. Adding should to a First conditional makes the condition a slightly more "remote possibility": If you should see him, give him my regards. (If you happen to see him…) The were to pattern, meanwhile, dramatises an imaginary future: If the sun were to disappear, life would end.

Punctuation and order. The position of the two clauses is free. If if is at the start, a comma goes between the two clauses; if it's at the end, no comma is needed. If it rains, we'll stay in. ↔ We'll stay in if it rains. It's a small rule, but one that makes a difference in exams and in writing.

Bu bağlaçlar arasındaki ince farkı sezmek konuşmana doğallık katar: as long as ve provided that bir izin şartı koyar, in case ise henüz olmamış bir ihtimale karşı önlem alır. İkincisini gelecek anlamıyla karıştırma: in case cümlesinde present kullanılır, çünkü tedbir "şimdiden" alınır.

Beyond if: Alternative Conditional Connectors

A condition isn't always built with if. Other connectors that carry the same logic add nuance and variety to a sentence; they are also tested frequently in exams.

ConnectorMeaningExample
unlessif notYou'll fail unless you study.
as long as / provided thatonly on condition that …You can go as long as you're back by ten.
in caseas a precaution against …Take an umbrella in case it rains.
even ifeven if …I'll go even if it snows.
suppose / imaginelet's say (hypothetical)Suppose you lost your job — what then?

watch out with unless

unless already means "if … not"; don't use a second negative inside it. Not Unless you don't hurry, but Unless you hurry, you'll be late.

Sensing the subtle difference between these connectors adds naturalness to your speech: as long as and provided that set a condition of permission, while in case takes a precaution against a possibility that hasn't happened yet. Don't confuse the latter with a future meaning: an in case clause uses the present, because the precaution is taken "in advance".

Sık Yapılan Hatalar

if-clause içinde "will" olmaz

If it will rain… yanlış. Şart cümlesinde gelecek anlamı present ile verilir: If it rains, we'll cancel.

Second'da "was" değil "were"

Resmî/standart kullanımda tüm öznelerde were: If I were rich… Kalıp ifade: If I were you, I'd…

if yerine alternatifler

unless (= if not), as long as, provided that, in case de koşul kurar. Ayrıca wish / if only ile pişmanlık ifade edilir.

would'u iki cümleye birden koymak

would yalnızca sonuç cümlesine gelir, şart cümlesine değil. If I would have time… yanlış; doğrusu If I had time, I would help. Bu, Türk öğrencilerin Second ve Third'de en çok yaptığı hatadır.

Common Mistakes

no "will" inside the if-clause

If it will rain… is wrong. In the if-clause, future meaning is expressed with the present: If it rains, we'll cancel.

In the Second, "were" not "was"

In formal/standard usage, were with all subjects: If I were rich… A set phrase: If I were you, I'd…

alternatives to if

unless (= if not), as long as, provided that, in case also build conditions. Regret is expressed with wish / if only as well.

putting "would" in both clauses

would goes only in the result clause, not in the if-clause. If I would have time… is wrong; the correct form is If I had time, I would help. This is the mistake Turkish learners make most often in the Second and Third conditionals.

How Should You Study Conditionals?

Trying to memorise conditionals as five separate patterns is tiring and error-prone. Instead, grasping a single logic — reality = distance — and practising on top of it is far more lasting:

Start with the reality question

In every sentence, ask "is this real/likely, or imaginary?" The answer determines the type on its own.

real → present · unreal → past

Visualise the backshift

On a timeline, shift the verb one step back; two steps back = imaginary past (Third).

have → had → had had

Build from your own life

For each type, write a real sentence of your own: a real plan, a real wish, a real regret.

If I had more time, I would…

Move on to the mixed type

Once you've settled the five types, reinforce with Mixed and wishes; these are extensions of the same logic.

If I had slept, I wouldn't be tired.

One final piece of advice: so you don't hesitate when speaking, make the two patterns you'll use most — First (a real plan) and Second (an imaginary situation) — automatic. These two are the backbone of everyday conversation; structures like the Third, Mixed and inversion, meanwhile, let you shine in writing and at an advanced level. After learning each new type, compare it with the previous one, show the difference in a single pair of examples, and then consolidate it by working through questions.

"In conditionals, the verb tense answers not "when" but "how real". The further you move from reality, the further back you shift the verb — the rule is that simple; the rest is practice.
