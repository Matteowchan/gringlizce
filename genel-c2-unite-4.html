Grammar: Cleft Mastery

A cleft is a spotlight. At C2 you stop asking "is this correct?" and start asking "which part of my sentence deserves the light — and which cleft aims it there?"

A cleft is a spotlight, not a decoration

An ordinary sentence spreads its attention evenly: "Courage saved the city." A cleft sentence refuses that evenness. It splits one clause into two — a frame and a focus — so that a single element is thrust into the light while everything else recedes into shared, assumed background. "It was courage that saved the city" says the same thing, yet now one word carries the whole weight of the claim.

This is information management, the defining C2 skill. A cleft lets you presuppose what the reader already accepts and assert only the point in contention. It lets you contrast what is true against what is false in one balanced stroke. It lets you push new, weighty information to the stressed end of the sentence, where the ear expects it. The mastery is not knowing that clefts exist — it is choosing, for each rhetorical moment, which cleft aims the light exactly where you want it.

  • It was the map that saved us, not the guide.it-cleft → spotlights "the map" and rejects a rival ("the guide").
  • What we need is more time.wh-cleft → pushes the new element ("more time") to the stressed end.
  • Peace is what the people demanded.reversed wh-cleft → fronts the focus for rhetorical weight.
  • All I want is a fair hearing.all-cleft → restricts and plays down: "nothing more than".

1. The it-cleft (It + be + focus + that/who-clause)

The it-cleft is the sharpest focusing tool in English. Its frame is fixed: It + be + FOCUS + that/who-clause. Whatever you drop into the focus slot — a subject, an object, a time, a place, an adverbial — is spotlighted; the rest is presupposed. Its natural home is correction and contrast: It was X, not Y, that...

Plain sentenceIt-cleft (focus in bold)What is spotlighted
Sara broke the vase.It was Sara who broke the vase.the subject — who did it, not what happened.
They met in Vienna.It was in Vienna that they met.the place — an adverbial in focus.
The noise frightened her.It was the noise, not the dark, that frightened her.contrastive focus — asserting X, rejecting Y.
C2 subtlety
  • The verb in the frame is nearly always was/is; the tense of the real event lives in the that-clause: "It was Sara who broke it."
  • Use who for people, that for everything else — but that is acceptable for people too: "It was Sara that broke it."

2. The wh-cleft / pseudo-cleft (What... is/are...)

The wh-cleft (or pseudo-cleft) works the opposite way round. It opens with a what-clause that packages the given ground, then delivers the new element at the stressed end — the natural resting place of new information. Its frame is What + clause + be + FOCUS. Where the it-cleft points backwards to correct, the wh-cleft builds forwards to reveal.

Plain sentenceWh-cleft (focus at the end)Effect
We need a clear deadline.What we need is a clear deadline.holds "what we need" as given, lands the new point last.
She admires his honesty.What she admires is his honesty.delays and therefore stresses "his honesty".
They did nothing.What they did was nothing at all.builds suspense before the deflating focus.
C2 subtlety — verb match
  • The what-clause takes a singular verb; the linking verb then agrees with the focus: "What matters is honesty" but "What matters are the details."
  • To focus an action, use a "do"-frame: "What she did was (to) resign."

3. The family: reversed, all- and inferential clefts

Beyond the two core clefts lies a family of variants, each tuned to a different rhetorical effect. Learn to reach for the one whose colour fits the moment.

TypeFrame & exampleWhen to reach for it
reversed wh-cleftFOCUS + be + what-clause — "Peace is what we want."to front the value and make the line ring; oratory, slogans.
all-cleftAll + clause + be + FOCUS — "All I want is a fair hearing."to restrict and downplay: "nothing more than this".
inferential cleftIt's not that... ; it's that... — "It's not that I doubt you; it's that I doubt the plan."to correct a whole inference, not a single word.
C2 subtlety
  • The reversed wh-cleft states a contrast plainly; the it-cleft dramatises it. "Peace is what we want" asserts; "It is peace, not war, that we want" argues.
  • The inferential cleft never focuses a noun — it reframes the reader's assumption about the situation as a whole.

4. Choosing the cleft: given, new and end-focus

Mastery is selection. Every cleft rearranges the flow of given and new information, and the effect depends on what you place where. English prefers given information first, new information last — the principle of end-focus. The cleft you choose should serve that flow, or break it deliberately for emphasis.

Your rhetorical goalThe cleft to chooseWhy it works
correct a mistaken beliefit-cleft with "not Y"spotlights the truth and names the error in one move.
reveal a new, weighty point lastwh-cleftgiven ground first, new element at the stressed end.
make a value resoundreversed wh-cleftfronts the focus for slogan-like force.
play something downall-cleftlimits the claim to "nothing more than".
reframe a whole assumptioninferential cleftcorrects the reader's inference, not one noun.

5. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

Even fluent C2 writers trip over clefts. Each row below is a failure of control — a broken frame, a wrong focus, or a mismatched verb. Audit your drafts against every one.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
What I need it is more time.What I need is more time.a stray resumptive "it" breaks the wh-cleft frame.
What matters most are honesty.What matters most is honesty.the singular focus "honesty" needs a singular verb.
It was because I was tired why I left.It was because I was tired that I left.the it-cleft uses "that", never "why", after the focus.
That I need is a break.What I need is a break.a wh-cleft must begin with "what", not "that".
It was to rest what I wanted.It was rest that I wanted. / What I wanted was to rest.don't blend an it-cleft frame with a "what"-clause.

6. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

A speechwriter drafts a closing line; a senior editor coaches the cleft choice. Read it twice and watch how each suggestion re-aims the spotlight — and why each cleft fits the rhetorical moment.

W:My draft says: "We achieved this through unity."Unmarked: true, but the light is spread thin.
E:The whole speech has been about division. Spotlight the cure: "It was unity that carried us through."it-cleft — throws the light onto the one value that matters.
W:And if I want to deny that luck played a part?The writer wants contrast, not just focus.
E:Then add the antithesis: "It was unity, not luck, that carried us through."the "not Y" frame corrects a rival explanation in one stroke.
W:For the very last line I want something that rings.Now the goal is resonance, not correction.
E:Front the value: "Unity is what saved us." Save one cleft for the climax — a speech built of nothing but clefts exhausts the ear.reversed wh-cleft for the closing ring; restraint for the rest.
Test yourself
  • Why does the editor prefer "It was unity, not luck, that carried us through" to a plain sentence? (The it-cleft spotlights the true cause and rejects a false rival in one balanced move.)
  • What changes when "It was unity that saved us" becomes "Unity is what saved us"? (Fronting the focus in a reversed wh-cleft gives the line slogan-like resonance for a climax.)
  • Rewrite the first line as an all-cleft to play the claim down. ("All that carried us through was unity" — the same focus, but modest rather than triumphant.)

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Skilled readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think of a sentence you have deliberately arranged so one word landed hardest. What did you move to the front, or hold for the end?
  • The text profiles a speechwriter famous for cleft sentences. Do you expect her to use them constantly, or to save them for rare moments?
  • Writers sometimes show the same idea "plain" and then "clefted" to prove a point. Watch for the moment the text does this, and ask what it reveals.
How to read
  • First read: read quickly for the whole argument — what Okonkwo is famous for and why the cleft matters. Don't stop for every word.
  • Second read: track the cleft structures — it-clefts ("It was courage that..."), wh-clefts ("What every great speech needs is..."), reversed clefts ("Justice is what..."), and the formal connectors that stitch the argument together. Ask what each choice does.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): cleft · foreground · emphasis · presuppose · salient · juxtapose · rhetorical

The Sentence That Points

For thirty years, the speeches that have moved parliaments and swayed juries have borne the invisible fingerprint of Adaeze Okonkwo. She has never sought the podium herself; nonetheless, the lines she crafts have a way of lodging in the memory long after the applause dies. Her secret is deceptively small: the cleft sentence — the deliberate splitting of a clause so that one idea is thrust into the spotlight while everything else recedes into shared, assumed ground.

It is a strange sort of fame, this fame of the ghost. Ministers who could not identify her in a crowded room have wept over cadences she assembled at a kitchen table the night before; barristers have watched juries turn on the pivot of a phrase she scribbled between hearings. Colleagues describe her method as a kind of listening rather than writing. Before she drafts a syllable, they say, she reads the room she will never enter — the fatigue of a legislature at the end of a long session, the wariness of jurors who have already been lied to twice that week — and only then does she decide which single word the whole occasion must be made to carry. The rest, she maintains, is mere carpentry: sturdy, necessary, and best left unnoticed.

Consider her most quoted line. A plain writer would say, "Courage saved the city." Okonkwo wrote, "It was courage that carried them, not luck." The proposition is identical; the effect is not. By fronting "courage" inside an it-cleft and juxtaposing it against "luck", she foregrounds one value and denies its rival in a single balanced stroke. Every emphasis, she insists, is an argument.

The claim sounds like paradox until one examines the grammar beneath it. An ordinary declarative sentence, she observes, distributes its attention with a democratic evenness that no orator can afford; each element receives roughly equal light, and the listener, given no instruction about what matters, supplies emphasis of his own — often the wrong one. A cleft revokes that freedom. It divides a single proposition into two clauses, one that quietly presupposes what everyone already grants and one that asserts, alone and unmistakable, the point in contention. To choose the focus, therefore, is to choose the reader's conclusion before he has consciously reached it; the syntax has already voted on his behalf. This is why she distrusts writers who treat emphasis as decoration. Emphasis, in her grammar, is not the ribbon on the argument but the argument itself, smuggled in through the back door of word order while the front door busies the mind with content.

What every great speech needs, she argues, is a single, memorable focus — and the cleft is how you build one. Where an ordinary sentence lets attention wander, a cleft presupposes the common ground and asserts only the salient point. "Justice is what the people demanded," she once wrote for a grieving mayor; the reversed order places the value first and the crowd's demand second, and the sentence rings like a bell.

She keeps, for teaching purposes, a small taxonomy of the forms she reaches for, and she insists that each has its proper occasion, as a joiner keeps chisels of different widths. The it-cleft, with its fixed frame of it was X that, she reserves for correction and contrast: it points backwards, isolates the true cause, and, where a rival lurks, dismisses it in the same breath. The wh-cleft — what we need is — does the opposite work; it opens with the ground already shared and reserves the new, weighty element for the sentence's stressed end, where the ear has been trained since childhood to expect the important word to fall. There is also the all-cleft, which restricts and plays a claim down — all I ask is a fair hearing — conceding smallness the better to seem reasonable. And there is the inferential cleft, subtlest of all, which corrects not a word but a whole assumption a listener did not know he had made. Mastery, she says, is not the ability to form these structures but the judgement to know, in a given rhetorical instant, which one aims the light exactly where the moment demands.

Yet Okonkwo is no lover of ornament for its own sake. On the contrary, she warns young writers against overuse: a speech built entirely of clefts grows exhausting, whereas a single cleft, placed at the climax, can define a career. Moreover, she notes, a mismatched cleft — one whose verb quietly disagrees with its focus — betrays an untrained hand at once.

Her workshops, held twice a year in a borrowed lecture room, have acquired the reputation of an ordeal. She will take a promising apprentice's paragraph, strip out every cleft but one, and demand a defence of the survivor: why this word, why this moment, why not a plain sentence that would have done less harm? Most drafts, she finds, are guilty of the same vanity — the writer has enjoyed the device so thoroughly that he has forgotten the audience, spotlighting six ideas in a row until, predictably, none of them is lit at all. Restraint, she tells them, is the whole art; the difference between a speaker the chamber remembers and one it merely endures is often a matter of the clefts withheld rather than the clefts deployed. And she is merciless about the technical fault that no rhetorical brilliance can excuse: a focus and a verb that fail to agree, a frame begun and then abandoned, a resumptive pronoun left stranded in a clause that no longer needs it. Such slips, she says, announce the amateur before the argument has even been heard. A brilliant idea trapped inside a broken frame, she is fond of remarking, persuades nobody; the listener hears only the fracture, and the meaning bleeds out through it. She keeps a private ledger of these failures — the stray "it" that clogs a wh-cleft, the "why" smuggled in where only "that" belongs, the plural focus yoked to a stubbornly singular verb — and she reads from it aloud, without malice but without mercy either, until the room has learned to hear the fault before it commits it.

Meanwhile, her slim guide to rhetorical structure circulates among speechwriters like contraband. It offers one discipline above all: decide what the audience already knows, decide what you want them to feel, and then let the sentence point. All that remains, she writes, is the vote — and the handful of words that shaped it.

Those who have watched a debate turn on one of her lines describe a curious hush — the half-second in which a room recognises that it has been shown exactly where to look. It is a modest kind of power, invisible to everyone but the writer who arranged it, and Okonkwo seems content that it remain so. Fame, after all, would require the podium she has spent a lifetime avoiding. She would rather be the pressure behind the sentence than the face above it, the hand that turns the syntax a single degree so that a whole assembly, without quite knowing why, leans in the direction she chose for it before she ever put down her pen.

1. The writer says "Every emphasis, she insists, is an argument." In your own words, what does that mean for a writer who uses clefts?

Sample Answer"It means that where you aim the spotlight is never neutral. When a cleft foregrounds one element, it quietly presupposes the rest and asks the reader to accept it as given; the very act of choosing a focus makes a claim about what matters most. So every cleft is a small argument about importance, not just a stylistic flourish."

2. Why do you think the writer contrasts "Courage saved the city" with "It was courage that carried them, not luck"? What does the comparison prove?

Sample Answer"The two versions carry the same proposition, yet only the cleft foregrounds 'courage' and, through the 'not luck' antithesis, denies a rival explanation. By setting them side by side, the writer proves that the cleft adds no new facts but changes the emphasis and therefore the effect — which is the whole argument of the piece: form directs meaning."

Sample Answer"I agree, because a device that is meant to be salient stops being salient once everything uses it. When I once tried to stress every point in a presentation with heavy emphasis, the audience simply stopped noticing; the moment I saved my strongest phrasing for a single line, that line landed. Emphasis, like a spotlight, only works against darkness."

Writing: Persuade with the Spotlight

Write a short persuasive piece that deliberately deploys clefts to focus, contrast and emphasise. Your teacher will review it.

Clefts as rhetorical tools (A Quick Map)

At C1 you learned to build clefts; at C2 you learn to deploy the right cleft for the right effect. Persuasion lives on emphasis, and the cleft is your instrument for aiming it. Below is the working map you will draw on for the task.

CleftRhetorical effectModel line
it-cleftspotlight one elementIt is the library, above all, that binds this town.
it-cleft + "not Y"correct by contrastIt was neglect, not cost, that closed the doors.
wh-cleftreveal the point at the endWhat this community needs is a place to gather.
reversed wh-cleftmake a value ringAccess is what we are truly defending.
all-cleftplay a claim down / concedeAll we are asking for is a single year.
inferential cleftreframe an assumptionIt's not that the town is poor; it's that its priorities have drifted.
Rule
  • Use each cleft for a distinct job — do not repeat the same type. Variety is what keeps emphasis alive.
  • Ration your clefts: reserve the strongest (the reversed wh-cleft) for your climax. A paragraph of nothing but clefts exhausts the reader.

Sample Answer (~225 words) Friends, let me be plain about why our library stands empty tonight. It was neglect, not cost, that closed its doors — the antithesis matters, because the council keeps telling us the building was simply unaffordable. It wasn't. It was three years of quiet indifference that let the roof leak and the readers drift away.

What this town actually needs is not a grand new project but the one it already owns: a warm, free room where a child can read and a pensioner can escape the cold. That is the salient fact, and I want to spotlight it. All we are asking for is a single year of proper funding — nothing more, nothing grander.

And let me foreground one thing the sceptics miss. It's not that we cannot afford the library; it's that we have not yet chosen to. A budget is a rhetorical document too: it argues, through its emphasis, about what a community values. Ours has quietly argued that reading does not matter.

So let us change the emphasis. Juxtapose the cost of a year's heating against the cost of a generation that never learned to love a book, and the choice makes itself. Access is what we are truly defending. Access is what we will not surrender.
