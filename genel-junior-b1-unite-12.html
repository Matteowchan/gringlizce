Grammar: Relative Clauses

Study the rules, look at the examples, then do the exercises.

Joining two ideas into one sentence

A relative clause gives more information about a noun (a person, thing or place). Instead of writing two short sentences, we join them with a relative pronoun: who, which, that, whose, where or when. This is one of the most useful skills at B1, because it makes your speaking and writing sound smooth and grown-up.

Look at how two sentences become one:

  • I know a girl. She can speak three languages.Two short sentences.
  • I know a girl who can speak three languages.One smooth sentence → relative clause with "who".
  • This is the park. We first played here.Two short sentences.
  • This is the park where we first played.One sentence → relative clause with "where".

1. Choosing the relative pronoun (who / which / that / whose / where / when)

The pronoun you choose depends on the kind of noun you are describing. Learn this table well — it answers most exam questions.

PronounUse it for...Example
whopeopleThe boy who lives next door is my best friend.
whichthings / animalsThis is the toy which makes funny sounds.
thatpeople or things (informal)The story that won the prize is here.
whosepossession (belonging)She's the girl whose drawings are famous.
whereplacesThat's the park where I learned to ride my bike.
whentimesI'll never forget the day when we met.
Key point
  • that can replace who or which in a defining clause: The story which / that won... Both are correct.
  • Use whose to show that something belongs to the noun: the child whose bag was lost (= the child's bag).

2. Defining relative clauses (essential information)

A defining relative clause tells you which person or thing we mean. Without it, the sentence does not make full sense. We do NOT use commas.

  • The children who live next door are very friendly.Which children? The ones next door → essential, no commas.
  • The cartoon that we watched last night was funny.Which cartoon? The one we watched → essential.
Test
  • Cover the relative clause. If the sentence loses its meaning ("The children ... are very friendly" — which children?), it is defining → no commas.

3. Non-defining relative clauses (extra information)

A non-defining relative clause adds extra information that we could remove. The sentence still makes sense without it. We put it between commas, and we do NOT use that.

  • My brother, who is only six, loves dinosaurs.We already know who my brother is; the clause is extra → commas, use "who" (not "that").
  • Istanbul, which has millions of people, is a huge city.Extra fact about Istanbul → commas, "which".
Golden rule
  • Non-defining clause = commas + who / which / whose / where. Never use "that" in a non-defining clause. My bike, which is red, ... ✓ — My bike, that is red, ... ✗
  • Defining clause = no commas, and that is allowed.

4. Defining vs non-defining (the comma changes the meaning)

The two commas are not decoration — they can change what the sentence means. Compare:

SentenceMeaning
My sister who lives in Berlin is very tall.I have more than one sister; I mean the Berlin one. (defining, no commas)
My sister, who lives in Berlin, is very tall.I have only one sister; "in Berlin" is just extra news. (non-defining, commas)

5. Leaving out the relative pronoun (the object relative)

In a defining clause, if the relative pronoun is the object of the verb, we can leave it out. This is very common in natural English.

  • The book (that) I read yesterday was great."I read the book" → the pronoun is the object → we can drop it.
  • The girl (who) we met at the party is my cousin."We met the girl" → object → can be dropped.
  • The boy who lives here is friendly. ✗ (cannot drop)Here "who" is the subject of "lives" → you must keep it.
Quick test
  • Is there another subject right after the pronoun (the book that I read)? Then the pronoun is the object → you can drop it.
  • Is a verb right after the pronoun (the boy who lives)? Then it is the subject → you must keep it.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B1 students make most often with relative clauses. Check your own writing against this list.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
My grandma, that tells great stories, is kind.My grandma, who tells great stories, is kind.non-defining clause → never use "that".
My bike which is old still works.My bike, which is old, still works.extra information → needs two commas.
She's the girl who's dog won.She's the girl whose dog won."whose" = possession; "who's" = who is.
This is the house where I grew up in it.This is the house where I grew up."where" already means "in that place"; no extra "in it".
The friend which called you is here.The friend who called you is here.use "who" (or "that") for people, not "which".

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two friends look at old photos and describe the people and places in them. Read it twice and notice each relative clause — which are defining (no commas) and which are non-defining (commas)?

  • — Who's the boy who is standing next to you?defining "who" (which boy?).
  • — That's Kaan, whose sister used to be in my class.non-defining "whose" (extra info, commas).
  • — And this is the house where we spent every summer."where" for a place.
  • — Nice! Is that the beach (that) you always talk about?object relative → "that" can be dropped.
  • — Yes! It's the place where I learned to swim, in the summer when I turned ten."where" for place, "when" for time.
  • — Your grandmother, who looks so happy here, must have loved it too.non-defining "who" (commas).
Test yourself
  • Find one clause you could leave the pronoun out of. ("the beach (that) you always talk about".)
  • "Your grandmother, who looks so happy here, ..." — why commas? (non-defining: extra info about a person we already know.)
  • Change "That's Kaan, whose sister ..." into a question with whose. (Whose sister used to be in your class?)

Speaking: Describing People, Places & Things

Learn the words, then use them to describe the people and places in your life.

neighbour (n)
a person who lives near you (komşu)
Syn: local · Ant: stranger
"The neighbour who lives above us is very quiet."
classmate (n)
a person who is in your class (sınıf arkadaşı)
Syn: schoolmate · Ant: stranger
"She's a classmate whose ideas I really respect."
relative (n)
a member of your family (akraba)
Syn: family member · Ant: stranger
"I have a relative who lives in Canada."
landmark (n)
a famous building or place that is easy to recognise (simge yapı)
Syn: monument · Ant: —
"The tower is a landmark which you can see for miles."
invention (n)
a new thing that someone has made for the first time (icat)
Syn: creation · Ant: copy
"The phone is an invention that changed the world."
reliable (adj)
that you can trust or depend on (güvenilir)
Syn: dependable · Ant: unreliable
"He's a friend whose word is always reliable."
talented (adj)
having a natural ability to do something well (yetenekli)
Syn: gifted · Ant: talentless
"She's a talented singer who started at ten."
memorable (adj)
easy to remember because it is special (unutulmaz)
Syn: unforgettable · Ant: ordinary
"It was a memorable day when we all met again."
spacious (adj)
large, with a lot of room inside (ferah, geniş)
Syn: roomy · Ant: cramped
"They live in a spacious flat which has a garden."
cosy (adj)
warm, comfortable and pleasant (sıcak, samimi, rahat)
Syn: snug · Ant: cold
"It's a cosy café where I often study."
remote (adj)
far away from towns and people (ıssız, uzak)
Syn: isolated · Ant: central
"We stayed in a remote village where no cars go."
well-known (adj)
that many people know about (tanınmış, meşhur)
Syn: famous · Ant: unknown
"He's a well-known author whose books sell fast."
describe (v)
to say what someone or something is like (tanımlamak, betimlemek)
Syn: portray · Ant: —
"Can you describe the person who helped you?"
belong (v)
to be owned by someone (ait olmak)
Syn: be owned by · Ant: —
"That's the man whose car this belongs to."
Describing people and places: use who, whose and where to add detail.

Reading: The Boy Who Saved the Old House

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think about your grandparents' home. Is there an old place that you would like to save or fix?
  • The title uses a relative clause ("The Boy Who Saved the Old House"). What extra information does "who saved the old house" give?
  • Do you think it is easy or hard to fix a very old house? Guess before you read!
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text for the answers. Underline every relative pronoun (who, which, that, whose, where, when).

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): relative · landmark · reliable · talented · remote · spacious · describe · belong

One old house on a forgotten street, brought back to life.
The Boy Who Saved the Old House

Deniz is a boy who loves old things. Every summer he visits a small, remote village where his grandmother lives. At the end of her quiet street there is an old stone house that has belonged to his family for a hundred years. It is the house where his father grew up, and Deniz had always wanted to see inside it.

When his father was young, the house was full of life. Grandmother loves to describe the summers when all the relatives came to stay, and the neighbour whose garden was full of roses. In those days the house, which was painted a soft yellow, was a little landmark that everyone in the village knew. Deniz loved the old photos where the whole family looked so happy.

But slowly things changed. The family who once filled the rooms moved to the city, and the house that they left behind grew old and grey. The wooden door which led to the garden broke, and birds built nests under the roof. Rain came in whenever there was a storm. It made Deniz sad to stand in the place where his father had been so happy.

One morning Deniz told his grandfather, who was very good with tools, that he wanted to save the old house. His grandfather smiled and said, "This is the home where our family began. Let's bring it back to life." That was the moment when everything started. The house looked almost hopeless. However, Deniz had a clear plan.

The work was not easy, and there were days when Deniz wanted to give up. His friends, who were kind and reliable, came every weekend because they loved the old house too. The oldest boy, whose father could fix almost anything, showed them how to mix the plaster that the old walls needed. They also found a box that was full of tools, and a tin of yellow paint which still worked.

Little by little, the house began to change. They mended the door that was broken, cleaned the big, spacious room upstairs, and painted the walls, which had once been grey, a warm and cheerful yellow. A girl who was very talented at drawing painted flowers around the window.

There were hard days too. One night a storm broke a shutter that they had only just fixed. Another time it rained for a whole week, so nobody could work. On the evening when Deniz felt most tired, his grandmother brought them a plate of warm cookies and said, "This is the street where your family lived. Please don't stop." Those words, which Deniz never forgot, gave him the courage to carry on.

Today the old house is a place where all the children of the family meet. There is a bright room where they read and draw, and a garden where they play until the sun goes down. Deniz still remembers the day when he first opened the mended door. "This is the house where my father grew up," he says, "and now it belongs to all of us."

Writing: A Person or Place That Matters to Me

Write and submit your description; your teacher will review it.

Relative clauses make descriptions richer (Linking Words)

A good description does two things: it adds detail with relative clauses, and it links ideas with connectors. At A2 you used and, but, so, because. At B1 you add also, when, while, although and however, and you use relative clauses to pack more information into each sentence.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
andadd informationShe's a friend who is kind and always honest.
butshow a small contrastMy room, which is small, is cosy, but it has no window.
soshow a resultHe's reliable, so he's the friend who I always ask for help.
becausegive a reasonI love the park because it's a place where I can play.
alsoadd an extra pointShe's talented. She's also a friend whose drawings inspire me.
whensay at what past timeWhen I was little, the village where we lived was quiet.
whiletwo things at the same timeWhile I was there, I met a boy who became my best friend.
althoughshow a strong contrastAlthough the village is remote, it's a place that I love.
howevercontrast between two sentencesThe house was old. However, it was the home where I grew up.

See the difference — the same facts, written two ways:

Separate sentences ✗Connected, fluent writing ✓
I have a friend. Her name is Aylin. She is very funny. She lives in Izmir. Her house is lovely. We spend summers there. I have a friend called Aylin, who is very funny and lives in Izmir. Her house, which is lovely, is the place where we spend every summer.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 relative clauses and at least 3 different connectors. Do not write sentence by sentence; link your ideas.
  • Remember: a non-defining clause needs commas and never uses that: "Aylin, who is very funny, ..."

Description structure (Paragraph Structure)

A description paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same every time — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningIntroduce the person or place with a defining relative clauseThere is a place that means a lot to me.
2. BodyAdd details with more relative clauses. Use non-defining clauses (commas) for extra facts, and link with connectors.The house, which is over a hundred years old, is where my grandmother lived. She was a person whose stories I loved.
3. ClosingA final comment about why it matters nowIt is still the place where I feel most at home.

Task (Görev)

Describe a person, place or thing that is important to you. Use relative clauses to add detail.

WHAT TO INCLUDE

  • an opening sentence that introduces the person or place (with a relative clause)
  • who or what they are, and why they are special to you
  • one non-defining clause with commas (e.g. My grandpa, who is very funny, ...)
  • one sentence with whose (possession) and one with where or when
  • a closing sentence about why this person or place still matters
  • at least 3 relative clauses: who, which, that, whose, where, when
  • at least 3 connectors: and, but, so, because, also, when, while, although, however
  • at least 4 words from the Speaking section (neighbour, classmate, relative, reliable, talented, memorable, spacious, cosy, remote, well-known)

EXAMPLE OPENERS

"There is a person who means a lot to me ..." "The place where I feel happiest is ..." "I want to describe my ..., who ..."

Sample Answer (~165 words) There is a person who means a lot to me: my grandmother, whose small house in a remote village was the centre of my childhood. She was a warm, reliable woman who always had time for everyone. The house, which was old and a little cosy, is the place where I spent every summer holiday.

My grandmother was also a talented cook, and the kitchen was the room where we all gathered. I still remember the mornings when she made fresh bread, because the smell filled the whole street. Although the village was far from any city, it never felt lonely, so I was always happy there.

Sadly, the house now belongs to another family. However, it is still a memorable place that lives in my memory. My grandmother, who taught me so much, is the relative I miss the most, and the village where she lived will always feel like home.
