Grammar: Participle Clauses

Compress two clauses into one and make your writing sound genuinely advanced.

Two ideas, one elegant sentence

A participle clause lets you join two ideas without a second subject or a linking word. Instead of "Because I felt tired, I went to bed," a confident B2 writer says "Feeling tired, I went to bed." The clause is shorter, smoother and noticeably more formal — exactly the register examiners reward.

There are three shapes to master: the present participle (-ing) for active ideas, the past participle (V3) for passive ideas, and the perfect participle (having + V3) for an action that clearly happened first. Get these right and one clean sentence can carry time, cause and consequence at once.

  • Walking home, I saw an old friend.Two things at the same time, active → present participle (-ing).
  • Built in 1890, the bridge still stands.The bridge was built → passive → past participle (V3).
  • Having finished her homework, she went out to play.One action complete before the next → perfect participle.
  • Shocked by the news, he sat down.He was shocked → passive → past participle.

1. The three forms (quick review)

Fix the shape of each participle in your mind before you worry about meaning. Notice that the passive ideas all use a past participle (V3), while the -ing forms stay active.

ParticipleFormExample
Present participleverb + -ingWalking home, I saw a fox.
Past participleV3 (-ed / irregular)Built in 1890, it still stands.
Perfect participlehaving + V3Having finished, she left.
Perfect passivehaving been + V3Having been warned, they left.
Key point
  • The participle has no subject and no tense of its own — it borrows both from the main clause: Feeling tired, she left = she felt tired.
  • Participle clauses belong to formal, written English — reports, stories, articles. In casual speech, a full clause often sounds more natural.

2. Present participle (active & simultaneous)

Use the -ing participle when the subject does the action, and it happens at the same time as the main verb, or explains its reason.

Full clauseParticiple clauseMeaning
While I was walking home, I saw a fox.Walking home, I saw a fox.time (at the same time)
Because she felt tired, she left early.Feeling tired, she left early.reason / cause
Children who are travelling to the museum...Children travelling to the museum...reduced active relative
Golden rule
  • Ask: does the subject do the action? If yes, use -ing: Opening the letter, she smiled (she opens it).

3. Past participle (passive)

Use the past participle (V3) when something is done to the subject — the idea is passive. This is the form B2 students most often get wrong, reaching for -ing instead.

Full clauseParticiple clauseMeaning
The bridge, which was built in 1890, ...The bridge, built in 1890, ...reduced passive relative
Because he was shocked by the news, ...Shocked by the news, he sat down.reason (passive)
The letters that were sent last week...The letters sent last week...reduced passive relative
Golden rule
  • Ask: is the action done to the subject? If yes, use the past participle: Shocked by the news, he... (he was shocked) — not "Shocking by the news".

4. Perfect participle (an earlier action)

When one action is clearly completed before the next, use the perfect participle: having + V3 (active) or having been + V3 (passive). It works like the Past Perfect, marking the earlier event.

  • Having finished her homework, she went out to play.Finishing came first, then going out → having + V3.
  • Having been warned about the storm, they stayed indoors.They were warned first → passive → having been + V3.
  • Having caught the earlier bus, we reached the match with time to spare.Catching the bus came before arriving.
B2 subtlety
  • Use the perfect participle only when the time gap matters. For two things at the same moment, the simple -ing is enough: "Opening the door, she stepped in" ✓ — not "Having opened the door, she stepped in" unless you want to stress that one clearly finished first.

5. Reducing a clause (where participles come from)

Most participle clauses are simply shortened reason, time or relative clauses. Cut the subject and the linking word, and change the verb into a participle.

Original (full)Reduced (participle)Type
After she had eaten, she left.Having eaten, she left.time (sequence)
As it was made of glass, the tower shone.Made of glass, the tower shone.reason (passive)
The boy who is standing there is my best friend.The boy standing there is my best friend.relative (active)

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B2 students still make with participle clauses. The biggest one is the dangling participle — when the participle's subject does not match the main clause.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
Walking home, the rain started.Walking home, I got caught in the rain.the rain can't walk — the subject must match (dangling).
Building in 1890, the bridge still stands.Built in 1890, the bridge still stands.a passive idea takes the past participle, not -ing.
Being knowing her for years, I trust her.Having known her for years, I trust her.'know' is stative; use the perfect participle for prior time.
Having finished the project, the teacher praised it.Having finished the project, I felt relieved.the one who finished must be the main subject.
Finished my homework, I went out.Having finished my homework, I went out.an active, completed earlier action needs having + V3.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two friends talk about a restored railway station. Read it twice and watch the participles cooperate: present participles for simultaneous action, past participles for passive ideas, and a perfect participle for the earlier event.

A:Have you seen the old station? Restored last year, it looks incredible.It was restored → passive → past participle.
B:I know! Walking past it yesterday, I hardly recognised it.Two things at once, active → present participle.
A:Having stood empty for decades, it was almost a ruin.Standing empty came before → perfect participle.
B:Shocked by the state of it, the council finally acted.The council was shocked → passive → past participle.
A:Rebuilt with the original stone, it feels completely authentic.It was rebuilt → passive → past participle.
B:Designed to last, it should stand another century.It was designed → passive → past participle.
Test yourself
  • Find the perfect participle. Which action happened first? (Having stood empty for decades — before it became a ruin.)
  • Why is "Restored last year" a past participle, not an -ing form? (The station was restored — the idea is passive.)
  • Rewrite "While I was walking past it, I hardly recognised it" as a participle clause. (Walking past it, I hardly recognised it.)

Speaking: Old Places, New Life

Learn the words, then describe a place that has changed — using participle clauses.

abandoned (adj)
left empty and no longer used or cared for (terk edilmiş)
Syn: deserted · Ant: occupied
"Standing on the hill, the abandoned mill looked ghostly."
restore (v)
to return something to its original condition (aslına uygun onarmak)
Syn: renovate · Ant: ruin
"Restored with care, the theatre reopened last year."
derelict (adj)
in very poor condition after long neglect (harap, bakımsız)
Syn: run-down · Ant: pristine
"The derelict factory, abandoned for years, is finally being cleared."
crumbling (adj)
slowly breaking apart into small pieces (dökülen, ufalanan)
Syn: decaying · Ant: solid
"Crumbling walls surrounded the silent courtyard."
magnificent (adj)
extremely impressive and beautiful (muhteşem)
Syn: splendid · Ant: modest
"Built by a famous architect, the hall is magnificent."
neglect (n/v)

Syn: disregard · Ant: care
"Damaged by years of neglect, the roof had collapsed."
preserve (v)
to keep something in its original state (korumak, muhafaza etmek)
Syn: protect · Ant: destroy
"Carefully preserved, the old frescoes still shine."
heritage (n)

Syn: legacy · Ant: —
"Restoring the mosque protects the town's heritage."

Reading: The Library That Refused to Die

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Is there an old or abandoned building in your town? What could it become?
  • The text is a formal, written story. Which participle clauses do you expect to see a lot?
  • The title is "The Library That Refused to Die". Do you expect a hopeful ending? Guess!
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text. Notice each participle — a past participle ("Built in 1901"), a present participle ("Standing on a hill"), a perfect participle ("Having studied...") — and ask why the writer chose it.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): abandoned · restore · derelict · crumbling · neglect · preserve · heritage · magnificent

From derelict to restored: an old library brought back to life.
The Library That Refused to Die

Standing quietly on a hill above the town, the old library had watched over Selçuk for more than a century. Built in 1901 and designed by a French architect, it had once been the proudest building in the region. By the 1990s, however, it lay abandoned, its windows broken and its roof leaking.

For nearly forty years the building had simply been forgotten. Ivy, creeping slowly across the stonework, had swallowed the lower windows, while pigeons nested in the collapsing roof and weeds pushed up between the marble steps. Local children, daring one another to slip through a gap in the fence, whispered that the place was haunted. Once the pride of Selçuk, the library had become a derelict shell, its shelves emptied and its tall doors chained shut. Some residents wanted it demolished, arguing that a crumbling ruin was both dangerous and an embarrassment to the town. Others, remembering the stories their grandparents had told about grand reading rooms and travelling scholars, felt that pulling it down would erase a piece of their heritage forever. Year after year the argument went nowhere, and the once magnificent building continued to decay, neglected by almost everyone who hurried past it.

Deniz, a young architect trained in Istanbul, first saw the building on a rainy afternoon. Shocked by its condition yet moved by its beauty, she decided to save it. Having studied dozens of old photographs, she drew up a detailed plan. The local officials, worried about the cost, hesitated at first. Nevertheless, convinced by her passion, they finally agreed to fund the project.

Climbing over the broken threshold on that first grey afternoon, Deniz had felt her heart sink. Damp had spread in dark patches across the ceilings, and rain, dripping steadily through the ruined roof, had left long stains down the plaster walls. Yet, looking up through the gloom, she noticed something extraordinary. Beneath decades of grime, a painted ceiling of deep blues and faded gold still glowed, hinting at the beauty the building had once possessed. Fascinated, she spent the rest of the day photographing every cracked tile, every carved column and every faded fresco she could reach. Trained to respect old materials rather than replace them, she understood at once that this was not a building to be torn down but one to be rescued. Back in her small rented flat that night, surrounded by her photographs, she began to picture exactly how the library might look once it was carefully restored.

The work, lasting almost three years, was exhausting. Although the budget was tight, Deniz refused to cut corners. Walls damaged by damp had to be rebuilt stone by stone. Her rivals often used cheap concrete, whereas she insisted on traditional stone and timber. Moreover, since the original plans had survived, every detail could be restored faithfully.

Bringing the library back to life demanded a level of patience that few people had expected. Skilled masons, hired from villages across the region, cut fresh stone by hand to match the worn originals, while carpenters copied the elegant window frames piece by piece. 'Working on a building like this,' one elderly craftsman admitted with a smile, 'you can almost feel the hands of the people who raised it a hundred years ago.' Progress was painstaking and often frustrating. A single carved doorway, damaged beyond saving, took nearly two months to reproduce faithfully. Fine dust covered everything, and on the worst days, when the money had almost run out, even Deniz quietly wondered whether she had taken on far too much. Encouraged by the beauty slowly reappearing in the great hall, however, the team refused to give up, determined to preserve every original feature they possibly could.

On the morning of the reopening, a large crowd gathered outside, curious to see what had become of the building they had long ago written off. Bright ribbons, tied across the restored entrance, were cut to warm applause from the mayor and the waiting townspeople. Stepping inside, the first visitors fell completely silent, gazing up at the majestic ceiling now cleaned to its original glory. 'I never truly believed I would see it like this again,' said one elderly woman, quietly wiping her eyes. 'Neglected for so long, and now it feels almost alive.' Newspapers, reporting the event right across the region, described the project as a model of how a forgotten treasure could be saved rather than swept away. Where there had recently been only a dangerous, crumbling ruin, there now stood a warm and living centre for the whole community.

Reopened last spring, the library now hums with life again. Children reading in the bright hall sit beneath the same painted ceiling that had amazed visitors a hundred years earlier. The building had been neglected for decades; therefore, its rescue felt almost miraculous.

Today the library is busier than it has been for generations. Students, revising quietly at long wooden tables, share the reading room with retired locals who come simply to sit beneath the famous ceiling. Guided tours, offered free every weekend, explain how the ruin was rescued and why its original details were so carefully kept. Deniz, now invited to advise on projects across the country, still returns whenever she can, checking the roof after heavy rain and greeting the librarians by name. Asked whether she would ever take on such an enormous task again, she only laughs. Having poured so much of herself into these walls, she admits, she cannot imagine walking away from them for good.

The story of the little library soon spread far beyond Selçuk. Other towns, inspired by its unlikely success, began to look again at their own abandoned mills, empty stations and shuttered mansions. Heritage experts, interviewed on television, argued that restoring such places, rather than replacing them with cheap concrete blocks, kept a nation's living memory intact. For Deniz, the lesson drawn from those exhausting years was refreshingly simple. A building, once demolished, is gone for good, whereas a ruin, however hopeless it may first appear, can nearly always be renovated with enough care, skill and patience. Money spent on preserving the past, she liked to remind her students, is very rarely money wasted.

Having spent her twenties on a single project, Deniz has no regrets. 'A place like this, once lost, can never be replaced,' she says. 'Saved from ruin, it belongs to everyone now.'

Writing: A Place Transformed

Write and submit your paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound B2 (Linking Words)

Participle clauses give your writing a formal shape; the right connectors then guide the reader through your ideas. Four of them are essential this unit — and two familiar ones, although and however, become sharper tools.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
moreoveradd a stronger, extra pointThe roof leaked. Moreover, the walls were crumbling.
thereforeshow a logical resultIt had been neglected for years; therefore, its rescue was urgent.
whereascontrast two ideas directlyRivals used concrete, whereas she chose traditional stone.
neverthelesssay "in spite of that"The budget was tiny. Nevertheless, they finished the job.
althoughconcede a point inside one sentenceAlthough it was derelict, the building still felt majestic.
howevercontrast between two sentencesThe plan was risky. However, it worked.

See the difference — the same facts, written two ways:

Basic linking ✗Confident B2 writing ✓
The mill was old. It was empty. People fixed it. Now it is nice. Many visitors come. Built in the 1920s, the mill stood empty for decades. Although it was derelict, a local group refused to let it die. Having raised the money, they restored it faithfully; therefore, it now attracts visitors from across the region.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different B2 connectors from the table. Do not write sentence by sentence; connect your ideas.
  • Moreover, Therefore, However, Nevertheless usually start a sentence and take a comma: "However, it worked."

Paragraph structure (Paragraph Structure)

A "place transformed" paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningIntroduce the place with a participle clauseBuilt in 1901, the old library once stood proudly above the town.
2. BodyDescribe the decline and the rescue. Use past participles for passive ideas, -ing participles for what people did, and a perfect participle for an earlier step. Link with B2 connectors.Having stood empty for years, it was almost a ruin. Nevertheless, a small group restored it faithfully.
3. ClosingA final comment about the place now or the futureReopened last spring, it now belongs to everyone; therefore, the effort was worth it.

Sample Answer (~185 words) I have always loved the old cinema in my neighbourhood, and its story still amazes me. Built in the 1950s, it was once the most magnificent building on our street. By the time I was born, however, it had closed. Standing empty for years, it slowly became derelict, its walls crumbling and its sign broken.

Then everything changed. Bought by a local charity, the cinema was carefully restored. Although the work was slow and expensive, the team refused to cut corners. Whereas some people wanted a cheap, modern design, the architects insisted on preserving the original details. Moreover, having studied old photographs, they repainted the ceiling faithfully.

Reopened last spring, the cinema now attracts hundreds of visitors every weekend. Walking past it yesterday, I hardly recognised the place I remembered. Children queuing for tickets sit beneath the same golden ceiling that had amazed audiences seventy years earlier.
