Modals of AdviceTavsiye Modalleri (should / ought to)

Türkçedeki "-meli/-malı" tavsiye anlamına yakındır; should, ought to, had better öneri gücü açısından farklıdır.

It is close to the Turkish advice meaning of '-meli/-malı'; should, ought to, and had better differ in the strength of the suggestion.

Tavsiye modalleri, birine ne yapmasının iyi ya da doğru olacağını söylerken kullandığımız yardımcı fiillerdir. En yaygını should'dur; ought to aynı anlamı biraz daha resmi/nesnel bir tonla verir, had better ise tavsiyeye bir uyarı ve aciliyet katar. Türkçedeki -meli/-malı ekine yakındırlar ama birebir aynı değildirler: Türkçe tek bir ek üç ayrı İngilizce yapının işini görür, bu yüzden Türkçe düşünen öğrenci hangisini seçeceğini şaşırır. Bu konu, güçlü zorunluluk anlatan must / have to yapılarıyla (zorunluluk modalleri) karıştırılmaya çok müsaittir; aradaki güç farkını netleştirmek bu bölümün ana amacıdır.

Advice modals, the auxiliary verbs we use when telling someone what would begood orright to do. The most common isshould; ought to gives the same meaning in a slightly more formal/objective tone,had better adds awarning andurgency to the advice. The Turkish-meli/-malı suffix is close to them but not exactly the same: a single Turkish suffix does the work of three separate English structures, so learners thinking in Turkish get confused about which to choose. This topic is very easily confused with themust / have to structures (necessity modals); the main aim of this section is to clarify the difference in force between them.

A friend of yours looks tired and you say to them "you'd better get some rest". This, right here, is a piece ofadvice: you tell the other person what would be the right thing to do for their own good. In English, this job is most often done byshould. Alongside it comeought to andhad better. All three roughly correspond to the Turkish-meli / -malı suffix.

"All three correspond to the Turkish-meli / -malı; the difference is in thetone:shouldpolite,ought toobjective,had betterwarns.

So why are there three structures and not just one word? Because English likes to mark thethe tone andforceof advice with separate words.should is polite and neutral ("I think you'd better do this").ought to says the same thing in a slightly more objective, rule-like tone ("this is the right thing").had better adds a warning: "do this, or something bad will happen."

should

A polite, neutral suggestion:You should rest.

ought to

The same suggestion, in a slightly more objective / rule-like tone.

had better

Adds a warning — do it, or there's a bad consequence:You'd better hurry.

The real difficulty for a Turkish-speaking learner is this: in Turkish the sentence 'gitmelisin' can mean bothadvice andnecessity; we tell the difference from tone of voice and context. English, however, splits these two meanings into two separate words: for advice,should, and for strong obligation,must / have to(seeNecessity (must / have to)). That is why sayingshould everywhere you see "-meli" is a big trap.

Common trap

Don't automatically writeshould everywhere you see "-meli"; for real obligation,must / have tois needed.

The gist in one sentence
should / ought to= "you'd better…, this is the right thing" (polite advice).had better= "…do it, or else!" (advice with a warning).must / have to= "you have to…" (obligation — that is a separate topic).

Remember: The Strength Ladder
Think of advice as aladder, from softest to strongest:could / might(maybe you could?) →should ≈ ought to(you should) →had better(do it, or else!) →must / have to(you have to). Each step up increases the pressure. In an exam or conversation, ask 'how firm should I be?' and pick that step on the ladder.
Key Tip: Three Words, Three Tones
A short, catchy formula:should = I'd suggest, ought to = the right thing, had better = or else!. Don't memorise all three just as 'advice'; each has an 'inner voice'. should's inner voice is 'in my opinion', while had better's is 'I'm warning you'.
