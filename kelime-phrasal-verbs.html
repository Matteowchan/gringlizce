A phrasal verb is a phrase formed by adding a particle (a preposition or adverb) to a verb, and it often takes on a meaning completely different from the verb alone: get up, give up, look after. Because Turkish covers most of these with a single verb (call off = to cancel), the learner cannot 'decode' the verb+particle link and has to combine memorisation with context. The two most critical distinctions are separable/inseparable (turn it on ✓ / *look her after ✗) and transitive/intransitive (takes an object / does not). This topic is a close relative of Idioms and Collocations; together with Word Formation it forms the backbone of vocabulary.

Bir konuşmacı 'The meeting was postponed' derse resmî; 'They put the meeting off' derse gündelik ve akıcı ses verir.

A phrasal verb is a phrase that arises from combining a verb with a small word added to it (a preposition or adverb; this is called a particle). The key point is this: this phrase often carries neither the meaning of the verb nor of the particle alone; when the two combine, a new and often unpredictable meaning emerges. For example, give means 'to give' and up means 'upward'; but give up means 'to quit, to give up'. Instead of forcing the logic between them, you need to learn the phrase like a single word.

Why does it exist? In everyday speech, English prefers short, Germanic verb+particle phrases (put up with, put out, put off) over 'heavy' Latinate verbs (tolerate, extinguish, postpone). That is why knowing phrasal verbs is the key to speaking English naturally rather than bookishly. If a speaker says 'The meeting was postponed' it sounds formal; if they say 'They put the meeting off' it sounds everyday and fluent.

"Knowing phrasal verbs is the key to speaking English naturally rather than bookishly: saying the same idea with a short verb+particle instead of a heavy single verb gives the sentence a native ring.

Is there a Turkish equivalent? Partly. Turkish also has compound/idiomatic verbs such as 'karar vermek', 'göz atmak', 'yola çıkmak'; the meaning does not come from the individual words. However, Turkish often covers this meaning with a single verb too: call off = to cancel, find out = to learn. This is exactly why phrasal verbs challenge Turkish learners: we see something that is a single verb in Turkish split into two parts in English, and the question 'which particle?' requires memorisation.

Mental Model
Think of a phrasal verb like 'twins': the verb and the particle always travel together and produce meaning together. look (to look) is on its own; look after (to look after/take care of), look for (to search for), look up (to find in a dictionary) are completely different twins. When the particle changes, the personality changes.

Why is it hard? Three reasons: (1) The meaning is unpredictable (opaque). (2) The same verb produces dozens of different meanings with dozens of different particles (get up, get on, get over, get through, get by...). (3) There is a grammar rule: some phrasal verbs take the object in the middle (turn it on), some do not (look after her). This topic is both a vocabulary and a grammar topic; it belongs to the same 'cannot derive the meaning from the parts' family as Idioms.

The 'structure' of a phrasal verb is not about subject conjugation but about the grammatical type of the verb. The verb itself is conjugated like a normal English verb (I get up, she gets up, they got up); the issue is where to place the object. There are four basic arrangements.

  1. Intransitive: there is no object, nothing goes in the middle — sit down, break down.
  2. Separable transitive: a noun object is free, both arrangements are correct — turn off the lights / turn the lights off.
  3. Inseparable transitive: the object, whether noun or pronoun, comes after the particle — look after her.
  4. Negative and question: the auxiliary moves to the front, the particle stays attached to the verb — Did you give up?

1. Intransitive (no object)

Formula: subject + verb + particle. Nothing comes in the middle; there is no object anyway.

Please sit down.
Lütfen otur.

set off = to set out, intransitive.

2. Separable transitive (noun object)

The Pronoun Rule — No Exceptions
If the object is a pronoun (it, them, him, her, us...), the pronoun always goes in the middle in a separable phrasal verb: Turn it off ✓ / Turn off it ✗. Learn this like a law: the pronoun never hides behind the particle.

3. Inseparable transitive

Formula: verb + particle + object. Whether it is a noun or a pronoun, the object comes AFTER the particle; it never goes in the middle.

She looks after her grandmother. / She looks after her.
Büyükannesine bakıyor. / Ona bakıyor.
look after is inseparable; not even a pronoun goes in the middle.
I came across an old photo. / I came across it.
Eski bir fotoğrafa denk geldim. / Ona denk geldim.
come across = to come across, inseparable.

Negative and question

The negative and question of a phrasal verb are made with the normal rule of the verb inside it; the particle just stays 'attached' to the verb. The auxiliary (do/does/did, be, will etc.) moves in front of the verb, the particle stays in place.

Üçüncü incelik: vurgu ve doğallık. Konuşmada phrasal verb kullanmak, seni ana dili İngilizce olan biri gibi gösterir. 'We need to discuss this' doğrudur ama biraz resmî; 'We need to talk this over' daha sıcak ve doğaldır. Doğru register seçimi, ne dediğin kadar nasıl algılandığını da belirler.

The most challenging aspect of phrasal verbs is polysemy: the same phrase can carry completely different meanings depending on context. take off means 'to depart' for a plane, 'to remove' for a garment, and 'to suddenly succeed/take off' for a business. The way to guess the right meaning is to look at the subject and the object.

The plane took off on time.
Uçak zamanında kalktı.
take off = (a plane) to take off.
Take off your wet shoes.
Islak ayakkabılarını çıkar.
take off = to remove (a garment).
Her business really took off last year.
İşi geçen yıl gerçekten patlama yaptı.
take off = to suddenly succeed.

Second nuance: the particle often carries a 'direction of meaning'. Sensing this helps you guess new phrases. up usually means completion/increase (finish/increase: eat up, use up, speak up); out emerging or finishing (find out, run out, sort out); on continuing (carry on, go on); off separating/stopping (take off, call off, break off); down decreasing or recording (calm down, write down).

Key Point — Particle Intuition
up = 'completely/until finished' (drink it up = drink to the last drop), out = 'from nothing to visible' (find out = to bring to light), on = 'continue' (carry on), down = 'calm/reduce/write' (calm down, note down). This is not a rule but a scent; not a hundred per cent, but it makes guessing much easier.

Third nuance: emphasis and naturalness. Using phrasal verbs in speech makes you sound like a native English speaker. 'We need to discuss this' is correct but a little formal; 'We need to talk this over' is warmer and more natural. Choosing the right register determines not only what you say but how it is perceived.

The closest Turkish structure to a phrasal verb is the compound/idiomatic verb (yola çıkmak, göz atmak, vazgeçmek, denk gelmek). However, there are critical differences between the two languages, and these differences are the source of Turkish learners' most frequent errors.

CAUTION

Do not fall into the trap of saying an action that is a single verb in Turkish (bakmak, beklemek) without its particle in English: look ≠ look after, wait ≠ wait for. Turkish logic pushes you to drop the particle; the part that actually carries the meaning is often that small particle.

  • Turkish covers most phrasal verbs with a single verb (call off = to cancel, find out = to learn). That is why the learner cannot generate the 'which particle?' information and has to memorise it.
  • In Turkish a suffix is added to the end of the word (kalk-tı-m); in English the meaning is spread onto a separate particle (get up). Our brain looks for 'suffixes', but English can separate the particle from the verb.
  • In Turkish the object + verb order (kitabı oku) is natural; in English, in a separable phrasal verb, the position of the object can change (turn the TV off / turn off the TV) — this flexibility does not exist in Turkish.
  • Turkish attaches case suffixes like 'için/-e/-den' to the verb; in English, if the wrong preposition is chosen (wait for ✓ / *wait to ✗), the meaning breaks. Choosing the preposition=particle is like the equivalent of the Turkish case suffix, but it does not overlap exactly.

The Single Most Common Mistake
The most common mistake is putting the pronoun object behind the particle in a separable phrasal verb: turn off it ✗. The correct form is always turn it off ✓. Correcting just this one thing already makes your English noticeably more correct.
TIP

Keep the right reflex in one sentence: pronoun → in the middle, noun → free. In a separable phrase, it/them/him always goes between the verb and the particle (turn it off); if the object is a noun, it can go in either place (turn off the light / turn the light off).

Correct Please turn it off.

A pronoun object (it) never comes after the particle in a separable phrasal verb; it always goes between the verb and the particle.

Correct I look after the children on Sundays.

Correct She gave up eating sugar.

Correct We waited for the film to start.

Correct He is looking for his keys everywhere but can't find them.

The rules of phrasal verbs are general, but there are a few special cases. Knowing them sets the advanced level apart.

  • Some separable verbs are not separated with a long object. If the object is very long, natural English puts the object at the end: Turn off all the lights that are still on in the upstairs rooms. (Putting it in the middle tires the ear.)
  • Some phrasal verbs are only used in the passive or become nouns: a checkup (an examination), a takeoff, a breakdown — when the verb turns into a noun it is written as one word.
  • Three-part verbs are never separated: put up with, get on with, look forward to, come up with, run out of. The object always comes at the very end.
  • The same phrase can be transitive and intransitive: break down intransitive 'to break down' (The car broke down) or transitive 'to analyse/break apart' (Break the problem down).

When the object is long ('all the lights in the house'), natural English does not squeeze it into the middle; it keeps the particle close to the verb and puts the object at the end.

The following questions measure not the rule but meaning and comprehension. Before answering, think about the context of each sentence; then reveal the answers.

Concept check Answer each question in your own words; then compare with the given answer.
  1. In 'The plane took off' and 'Take off your shoes', does 'take off' mean the same thing? Why?
    No. In the first, 'take off' = (a plane) to depart; in the second = to remove (a garment). The same phrase, different context, different meaning — this is the polysemy of phrasal verbs.
  2. Why is 'Turn off it' wrong?
    Because a pronoun object (it) never comes after the particle in a separable phrasal verb; it always goes in the middle: 'turn it off'.
  3. What is the difference between 'look after' and 'look for'?
    'look after' = to care for/look after (look after the baby); 'look for' = to search for (look for your keys). The particle completely changes the meaning.
  4. In 'She gave up smoking', why is it 'smoking' (a gerund) and not 'to smoke'?
    Because when 'give up' takes a verb, it is followed by -ing (a gerund). This is the grammar pattern of many phrasal verbs; 'to smoke' is wrong.
  5. Is 'put off' suitable for a formal report? If not, what is used?
    It is usually everyday; in formal writing 'postpone' is preferred. However, if the context is semi-formal, 'put off' can also be acceptable.

Do not have students memorise phrasal verbs in lists ('30 phrasal verbs with GET'). This is overwhelming and forgotten.
