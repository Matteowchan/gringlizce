Grammar: Advanced Reported Speech

Go beyond "say" and "tell": report the meaning of what people do with their words.

Beyond "say" and "tell"

At B1 you reported speech mostly with say and tell: "She said (that) she was tired." That works, but it is flat. At B2, good speakers report the function of an utterance — whether someone advised, warned, admitted, refused or threatened. One precise verb replaces a whole clause and shows exactly what the speaker was doing.

The challenge is that each of these verbs demands its own grammatical pattern. Some take an object + a to-infinitive; some take the -ing form; some take a preposition; some take a that-clause. Choose the wrong pattern and the sentence collapses. This unit sorts the most useful reporting verbs into clear families so that the pattern comes to you automatically.

  • "Don't forget your passport." → She reminded me to take my passport.reminding function → verb + object + to-infinitive.
  • "I broke the window." → He admitted breaking the window.confession → verb + -ing.
  • "I'll help you, I promise." → She promised to help.promise → verb + to-infinitive.
  • "I'm sorry I was late." → He apologised for being late.apology → verb + preposition + -ing.

1. The patterns at a glance (the five families)

Every reporting verb belongs to a pattern family. Learn the family, and you can report dozens of situations without hesitating.

PatternReporting verbsExample
verb + sb + to-infadvise, warn, remind, encourage, tell, askShe advised me to rest.
verb + to-infoffer, promise, refuse, agree, threatenHe promised to call.
verb + -ingadmit, deny, suggest, recommendThey denied cheating.
verb + prep + -ingapologise for, insist onI apologised for being late.
verb + that-clauseadmit, insist, suggest, promise, recommendHe insisted that she stay.
Key point
  • Some verbs live in two families: admit, suggest and recommend take both -ing and a that-clause — but never object + to-infinitive.
  • The verb tells you the pattern, not the situation. Learn advise + sb + to, suggest + -ing / that as fixed shapes.

2. verb + somebody + to-infinitive (directing others)

Use this family when someone directs, guides or warns another person. There is always an object (the person spoken to) before to.

VerbOriginal wordsReported
advise"You should see a doctor."She advised him to see a doctor.
warn"Don't touch the wire!"He warned us not to touch the wire.
remind"Remember to lock the door."She reminded me to lock the door.
encourage"Go on, apply for it!"They encouraged her to apply.
Negatives — where does "not" go?
  • For a negative order or warning, put not before to: "He warned me not to go." ✓ — "warned me to not go" ✗ and "warned me don't go" ✗.
  • The person spoken to is obligatory: "advised me to rest", not "advised to rest me".

3. verb + to-infinitive (speaking about yourself)

These verbs report what the speaker promises, offers, refuses or threatens to do. There is no object before to — the subject and the doer are the same person.

  • "I'll drive you." → He offered to drive me.offer + to-infinitive (no object before "to").
  • "I promise I'll finish it." → She promised to finish it.promise + to-infinitive.
  • "No, I won't sign." → He refused to sign.refuse + to-infinitive (a firm 'no').
  • "Pay me, or I'll sue you." → She threatened to sue him.threaten + to-infinitive.
Compare the two "to" families
  • Object present → directing someone: She advised me to leave (I leave).
  • No object → talking about the speaker: She offered to leave (she leaves). Mixing these up is the classic B2 error.

4. verb + -ing (admitting, denying, suggesting)

This family reports confessions, denials and suggestions. The verb is followed directly by the -ing form — never by a to-infinitive.

VerbOriginal wordsReported
admit"Yes, I took it."He admitted taking it.
deny"I didn't do it."She denied doing it.
suggest"Let's wait a week."He suggested waiting a week.
recommend"You should book early."She recommended booking early.

Two more verbs need a preposition before the -ing form:

  • "Sorry I forgot." → He apologised for forgetting.apologise for + -ing.
  • "You must come with us." → She insisted on coming with us.insist on + -ing.
Watch out
  • suggest and recommend never take object + to-infinitive: "She suggested that I rest" ✓ or "suggested resting" ✓ — but "suggested me to rest" ✗.
  • deny takes -ing, not to: "He denied taking it" ✓ — "denied to take it" ✗.

5. verb + that-clause (insisting, suggesting formally)

Several verbs can also report with a that-clause. This is useful for a full statement or a formal demand. After suggest, recommend and insist, the verb in the clause often stays in the base form (the "subjunctive"), with an optional should.

VerbExample (that-clause)
insistThe manager insisted that everyone (should) wear a badge.
suggestShe suggested that we (should) postpone the launch.
recommendHe recommended that the report (should) be rewritten.
admitShe admitted that she had made a mistake.
B2 subtlety
  • After suggest / recommend / insist + that, use the base form for all subjects: "insisted that she stay" (not "stays"). Adding should is also correct: "insisted that she should stay."
  • suggest never takes a to-infinitive at all — only -ing or a that-clause.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors B2 students still make with reporting verbs. Check your own writing against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
She suggested me to leave early.She suggested (that) I leave early. / suggested leaving early.suggest never takes object + to-infinitive.
He denied to take the money.He denied taking the money.deny is followed by -ing, not to.
They advised me leaving.They advised me to leave.advise + somebody + to-infinitive.
I apologised to be late.I apologised for being late.apologise for + -ing.
He warned me to not touch it.He warned me not to touch it.'not' comes before 'to'.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two colleagues talk about a meeting with the boss. Read it twice and watch how one precise reporting verb replaces a whole reported sentence: admitted, apologised for, promised, advised, encouraged, recommended.

A:The manager called me in. He admitted making a mistake with my schedule.confession → admit + -ing.
B:Really? Did he apologise for it?apology → apologise for + -ing.
A:He did. He apologised for causing the confusion and promised to fix it by Friday.apologise for + -ing; promise + to-infinitive.
B:Good. Did he say anything about the overtime?an ordinary question, no reporting verb needed.
A:He advised me not to work extra hours and encouraged me to take a proper break.advise / encourage + object + to-infinitive; note 'not to'.
B:Fair enough. I'd recommend putting it all in an email, though — just in case.recommend + -ing.
Test yourself
  • Which verb is followed by -ing here? (admit → "admitted making"; recommend → "recommend putting".)
  • Why is it "advised me to take" but "recommend putting"? (advise needs object + to-infinitive; recommend takes -ing.)
  • Rewrite "He promised to fix it" as reported speech beginning "He said...". (He said (that) he would fix it.)

Speaking: Reporting Conversations

Learn the reporting verbs, then use them to retell a real conversation of your own.

negotiate (v)
to discuss something to reach an agreement (müzakere etmek)
Syn: bargain · Ant: dictate
"They negotiated for hours before signing the deal."
apologise (v)
to say you are sorry for something (özür dilemek)
Syn: say sorry · Ant: blame
"He apologised for missing the deadline."
admit (v)
to accept that something is true, often unwillingly (itiraf etmek)
Syn: confess · Ant: deny
"She admitted making a mistake in the report."
deny (v)
to say that something is not true (inkâr etmek)
Syn: reject · Ant: admit
"He denied breaking the agreement."
insist (v)
to say firmly that something must happen (ısrar etmek)
Syn: demand · Ant: yield
"She insisted that we start on time."
persuade (v)
to make someone agree by giving reasons (ikna etmek)
Syn: convince · Ant: discourage
"They persuaded him to change his mind."
reassure (v)
to say something to make someone feel calmer (içini rahatlatmak)
Syn: comfort · Ant: alarm
"The doctor reassured us that it was nothing serious."
warn (v)
to tell someone about a possible danger or problem (uyarmak)
Syn: caution · Ant: encourage
"She warned me not to trust him."
encourage (v)
to give someone confidence to do something (teşvik etmek)
Syn: motivate · Ant: discourage
"My tutor encouraged me to keep trying."
remind (v)
to help someone remember something (hatırlatmak)
Syn: prompt · Ant: —
"He reminded me to bring my ID."
refuse (v)
to say firmly that you will not do something (reddetmek)
Syn: decline · Ant: agree
"She refused to answer the question."
recommend (v)
to suggest that something is good or worth doing (tavsiye etmek)
Syn: advise · Ant: discourage
"I recommend booking the tickets early."
confront (v)
to face a problem or person directly (yüzleşmek, karşı karşıya gelmek)
Syn: challenge · Ant: avoid
"He finally confronted his manager about the pay."
Reporting it later: "She reminded him that he had promised to call, and he apologised for forgetting."

Production Task (Üretim Görevi)

Retell a real (or imagined) conversation in 8 sentences — a disagreement, a piece of advice, or a negotiation. Use at least 8 of the words above.

Grammar requirement — apply this unit's topic
  • Use advise / warn / remind / encourage + somebody + to for directions (She advised me to wait).
  • Use admit / deny / suggest / recommend + -ing for confessions and suggestions (He admitted forgetting; she suggested waiting).
  • Use offer / promise / refuse / threaten + to for what the speaker will or won't do (He promised to help; I refused to sign).
  • Include at least one negative with not to (She warned me not to be late) and one apologise for + -ing.

Sentence starters (you can begin with these):

"She advised me to ..." "He admitted ...-ing ..." "They warned us not to ..." "I promised to ..." "In the end, we agreed to ..."

Reading: A Meeting That Changed Everything

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Good readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think of a time you made a mistake at work or school. Did you admit it, or try to hide it?
  • The text is about a tense business meeting. Which reporting verbs (admit, deny, promise, threaten...) do you expect to see?
  • The title is "A Meeting That Changed Everything". Do you think it ends well or badly? Guess!
How to read
  • First read: read quickly, without stopping. Get the general idea; you do not need every word.
  • Second read: read the questions, then search the text. Underline each reporting verb — admitted, apologised for, promised, threatened, warned, encouraged — and notice the pattern that follows it.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): negotiate · apologise · admit · deny · insist · warn · encourage · remind · refuse · recommend

One honest apology and a clear plan can turn a failing meeting into an agreement.
The Meeting Nobody Forgot

When a small design studio nearly lost its biggest client, everything came down to a single tense meeting — and to what each person was willing to say.

The studio, called Northlight, was well known for its clean, modern furniture designs, and the furniture company had been one of its very first customers. Over the years the two teams had grown comfortable with each other, perhaps a little too comfortable. When a new product line was announced, almost everyone assumed that the schedule would somehow look after itself. Nobody imagined that a handful of missed emails and one badly overloaded calendar could push a friendly, long-standing partnership to the edge of collapse in a matter of weeks.

The trouble had started quietly. Deniz had taken on three large projects at once, convinced that her small team could handle the pressure without any extra help. For several weeks she reassured everyone that the work was firmly on track, yet the progress reports she sent grew shorter and vaguer with each passing Friday. By the time the furniture company asked for a proper update, two of the key drawings were still unfinished. The client began to suspect that something had gone seriously wrong long before anyone in the studio was willing to admit it out loud.

The client, a furniture company, insisted that the studio had missed two important deadlines. The project manager, Deniz, did not argue. Instead, she admitted making a serious mistake with the schedule and apologised for the delays. She promised to deliver the remaining work within ten days.

The meeting itself took place on a grey Tuesday morning in a narrow conference room that smelled faintly of coffee and fresh paint. Six people sat around the table, and for the first few minutes nobody seemed willing to speak first. Deniz had carefully prepared a long list of technical reasons for the delays, but as she looked at the tired, doubtful faces opposite her, she decided at the last moment to put the list away. She explained afterwards that a pile of excuses would only have made the silence in the room feel even heavier.

At first, the client refused to continue. Their representative even threatened to cancel the whole contract and warned Deniz not to make any more excuses. The atmosphere in the room was cold.

For a moment it seemed that the partnership was already over. The representative, a careful man named Mr Aydın, said that his managers had been asking difficult questions for weeks. He complained that every promise from the studio had quietly slipped, and he insisted that his company could not keep waiting for work that never seemed to arrive. Two of his colleagues nodded firmly in agreement, and one of them even began gathering her papers together, as though the meeting had already reached its end.

However, Deniz stayed calm. She reminded the client that the studio had produced excellent work for three years, and she encouraged them to look at the bigger picture. Then she offered to reduce the final invoice as a gesture of good faith.

She did not raise her voice or beg them to stay. Instead, she opened a simple folder and showed them photographs of the earlier projects the two companies had completed together — a bright showroom in the city centre, a series of award-winning chairs, and a catalogue that had helped the furniture company win new customers of its own. She admitted honestly that the recent months had been badly managed, but she asked them to weigh one difficult period against three genuinely successful years. Reducing the invoice, she explained, was not a clever trick to buy back their loyalty; it was simply a fair way to share the cost of her own mistake.

Her honesty changed the mood completely. One of the client's directors, who had said almost nothing, finally spoke. He advised his colleagues to give the studio a second chance, and he suggested extending the deadline rather than ending the partnership.

The director, an older man who had helped to found part of the company decades earlier, spoke slowly and chose every word with care. He reminded the room that even the strongest business relationships pass through hard moments, and he warned his younger colleagues against throwing away a trusted partner over a single disappointing quarter. He then proposed a practical compromise: the studio would deliver the outstanding work within the agreed ten days, and the two companies would meet once a month from then on, so that small problems could never quietly grow into large ones again. To everyone's obvious relief, Mr Aydın agreed to consider it seriously.

By the end of the meeting the mood in the narrow room had changed entirely. Cups of cold coffee were pushed aside, chairs were pulled a little closer, and people who had arrived as opponents slowly began to talk like colleagues once more. Deniz noted down every point that had been raised and repeated the new deadline aloud, so that there could be no misunderstanding later. She thanked the client sincerely for their patience and confirmed, in writing that same afternoon, exactly what her team would deliver and precisely when.

In the end, the client agreed to stay. Afterwards, Deniz denied being a hero. 'I simply told the truth,' she said. 'When something goes wrong, people don't want excuses; they want a plan.' Her team later recommended writing her approach into the studio handbook. Since that day, everyone there remembers one quiet rule: admit the problem, then promise the solution.

News of the meeting spread quickly around the studio. Deniz's colleagues had expected her to return either furious or completely defeated, so they were genuinely surprised when she walked in looking thoughtful rather than shaken. She gathered the whole team together, described honestly how close they had come to losing the account, and thanked the designers who had offered to work extra hours to rescue the schedule. Nobody was blamed in public. Instead, she encouraged everyone to speak up the very moment a deadline started to feel impossible, rather than hiding the problem until it finally exploded.

In the weeks that followed, the studio quietly changed the way it worked. A large whiteboard appeared near the entrance, and each project was marked with a colour that clearly showed whether it was safe, slipping, or genuinely in danger. Deniz began sending short, honest updates every Friday, even when the news was uncomfortable to write. The furniture company, for its part, stopped assuming that silence meant steady progress and started asking gentle questions much earlier. Both sides later agreed that the crisis, painful as it had been at the time, had taught them to communicate far better than before.

Writing: Reporting What Was Said

Write and submit your paragraph; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound B2 (Linking Words)

When you report a conversation, you also need connectors to show sequence and contrast. Four of them are essential this unit — and two familiar ones, although and however, become sharper tools.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
moreoveradd a stronger, extra pointHe apologised. Moreover, he promised to pay for the damage.
thereforeshow a logical resultShe had missed the deadline; therefore, she offered to work late.
whereascontrast two people or ideasI wanted to wait, whereas he insisted on leaving.
neverthelesssay "in spite of that"They warned me. Nevertheless, I refused to change my mind.
althoughconcede a point inside one sentenceAlthough he denied lying, nobody believed him.
howevercontrast between two sentencesShe refused at first. However, we finally persuaded her.

See the difference — the same facts, written two ways:

Basic linking ✗Confident B2 writing ✓
My boss was angry. I said sorry. I said I would fix it. He said okay. He told me to be careful. My boss confronted me about the error. Therefore, I apologised for the mistake and promised to fix it. Although he was still annoyed, he encouraged me to move on; however, he warned me not to let it happen again.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different B2 connectors from the table. Do not write sentence by sentence; connect your ideas.
  • Moreover, Therefore, However, Nevertheless usually start a sentence and take a comma: "However, he refused."

Paragraph structure (Paragraph Structure)

A "reported conversation" paragraph has three parts. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the structure:

PartWhat to writeExample
1. OpeningSet the scene: who spoke to whom, and whyLast month my manager called me in to discuss a serious mistake.
2. BodyReport what each person said, using a range of reporting verbs (admit, apologise for, promise, warn... not to, advise... to). Link with B2 connectors.I admitted making the error and apologised for it. Nevertheless, she warned me not to repeat it.
3. ClosingSay how it ended and what you learnedIn the end, we agreed to review my work together each week.

Task (Görev)

Report a conversation that mattered — a piece of advice, a disagreement or a negotiation from your studies, work or personal life. Retell what was said using a range of reporting verbs.

WHAT TO INCLUDE

Sample Answer (~185 words) Last month, my manager called me in to discuss a project I had handled badly. I was nervous, but I decided to be honest. I admitted making several mistakes and apologised for the delays they had caused. Therefore, instead of blaming my colleagues, I promised to redo the work by the end of the week.
