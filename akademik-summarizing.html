Summarizing is the skill of re-expressing a source's main idea and key points much more briefly, objectively and in your own sentences. Whereas a paraphrase says a sentence in other words at the same length, a summary compresses the whole text and takes only the core. A good summary does three things at once: it states the source with reporting verbs (the author argues that...), filters out detail and examples, and reports faithfully without adding your own comment. It is the cornerstone of TOEFL integrated writing, the academic literature review, presentation and report writing. In this section we cover in detail the logic of the summary, the reporting language, compression techniques, fluent linking with Signposting, and the traps a Turkish-speaking student often falls into.

Bir romanı arkadaşınıza "kısaca" anlatmak, bir haberi "özetle şöyle" diye aktarmak, bir toplantı tutanağını üç maddeye indirmek — hepsi özetlemedir. Peki bu beceri neden var? Çünkü bilgi her zaman ham hâliyle işe yaramaz. Bir araştırmacı 300 sayfalık raporu okuyup meslektaşına "özetle şu üç bulgu" diyebilmeli. "Özet, ayrıntıyı değil iskeleti alır: kaynağın sesini değil, çekirdeğini taşırsın.

Türkçede bunun tam karşılığı vardır: "özetlemek", "kısaca", "özetle", "ana hatlarıyla". Türk öğrenci kavramı zaten bilir — zorluk kavramda değil, İngilizce'de nasıl yapıldığındadır. Summarizing is the skill of taking the essence of a text or a talk and re-expressing it much more briefly, in your own sentences and impartially. Telling a friend a novel 'in brief', reporting a news story 'to sum up', reducing meeting minutes to three points — all are summarizing. In the academic world, this is a fundamental skill on which everything is built: you read a source, extract its core, and place it faithfully in your own text.

So why does this skill exist? Because information is not always useful in its raw form. A researcher must be able to read a 300-page report and tell a colleague 'in brief, these three findings'. A student must be able to convey an article's argument in a single paragraph so they can build their own thesis on it. A summary makes information portable: it reduces the volume, keeps the core.

"A summary takes not the detail but the skeleton: you carry the core of the source, not its voice.

Turkish has an exact equivalent: "özetlemek", "kısaca", "özetle", "ana hatlarıyla". The Turkish student already knows the concept — the difficulty is not in the concept but in how it is done in English. Two points are especially hard: (1) choosing the reporting verbs (argue, claim, suggest, note) correctly, because each carries a different certainty and stance; (2) compressing without copying the source, yet remaining faithful to its idea.

A summary is often confused with its sibling, paraphrase. The difference is clear: a paraphrase says the same length in other words; a summary is much shorter and takes only the main ideas, dropping the detail. You paraphrase a sentence; you summarize a section.

Says it in other words at the same length. Rewrites the sentence, keeps the detail — drops nothing.

Much shorter. Takes only the main idea and key points, deliberately filtering out the detail.

Handy Tip
The golden rule of the summary: "Whose idea + what it says + why it matters" — but all of it in your own sentence, not in the source's language. Take the skeleton, not the detail. When summarizing a building you do not count the bricks, you say 'a three-storey office with a glass facade'.

A summary is not a grammar tense but a writing technique; but there is a solid, recurring sentence skeleton. You can think of this skeleton as three 'moods': direct reporting (affirmative), non-reporting/rejecting (negative) and presenting it to the reader as a question. Let us build each one below.

Affirmative reporting

The source defends an idea: X argues that… — a reporting verb + a that-clause.

Rejecting

The source opposes an idea: X rejects / disputes… — the negation is placed correctly.

Question framing

The question the source addresses: X examines why / whether… — an indirect question order.

1) Affirmative / direct reporting

The basic formula: [Source] + [reporting verb] + that + [main idea]. The reporting verb is used in the simple present (the academic convention: argues, states), because the text is taken to 'still say so'.

The author argues that social media reshapes political debate.
The author argues that social media reshapes political debate.
argues = a strong, reasoned claim. The that-clause carries the main idea.
According to Chen (2019), the policy failed for three reasons.
According to Chen (2019), the policy failed for three reasons.
According to + source: an objective opening at the sentence start.
The report concludes that early screening saves lives.
The report concludes that early screening saves lives.
concludes = the conclusion reached, ideal for the closing sentence of a summary.

2) Negative / rejecting reporting

Sometimes the source rejects an idea or says 'it is not so'. When reporting this, the negation must be placed correctly. [Source] + rejects/denies/disputes + [idea] or argues that X is not....

The author rejects the claim that technology alone can solve the crisis.
The author rejects the claim that technology alone can solve the crisis.
rejects = direct opposition; the 'the claim that' pattern packages the rejected idea.
The study finds no significant link between the two variables.
The study finds no significant link between the two variables.
finds no = reporting the absence of a finding; objective and precise.
Smith disputes the popular view that reading is in decline.
Smith disputes the popular view that reading is in decline.
disputes = objects to but does not firmly reject; a subtle difference.

3) Framing as a question

Within a summary, stating the question the source addresses guides the reader: [Source] + addresses/examines/asks + [indirect question]. In an indirect question the word order is like a plain sentence (not the question word order).

The paper examines why some students disengage from online learning.
The paper examines why some students disengage from online learning.

The balance of emphasis and objectivity

In a summary you cannot add your own view, but which point you foreground is a choice. Putting the main idea first, filtering out detail — these are legitimate tools of emphasis. What is not legitimate is adding a value judgement: an adjective like the author foolishly claims breaks the summary.

If an article says "Critics claim X, but I show Y", you must not mix this up in the summary: While acknowledging critics' claim that X, the author argues Y. Reporting who said what without tangling them together is true mastery.

Some rules of the summary bend. Knowing them lets you write a conscious summary rather than a mechanical one.

  • Terms and proper nouns are copied: You write the detail in your own words, but do not try to change technical terms (photosynthesis, GDP, mitochondria); they are already the single correct word.

Meaning check Think about each case and give your reasoning.
  1. What changes for the reader between "The author suggests that..." and "The author proves that..."?
    'suggests' shows that the author is cautious/unsure, 'proves' that they present definite evidence. The wrong verb distorts the source's degree of certainty and breaks the summary's faithfulness.
  2. Why does a summary usually drop a source's statistical detail (for example '210 of 347 participants') but keep the main-finding figure (a 40% drop)?
    Because a summary keeps the core and filters out detail. The participant breakdown is methodological detail; a 40% drop is the text's actual message. If the figure is the main idea it stays, if it is decorative detail it goes.
  3. Why is the sentence "The author foolishly argues..." not a valid summary?
    'foolishly' is the summarizer's own value judgement. A summary is objective; the moment you add your own criticism you stop summarizing and start commenting. Criticism should be separate, outside the summary.
  4. Why do paraphrasing and summarizing the same idea produce results of different lengths?
    A paraphrase rewrites the same information in different words, dropping nothing — it keeps the size. A summary reduces the whole to the core, deliberately dropping detail — it shortens. See Paraphrase.
  5. Why does an academic summary say 'argues' rather than 'is arguing'?
    The academic convention treats a text's claim as permanent/valid and uses the simple present. The continuous (is arguing) gives a feel of a temporary action in progress at that moment, which does not match the source's permanent claim.

Where to Start
When teaching summarizing, first clarify its difference from paraphrase; most students confuse the two. The 'thumbnail vs. photocopy' metaphor (Section 12) lands immediately. Then start with a single reporting verb (says/argues), and open up the range (suggests, notes, concludes) once confidence comes. The student must first learn to find the main idea — compression comes later.

For concept checking, ask: "Is the author sure or not sure here?" (to catch the suggests vs. proves distinction). "Is this your opinion or the author's?" (an objectivity check). "Can we cut this and still keep the main idea?" (to build the detail-filtering reflex). These three questions test the three pillars of the summary skill (certainty, objectivity, compression).

Activity suggestions: (1) Shrink it: a competition to reduce a paragraph first to three sentences, then to one. (2) Verb swap: have the same summary sentence written with three different reporting verbs and discuss the difference in meaning. (3) Spot the opinion: have students find the 'non-objective' word in summaries with a comment secretly slipped in. (4) Integrated mini-task: give a short reading + a short opposing view and have a single paragraph written comparing the two (TOEFL preparation).

The Most Common Problem in Class
Students copy the source, put in a few synonyms, and think they have 'summarized'. The most effective intervention against this: have them close the source and summarize from memory. Unable to see the text, they are forced to build their own sentence — copying becomes physically impossible.
  • Özet = ana fikir + kilit noktalar, çok daha kısa, nesnel, kendi cümlelerinle.
  • Paraphrase boyu korur (fotokopi); özet küçültür (thumbnail) ve ayrıntıyı atar.
  • Çekirdek kalıp: [Kaynak] + aktarma fiili + that + ana fikir.
  • Aktarma fiilleri anlam taşır: suggests (temkinli) < argues (güçlü) < proves (kesin). Yanlış fiil = çarpıtma.
  • Nesnel kal: kendi yorumunu (foolishly, sadly) katma; eleştirin ayrı, özetin dışında.
  • Ayrıntıyı ele, çekirdeği tut; ama ana bulgu rakamı ve teknik terim korunur.
  • Kaynağı belirt: According to X (year)... — 'according to me' YANLIŞ.
  • Akademik gelenek: present zaman (argues), 'that'-cümlesi tam cümle, Yönlendirme Dili ile akıcı bağla.
  • Türk öğrenci tuzakları: 'say-s' unutma, 'talk about that', 'according to my opinion', 'that why', 'mention about'.
  • Üç adımlık formül: özü sadakatle yakala — kendi sesinle ifade et — gerisini geride bırak.
  • Summary = main idea + key points, much shorter, objective, in your own sentences.
  • Paraphrase keeps the size (a photocopy); a summary shrinks it (a thumbnail) and drops the detail.
  • The core pattern: [Source] + reporting verb + that + main idea.
  • Reporting verbs carry meaning: suggests (cautious) < argues (strong) < proves (definite). The wrong verb = distortion.
  • Stay objective: do not add your own comment (foolishly, sadly); your criticism is separate, outside the summary.
  • Filter out the detail, keep the core; but the main-finding figure and technical terms are kept.
  • State the source: According to X (year)... — 'according to me' is WRONG.
  • The academic convention: present tense (argues), a 'that'-clause is a full sentence, link fluently with Signposting.
  • Turkish-student traps: forgetting 'say-s', 'talk about that', 'according to my opinion', 'that why', 'mention about'.
  • The three-step formula: capture the essence faithfully — express it in your own voice — leave the rest behind.
