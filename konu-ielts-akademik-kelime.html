1. Academic Word List (AWL) nedir, neden önemlidir?

Academic Word List (AWL), akademik metinlerde — makaleler, ders kitapları, araştırma yazıları — sürekli tekrar eden ama günlük konuşmada nadir görülen kelimelerin listesidir. Fikir şudur: akademik yazının belirli bir "orta katmanı" vardır. Bu katman ne alana özgü teknik terimlerdir (photosynthesis, mitochondria) ne de en temel günlük kelimelerdir (house, water, go). Aradaki bu akademik çekirdek kelimeler her disiplinde ortaktır: analyse, factor, significant, approach, concept, establish. Kaynak akademik metnin büyük bir bölümünü işte bu ortak katman taşır.

IELTS Academic bunu doğrudan ödüllendirir. Değerlendirici, Lexical Resource ölçütünde "konuya uygun, az-yaygın (less common) kelime" arar. Akademik çekirdek kelime tam olarak bu tanıma girer: günlük değil, ama zorlama da değil; doğal biçimde akademiktir. Bir aday The government should think about this yerine The government should consider this approach yazdığında, aynı fikri daha akademik bir kayıtta ifade etmiş olur ve puan yükselir.

“Akademik kelime "zor kelime" değildir; akademik metinde sık geçen kelimedir. Zorluk değil, kayıt (register) önemlidir.

Bu sayfada listenin kendisini kopyalamıyoruz; yalnızca mantığını anlatıyor ve tümüyle kendi özgün örnek kelime ve cümlelerimizle gösteriyoruz. Ham kelime çalışmasını sayfa sonundaki Kelime Bankası'ndan yaparsın.

Kelime ailesi mantığı

AWL "kelime" değil "kelime ailesi" sayar. Bir aile, kök kelime + türevlerinden oluşur. Örneğin analyse ailesi tek bir kalem sayılır ama şunları kapsar: analyses, analysed, analysing, analysis, analyst, analytical, analytically. Sen bir aileyi öğrenirken tüm biçimlerini öğrenmelisin, çünkü sınavda çoğu zaman isim biçimi (analysis) yerine sıfat biçimi (analytical) gerekir.

Akademik metnin kelime katmanlarını üç kata ayırmak, neye çalışman gerektiğini netleştirir. En altta yüksek-frekanslı çekirdek (günlük 2000 kelime) vardır; bunu zaten bilirsin. En üstte alana özgü teknik terimler vardır; bunları yalnızca ilgili pasajda görürsün. İkisinin arasında, her disiplinde ortak olan akademik çekirdek katman yatar — işte IELTS'in ödüllendirdiği ve bu sayfanın odaklandığı katman budur.

Alt kat — günlük çekirdek

Konuşmanın gövdesi: people, make, good, think, want. Anlaşılırsın ama akademik değilsin. IELTS bu katmanda kalırsan Band 5-6'da tutar.

Orta kat — akademik çekirdek

Her makalede geçen ortak katman: significant, establish, factor, approach, consequence. Getirisi en yüksek katman; Band 7+ tam olarak burada başlar.

Üst kat — teknik terim

Alana özgü: photosynthesis, inflation, algorithm. Yalnız konu gerektiriyorsa kullan; ezberlemeye değil, tanımaya çalış.

Bu üç katın anlamı şudur: sınırlı çalışma zamanını orta kata yatır. Alt katı zaten biliyorsun, üst katı ise pasaj sana verir. Orta kattaki her yeni aile hem Reading'de daha hızlı okumanı hem de Writing ve Speaking'de daha akademik üretmeni sağlar — yani dört becerinin dördüne birden dokunur. Bu yüzden akademik çekirdek kelime, IELTS hazırlığında en yüksek "getiri/zaman" oranına sahip yatırımdır.

1. What is the Academic Word List (AWL), and why does it matter?

The Academic Word List (AWL) is a list of words that recur constantly in academic texts — articles, textbooks, research papers — but are rare in everyday speech. The idea is this: academic writing has a certain "middle layer". This layer is neither field-specific technical terms (photosynthesis, mitochondria) nor the most basic everyday words (house, water, go). This academic core in between is common to every discipline: analyse, factor, significant, approach, concept, establish. A large part of a source academic text is carried by exactly this common layer.

IELTS Academic rewards this directly. In the Lexical Resource criterion, the examiner looks for "topic-appropriate, less common (less common) vocabulary". Academic core words fit this description exactly: not everyday, but not forced either; naturally academic. When a candidate writes The government should consider this approach instead of The government should think about this, they express the same idea in a more academic register and the score rises.

“An academic word is not a "hard word"; it is a word that occurs frequently in academic text. What matters is not difficulty but register.

The word-family logic

The AWL counts not "words" but "word families". A family consists of a root word + its derivatives. For example, the analyse family counts as a single item but covers: analyses, analysed, analysing, analysis, analyst, analytical, analytically. When you learn a family, you should learn all its forms, because in the exam you often need the adjective form (analytical) rather than the noun (analysis).

Dividing the vocabulary layers of an academic text into three tiers clarifies what you need to study. At the bottom is the high-frequency core (the everyday 2000 words); you already know these. At the top are field-specific technical terms; you see these only in the relevant passage. Between the two lies the academic core layer, common to every discipline — this is the layer IELTS rewards and this page focuses on.

Bottom tier — everyday core

The body of speech: people, make, good, think, want. You are understood but not academic. Staying in this layer keeps IELTS at Band 5-6.

Middle tier — academic core

The common layer in every article: significant, establish, factor, approach, consequence. The highest-return layer; Band 7+ begins exactly here.

Top tier — technical terms

Field-specific: photosynthesis, inflation, algorithm. Use only if the topic requires it; aim to recognise, not memorise.

The meaning of these three tiers is this: invest your limited study time in the middle tier. You already know the bottom tier, and the passage gives you the top tier. Every new family in the middle tier lets you both read faster in Reading and produce more academically in Writing and Speaking — so it touches all four skills at once. That is why academic core vocabulary is the investment with the highest "return per hour" in IELTS preparation.

2. The AWL sublist logic — 10 tiers from most to least frequent

The AWL divides words into 10 sublists by frequency. Sublist 1 holds the families most frequent in academic texts, Sublist 10 the least frequent. This is a golden map for study order: start with Sublist 1 and work down. The early tiers are the highest-return words, because they are both the words you read most often and the ones most useful in your own writing.

Let's make this tier difference concrete from both ends. significant in Sublist 1 is a word so useful that it comes in handy at least once in almost every Task 2 essay: it flows naturally to qualify a trend, a difference or a finding (a significant proportion, significant improvement, significantly higher). So you should automate it not to memory level but to reflex level — it must come without thinking. The Turkish student's typical error here is insisting on big, thinking it means "big", instead of significant; that single choice keeps a sentence in the Band 6 register. On the other hand, notwithstanding in Sublist 9, however strong its meaning ("despite"), fits correctly only in a very specific syntactic context and creates artificiality when misused. The lesson the tier gives is clear: early-tier words are learned for production, later-tier words mostly for recognition. When you see notwithstanding in Reading, it is enough to know its meaning; you don't have to force it into your own writing.

“A word's tier tells you not its difficulty but how often it will be useful. Sublist 1 is needed every day, Sublist 10 a few times a year — set your study order accordingly.
The wrong strategy

Memorising the rare words in Sublist 10 while skipping Sublist 1. Knowing notwithstanding looks impressive, but in the exam you use significant, approach and factor in every essay. Automate the frequent ones first.

Yükseltme "eş anlamlı bul, yapıştır" değildir. obtain ile get aynı çekirdek anlamı taşır ama acquire daha çok "zamanla edinme"dir. Yanlış nüans, akademik görünmek isterken hataya dönüşür. Her yükseltmeyi bir örnek cümlede gör, sonra kullan.

Memorising these pairs is not enough; you must see which fits which context. For example, obtain is a formal "getting" (data, permission, a result); acquire is acquiring over time (a skill, a habit, a company). Likewise, demonstrate is "to show with proof", while indicate is "to point to / suggest" — a graph indicates a trend but research demonstrates a link. Commit the upgrade pair to memory together with an example sentence; word + context stick together.

Let us underline the three highest-return pairs in this table in particular, because these are the three places where marks are most often lost in the exam. First, show → demonstrate/indicate/illustrate: many candidates repeat show throughout the essay, whereas "to establish with proof" (demonstrate), "to point to in a graph" (indicate) and "to explain with an example" (illustrate) are different jobs; choosing the right one gains both variety in Lexical Resource and precision of meaning, and these two things are exactly the definition of Band 7. Second, important → crucial/essential/paramount: the trap here is that the three are not the same — essential is "indispensable" (a condition), crucial is "decisive" (affecting an outcome), and paramount is "above all else" and sounds over-formal if overused. Third, so → therefore/consequently/thus: when the Turkish student carries the conversational "so" into writing, the examiner reads it instantly as low register; a single consequently lifts the same sentence to an academic plane. In other words, the upgrade pairs are not decoration but tools that touch the band criteria directly.

Raise the register, not the meaning

An upgrade is not "find a synonym and paste it". obtain and get share the same core meaning, but acquire is more about "acquiring over time". A wrong nuance turns into an error while you are trying to look academic. See every upgrade in an example sentence, then use it.

Let's open up in depth the three collocations Turkish students stumble over most in this list, because these break down when translated word for word from Turkish. The first is conduct research: because Turkish says "do research", the hand automatically writes make research or do research — do research may pass in speech, but the standard verb of academic writing is conduct (conduct a study, conduct an experiment); make research is simply wrong and drops Lexical Resource instantly in the examiner's eyes. 5. Nominalisation and academic register

Nominalisation is turning a verb or adjective into a noun to make a sentence denser and more academic. Everyday language is verb-heavy; academic language is noun-heavy. For example, Prices increased rapidly, and this affected demand is an everyday construction; its academic form is The rapid increase in prices affected demand. When the verb (increase) becomes a noun, the sentence contracts and a phrase does the job of a clause.

Why does nominalisation earn marks? Because it delivers two core features of academic writing at once: density and objectivity. Nominalisation lets you detach an event from its subject and treat it as an independent concept. People consume too much energy is personal and everyday; Excessive energy consumption is a measurable, debatable concept — it can be the subject of a sentence, take an adjective, be embedded in another sentence.

Verb-heavy (everyday)

Because cities grew so fast, they became polluted, and this made people unhealthy.

Three separate clauses; loose and conversational in tone.

↔
Noun-heavy (academic)

The rapid growth of cities and the resulting pollution have contributed to declining public health.

The same idea; two noun phrases, one dense sentence.

Nominalisation recipe (3 steps)

1) Find the main verb or adjective in your sentence (prices increased). 2) Turn it into a noun (the increase in prices). 3) Make that noun the subject of a new sentence and link it with a strong academic verb (The increase in prices reduced demand). Thus two everyday sentences merge into a single academic one.

The over-nominalisation trap

Drowning every sentence in nouns makes the writing heavy and unreadable. Nominalisation is a tool, not a goal. In IELTS seek a natural balance: let some sentences be verb-heavy, some noun-heavy. For detail, see the Nominalization and Academic Register pages.

When paraphrasing, watch for three slips: meaning slip (the word you thought was a synonym actually means something different), register slip (the word you chose belongs to speech), and collocation slip (the word is right but the verb/preposition beside it is wrong). For example, escalate and rise both mean "increase", but escalate is used only for negative things like conflict, tension or cost — happiness escalated is wrong. So use a synonym only after seeing it in an example sentence.

Let us open up three of these groups a little more, because the words in them look synonymous but do not in fact substitute freely for one another. In the "problem" group, issue is neutral and the safest choice, whereas challenge frames the problem as "a surmountable difficulty" — it adds a positive tone, so saying challenge when you mean to condemn an obstacle weakens your message. dilemma, meanwhile, is "being caught between two bad options" and cannot be used for just any problem; saying dilemma in the wrong place shows not that you know the word but that you don't know its nuance. In the "cause" group, lead to and result in state a neutral consequence, but trigger implies a sudden, sharp setting-off, while give rise to implies a slow, gradual producing — trigger suits a sudden jump in a graph, give rise to a long-term social outcome. In the "effect" group, register comes into play: repercussion is an advanced word for heavy, mostly negative and distant consequences; using it for every "effect" sounds over-formal, whereas impact and consequence flow safely in almost any context. This is exactly what separates Band 7 from Band 8: the candidate does not merely vary, but weighs correctly the subtle load each synonym carries.

“Paraphrase does not mean "change the word" but "carry the same meaning with a different but correct word". A wrong synonym loses more marks than repeating.

Caution — technical terms are kept

Do not try to replace every word with a synonym. Technical terms (temperature, percentage, unemployment, carbon dioxide) are not paraphrased; they are kept. A forced synonym breaks naturalness and lowers Lexical Resource. The aim is natural variety, not show.

8. Task 1 data language — academic bank

In Writing Task 1 you describe a graph, table or process in a neutral, academic language. This word bank does three jobs: trend (direction and speed), comparison (difference and similarity) and proportion/quantity (academic quantity expressions instead of exact figures). This bank deepens the general Task 1 language of this page's sister page in terms of academic register.

a) Trend — direction and speed

Decrease (verb)fall, decline, drop, decrease, plunge, plummet, dip

Decrease (noun)a fall, a decline, a drop, a downturn, a reduction

In the "stability" row, level off and plateau describe the moment a curve moves from rising into a flat course: After a steep climb, sales plateaued at around 400 units and held steady for three years. In comparison language, the real power move is the structures that compare two pieces of data in a single sentence. The pattern more than twice as high as gives the proportional difference in one stroke and replaces the weak much bigger than: Male enrolment was more than twice as high as female enrolment in 1990. exceed and outnumber are academic verbs for "to surpass / be more numerous than"; their difference is this — outnumber is for countable entities only (Tourists outnumbered locals), while exceed is for amounts and proportions (Costs exceeded the budget). c) Proportion and quantity — academic quantity

FunctionAcademic language

In proportion language, the aim is to present the raw figure to the reader in an "interpreted" form. account for, make up, represent and constitute all four mean "to make up such-and-such of a whole" and are interchangeable: Imports constituted nearly 60 per cent of the total. Approximation expressions soften and naturalise an exact figure — just over shows a little above the number, just under a little below: The figure stood at just under 15 per cent. In the "majority/minority" row, the vast majority describes an overwhelming majority (roughly 80%+), while a small fraction describes a very small portion; the Turkish student's trap here is confusing amount with an uncountable noun while saying the vast majority of people and producing a large amount of people — because people are countable, the correct form is a large number of people. Even this small distinction determines the academic credibility of your description.

The register trap in Task 1

Task 1 is objective description — no opinion, no cause-guessing, no I think. Also, do not try to paraphrase technical terms (percentage, kilowatt, population); they are kept. Variety is sought in direction/speed words, not in the unit of measurement.

9. Task 2 argument language — academic bank

In Writing Task 2 you generate ideas, discuss and justify. This bank covers the four functions of an argument in an academic register: cause-effect, contrast and concession, expressing an opinion, and exemplification/generalisation. Learning these patterns closes the gap of "I know what to say, but how do I say it academically?".

a) Cause-effect

FunctionAcademic language
Showing a causestem from, arise from, be attributed to, be rooted in, owing to
Showing an effectlead to, result in, give rise to, bring about, trigger, contribute to
Connectorconsequently, therefore, as a result, thus, hence
Traffic congestion largely stems from poor urban planning and, if unaddressed, may give rise to chronic air pollution.

In cause-effect language the most critical distinction is "direction": are you going from effect back to cause, or from cause forward to effect? stem from, arise from and be attributed to look back from the effect to the cause (The decline can be attributed to rising costs); lead to, result in and give rise to look forward from the cause to the effect (Rising costs led to a decline). Confusing the two reverses the logic of the sentence. be attributed to is especially strong because its passive structure adds objectivity — instead of "I claim", it says "is attributed to", which is exactly the tone sought in academic register. The Turkish student's classic error is repeating because of in every sentence; whereas a couple of variables like owing to or stem from carry the same idea without the burden of repetition and raise both Coherence and Lexical Resource together.

b) Contrast and concession

FunctionAcademic language
Setting up contrasthowever, nevertheless, on the other hand, conversely, in contrast
Making a concessionalthough, while it is true that, admittedly, despite the fact that
Returning after a concessionnonetheless, even so, that said, this does not mean that
While it is true that tourism boosts local economies, it can, nonetheless, place severe strain on fragile ecosystems.

The concession structure is the backbone of a Band 7+ argument, because it shows you acknowledge the opposing view yet still defend your own thesis — this "balanced thinking" is what the examiner directly looks for. while it is true that and admittedly grant a point to the other side (Admittedly, the policy is expensive), then nonetheless or even so returns to your thesis (even so, its long-term benefits outweigh the cost). This "concede, then rebut" move manages two viewpoints in a single sentence. Another very common Turkish-student error here is gluing connectors together with wrong punctuation: however and nevertheless join two separate sentences with a semicolon or a full stop (The plan is costly; however, it is necessary), whereas although builds a subordinate clause within one sentence (Although the plan is costly, it is necessary) — mixing the two and saying Although the plan is costly, however it is necessary is a double-connector error and lowers both flow and grammar marks.

c) Expressing an opinion and tone (hedging)

FunctionAcademic language
Opening an opinionit can be argued that, from my perspective, I would contend that
Measured expression (hedge)tend to, be likely to, may, arguably, to some extent, in most cases
Emphasis / certaintyundoubtedly, clearly, it is evident that, there is little doubt that
It can be argued that stricter regulation would, to some extent, curb the problem, though it is unlikely to eliminate it entirely.

In opinion language the real skill is "measured speech" (hedging) — the academic writer does not speak in absolutes but adds probability and degree. tend to softens a general tendency (Wealthier nations tend to consume more energy) and rescues you from an indefensible absolute like all rich nations consume more. to some extent and arguably leave room in the claim (This is, arguably, the main cause), so you do not appear over-assertive before the examiner. When opening an opinion, it can be argued that is an objective and safe start; I would contend that states a stronger personal stance and should be saved for a thesis you will genuinely defend. The Turkish student's most expensive error runs the other way: writing with absolutes like always, never, everyone, completely. This certainty looks not strong but refutable, so it weakens the argument; adding a single in most cases or may lifts the same sentence from Band 6 to Band 7-8 maturity.

d) Exemplification and generalisation

FunctionAcademic language
Giving an examplefor instance, to illustrate, a case in point, such as
Generalisingon the whole, broadly speaking, in general, as a rule
Adding / reinforcingmoreover, furthermore, in addition, what is more
Broadly speaking, renewable energy is expanding; a case in point is Scandinavia, where wind power now supplies a substantial share of the grid.

Exemplification language makes an argument convincing by grounding your abstract claim on concrete footing. The phrase a case in point is a more academic and emphatic form of for example; it says "here is an example that fits exactly this situation" and stands strong right after a thesis sentence (Some cities have reversed this trend; a case in point is Copenhagen). to illustrate, meanwhile, is the formal way to bring an example at the start of a sentence (To illustrate, consider the impact of remote work). On the generalisation side, broadly speaking and on the whole say "with exceptions, but generally" and precisely for that reason act as a hedge — protecting you from absolutes. The Turkish student's trap here is mismatching the addition connectors: moreover and furthermore are for adding a new and separate piece of evidence; using moreover while restating the same idea in different words signals "the content isn't progressing" to the examiner and pulls the Coherence and Cohesion score directly down.

“The most common "fancy word" error in Task 2 is forgetting the measured expression (hedge). The academic writer does not say always, never, everyone; they say tend to, in most cases, arguably. Not certainty but a balanced argument carries Band 8.

10. Band 5 → 7 → 9: the same idea, raised through academic vocabulary

How does a single idea — "the internet helps students" — change across three bands through academic word choice?

BandSentence
5The internet is a good thing and it helps students a lot in their study.
7The internet is highly beneficial, as it provides students with significant support in their studies.
9Digital connectivity has proved invaluable to learners, granting them access to a vast body of academic resources and thereby facilitating independent study.

What changed?

  • 5→7: the vague words are gone (good thing → beneficial); helps a lot → provides significant support turned into a collocation.
  • 7→9: academic core (connectivity, invaluable, access, facilitate); nominalisation (independent study); fluent and no forcing.

Let's see the same upgrade in two more ideas. Notice the pattern: at each step, vague word → academic core, everyday verb → collocation, loose structure → nominalisation.

BandIdea: "there are too many cars in cities and this causes pollution"
5There are too many cars in cities and this makes a lot of pollution.
7The rising number of vehicles in urban areas has led to significant air pollution.
9The proliferation of private vehicles in densely populated cities has been a major contributor to deteriorating air quality.
BandIdea: "the government should spend more money on education"
5The government should give more money to schools because education is important.
7Governments should allocate greater funding to education, as it plays a crucial role in development.
9Increased investment in education is arguably justified, given its pivotal role in fostering long-term economic and social development.

Let's decode word by word why the upgrade earns marks in these two examples, because seeing the pattern is the only way to carry it into your own writing. In the "cars" example, Band 5's too many cars becomes the proliferation of private vehicles at Band 9: two things are gained at once here — proliferation is both a less-common academic noun (Lexical Resource) and a nominalisation, i.e. it compresses the idea "there are many cars" into a single noun phrase (density). makes pollution becomes has been a major contributor to deteriorating air quality: the weak verb make is gone, replaced by a correct collocation (a contributor to) and a strong adjective-noun pair (deteriorating air quality) — and moreover a major contributor is a hedge that avoids the "sole cause" claim, so objectivity is gained too. In the "education" example, Band 5's vague important rises to its pivotal role in fostering ... development at Band 9: pivotal means "decisive/central" and carries far more precise weight than important; foster ("to nurture/develop") links to the noun development as a collocation; and the opening arguably justified is a hedge that keeps the claim measured. As you can see, each upgrade is not randomly dropping a "hard word" but gathering three criteria (core word + correct collocation + measured objectivity) in the same sentence — and what makes Band 9 is exactly having all three together.

The upgrade pattern

In all three examples the change runs on the same three axes: (1) too many cars → the proliferation of private vehicles (nominalisation + core word), (2) makes pollution → contributor to deteriorating air quality (collocation), (3) an absolute statement → a measured one (arguably, a major contributor). Raise your own sentences by asking these three axes.

Bağlama oturmayan ezberlenmiş "band 9 cümlesi" yapıştırmak. Değerlendirici bunu görür ve puanı düşürür. Kelimeyi kendi bağlamında üret.

7) Türkçe düşünüp çevirme

Türkçe collocation'ı kelimesi kelimesine çevirmek: make a decision doğru ama do a decision değil; take a photo doğru, throw a photo değil. Eşi İngilizce mantıkla öğren.

8) Kesinlik fazlası

always, never, all people, everything gibi mutlak ifadeler argümanı zayıflatır. Akademik yazar ölçülüdür: tend to, in many cases, arguably.

Ortak payda

Sekiz hatanın hepsinin kökü tektir: kelimeyi bağlamsız öğrenmek. Tek başına ezberlenen kelime yanlış nüansla, yanlış eşle, yanlış kayıtta çıkar. Çözüm de tektir: her kelimeyi bir örnek cümle + bir collocation ile öğren, sonra kendi cümlende üret. Tanıma öğrenme değildir; üretim öğrenmedir.

11. Common mistakes (Turkish students)

1) Over-formal

Cramming a Sublist 9-10 word into every sentence (notwithstanding, heretofore). The result is artificial and unreadable. Academic ≠ archaic.

2) Wrong collocation

make a research, do a conclusion, give an impact are wrong. Learn the word with its partner: conduct research, draw a conclusion, have an impact.

3) Register clash

Everyday words in an academic essay: kids, stuff, a lot of, get, guy. Instead: children, aspects, numerous, obtain, individual.

4) Wrong nuance

Pasting a synonym blindly: escalate is not for every increase but for a negative/dangerous one. Learn the nuance in an example sentence.

5) Learning a family incompletely

Knowing only the noun form (analysis) but not the adjective (analytical) or verb (analyse). Learn the whole family.

6) Memorised sentences

Pasting a memorised "band 9 sentence" that doesn't fit the context. The examiner sees this and lowers the score. Produce the word in your own context.

7) Thinking in Turkish and translating

Translating a Turkish collocation word for word: make a decision is right but do a decision is not; take a photo is right, throw a photo is not. Learn the partner by English logic.

8) Too much certainty

Absolute expressions like always, never, all people, everything weaken the argument. The academic writer is measured: tend to, in many cases, arguably.

The common denominator

The root of all eight mistakes is one: learning a word out of context. A word memorised alone comes out with the wrong nuance, the wrong partner, the wrong register. The solution is also one: learn every word with an example sentence + a collocation, then produce it in your own sentence. Recognition is not learning; production is learning.

12. How to study — making academic vocabulary stick

Academic vocabulary is learned through "production", not "recognition". A word is yours only once you have used it correctly in your own sentence. Here is a proven method:

  1. Collect as a family. Write a new word not alone but with its family: significant / significance / significantly. Add one correct collocation beside it.
  2. Flashcards + spaced repetition. Turkish meaning on the front, English chunk on the back. Review at 1-3-7-14 day intervals; break the forgetting curve.
  3. Active recall. Once a week, look at the Turkish and produce the English chunk (not the other way round). A word you cannot produce is not yet yours.
  4. Study by theme. One week Environment, one week Education. In the exam you come ready theme by theme.
  5. Write your own sentence. One original sentence for each word. If you didn't write it, you haven't learned it; the illusion of recognition does not guarantee production.
“8-10 academic words a day, as families and collocations, with your own sentence. More will not stick; less will not progress.

Setting up the flashcard correctly doubles the return on your study. A weak card carries only "word = meaning"; a strong card demands a production. A good academic word card has four elements: the word family, a correct collocation, a short example sentence and — most importantly — the obligation to produce a sentence of your own before you flip the card.

The logic of spaced repetition is simple: if you recall a piece of information just as you are about to forget it, that information settles far deeper. So review a new word on days 1, 3, 7 and 14; widen the interval on each correct recall. Reset the interval when you recall it wrongly. This way your time budget goes to the words you struggle with, not the ones you already know.

When we pour these principles into a four-week sample plan, the study becomes concrete. Each week you both acquire new words and review the previous weeks' cards; thus knowledge accumulates rather than evaporating.

Week 1 — Foundation

Sublist 1-2 + the Environment theme. 8 families a day, each with a collocation and example sentence. At the weekend: an original 5-sentence paragraph.

Week 2 — Expansion

Sublist 3-4 + Education & Technology. Apply the upgrade pairs: take an old piece of writing and replace the everyday words with academic ones.

Week 3 — Depth

Sublist 5-6 + Health & Work. Practise nominalisation: turn 10 verb-heavy sentences into noun-heavy ones.

Week 4 — Production

A selection from Sublist 7+ + a mixed review of all themes. Write a full Task 2 essay, feeding it from the banks on this page.
