Grammar: Nominalisation & Grammatical Metaphor

This is how a sentence grows up: actions harden into things, clauses fold into nouns, and prose gains the density of the academic page.

Turning happenings into things

A child says, "They failed, so the project stopped." A scholar writes, "Their failure led to the termination of the project." The facts are identical; what has changed is the grammar. Verbs (failed, stopped) have become nouns (failure, termination), and a story of people doing things has been repackaged as a chain of abstract entities. Michael Halliday, the linguist who mapped this move, called it grammatical metaphor: expressing a process not as a verb, where it "naturally" lives, but as a noun.

The tool that drives it is nominalisation — converting a verb or adjective into a noun (implement → implementation, dense → density). It is the single most important grammatical feature of advanced written English. Master it and you can compress a paragraph into a phrase, make an idea the subject of the next sentence, and sound authoritative. Overuse it, however, and your prose turns to concrete. This unit is about control of the dial, not maximum density.

  • They implemented the reforms, and the economy recovered.Congruent (verbal) → plain, human, agent visible.
  • The implementation of the reforms led to an economic recovery.Metaphorical (nominal) → dense, abstract, agent hidden.
  • Because prices rose sharply, people protested.Congruent → cause shown by a conjunction ("because").
  • The sharp rise in prices provoked protest.Metaphorical → the causal verb "provoke" links two nouns.

1. Congruent vs metaphorical (process → entity)

Halliday distinguishes the congruent wording — where a process is a verb and a quality is an adjective, matching how meaning is first learned — from the metaphorical wording, where that same process is re-cast as a noun. Nominalisation is the bridge between them. Learning to see both versions of an idea, and to move between them at will, is the core C2 skill of this unit.

Congruent (verbal) — the "story"Metaphorical (nominal) — the "thing"What shifted

Why "metaphor"?
  • It is metaphor because one grammatical class stands in for another: a noun (thing) represents what is really a process (verb). The meaning is transferred across a grammatical boundary.
  • The congruent form is not "wrong" or childish — it is simply closer to the event. The nominal form trades that immediacy for compactness and abstraction.

2. How to nominalise (verb / adjective → noun)

Most nominalisation happens through a small set of suffixes (-tion, -ment, -al, -ance, -ure, -th, -ity) — but the suffix must match the stem, and the spelling often shifts. Getting the derivation right is half the battle; the other half is choosing the correct preposition that the new noun demands.

C2 subtlety — derivation traps
  • Not every noun is regular: maintain → maintenance (not "maintainance"), abolish → abolition (not "abolishment"), pronounce → pronunciation (the "o" drops).
  • The wrong preposition betrays a non-native ear: it is a solution to a problem, an increase in costs, an investigation into fraud — never "solution of / increase of / investigation on".

3. Packaging & subject-making (information as a "thing" you can move)

The deepest power of nominalisation is not compression but packaging: once an entire event is a noun, you can put it in the subject slot, refer back to it, and build the next sentence on top of it. This is how academic prose achieves flow — each sentence turns the previous clause into a starting-point. Grammarians call this theme: what the sentence is "about", placed first.

Congruent chain (clunky)Nominalised chain (cohesive)
The factory closed. Because it closed, the town suffered, and because the town suffered, people left. The factory closed. This closure devastated the town, and the resulting decline drove residents away.
Subject-making in action
  • The noun closure repackages the whole first sentence and becomes the subject of the second — the reader is carried forward smoothly.
  • Nominalisation lets you evaluate a process compactly: "This unexpected closure..." adds judgement to the packaged event that a verb clause cannot carry as neatly.

4. The trade-off (density & objectivity vs readability)

Every nominalisation buys something and costs something. It buys density (more meaning per word), abstraction (ideas rather than anecdotes) and objectivity (the human agent can be dropped). It costs readability: the reader must unpack noun after noun, and the person responsible disappears. The C2 writer weighs the two on every sentence.

C2 subtlety — the bureaucrat's trick
  • Agent-hiding can be honest (science: the finding matters, not the finder) or evasive (politics: "an error occurred" instead of "we failed"). Read the purpose behind the density.
  • Density is a tool, not a virtue. "The implementation of a reduction of expenditure" is not cleverer than "cutting costs" — only heavier.

5. When to re-verbalise (turning the noun back into a verb)

Mastery is bidirectional. Just as you nominalise to pack and connect, you re-verbalise — unfold a heavy noun back into a verb — wherever the reader needs air. A public leaflet, an instruction, a warm email, a key sentence you must not let the reader miss: all call for the congruent, verbal form. The skill is knowing which sentences to leave dense and which to release.

Your consideration of an extension of the deadline would be appreciated.I would be grateful if you could extend the deadline.A human request should sound like a person asking.
There was a failure in the observation of the protocol.The team did not follow the protocol.Naming the agent restores accountability and clarity.

The Noun That Ate the Sentence

Somewhere between the plain sentence a child would speak and the dense prose of a research journal lies a single, powerful transformation. The linguist Michael Halliday gave it a name: grammatical metaphor. Where an everyday speaker says, "The engineers worked hard, so the bridge was finished early," an academic writer packages the whole event into nouns: "The engineers' diligence led to the early completion of the bridge." Nothing is lost; the actions have simply become things. This turning of processes into entities is called nominalisation, and it is the engine of formal, written English.

To grasp why this move should feel like a metaphor at all, it helps to recall how meaning is first acquired. A small child maps processes onto verbs and qualities onto adjectives: things happen, and people do them to one another. This is what Halliday termed the congruent wording — the form in which grammar mirrors lived experience most faithfully. Nominalisation departs from that natural correspondence, allowing a noun to stand in for what is really an action or a state. The transfer across grammatical classes is precisely what earns the label metaphorical: the ice no longer melts, but undergoes "the melting of the ice"; the argument no longer persuades, but possesses "persuasiveness". Once a writer can see both faces of a single meaning, the congruent and the metaphorical, and can travel freely between them, the whole resource finally opens up.

The appeal is real. Nominalisation compresses. A clause that once sprawled across a line can be folded into a single noun phrase and made the subject of a new sentence: "This completion reassured investors." Consequently, ideas can be stacked, referred back to, and built upon with remarkable economy. Moreover, by hiding the human agent — who exactly was diligent? — the style acquires an air of objectivity that science and law prize. The writing sounds detached, weighty and authoritative.

This capacity to nominalise is, above all, what lends expository prose its forward momentum. Because a completed clause can be sealed inside a noun, that noun may be lifted into the opening slot of the next sentence and made the starting-point — the theme — of all that follows. "The engineers finished the bridge" becomes "This completion reassured investors", and the completion, in turn, may be evaluated, qualified or contested: "This unexpected completion", "The premature completion", "Such completions". Each packaged event thus works as a hinge, fastening what has just been said to what comes next; and it is this quiet chaining of nouns, far more than any array of connectives, that produces the seamless texture readers admire in a well-argued paper. Compression, then, is only half the gift the technique bestows; the other half is cohesion.

Yet the same virtue is also the vice. When every verb hardens into a noun, prose grows airless and abstract. "The implementation of the reduction of expenditure" says less clearly what "cutting costs" says at once. Readers must unpack noun after noun, and the living agent vanishes behind a wall of -tion and -ment. Notwithstanding its prestige, over-nominalised writing can become opaque rather than clear, and bureaucrats have long exploited exactly that fog.

The consequences of that fog are worth weighing genre by genre. In the sciences the deletion of the agent is often honest and even desirable: what matters is that a reaction was observed, not who held the pipette, and the impersonal nominal keeps the reader's eye on the finding rather than the finder. In law and administration the same device can serve precision, folding an intricate provision into a single manageable phrase. But in politics and public relations it slides all too readily into evasion. "Mistakes were made", "a downsizing of the workforce was undertaken", "regrettable casualties occurred" — each sentence performs a small vanishing act, dissolving a decision and its author into an event that appears to have happened of its own accord. The grammar that lends science its objectivity lends the spin doctor his alibi, and the discerning reader learns to interrogate every weighty noun: who did this, and to whom?

The C2 skill, therefore, is not to nominalise as much as possible but to control the dial. A skilled writer nominalises to pack and connect ideas, then deliberately re-verbalises — turning the nouns back into verbs — wherever clarity matters more than density. Abstraction is a tool to be reached for, not a virtue to be displayed.

How, in practice, does a writer decide? The governing question is never "How impressive can I make this look?" but "What must this particular reader do with the sentence?" An examiner rewarding academic range, an editor policing a word limit, a specialist who already shares the vocabulary — all of these can absorb, and indeed expect, a high density of packed nouns. A patient reading a discharge summary, a citizen scanning a safety notice, a customer skimming an email cannot, and for them the buried verb must be dug out and set loose: "use the sanitiser", "we changed the method", "the team did not follow the protocol". Re-verbalising is therefore no retreat into simplicity but an act of consideration — a recognition that one and the same idea belongs at different points on the dial for different readers.

None of this control is possible, however, without command of the machinery beneath it. Each nominalisation must be derived correctly — "abolish" yields "abolition", not "abolishment"; "maintain" yields "maintenance", not "maintainance"; "pronounce" yields "pronunciation", with the middle vowel quietly gone — and each must be followed by the preposition it silently demands: a solution to a problem, an increase in costs, an investigation into fraud, an effect on outcomes. A single wrong ending or misplaced preposition betrays the non-native ear more surely than any bold vocabulary can disguise, because these are the collocations native readers absorb without ever consciously learning them. Mastery at the level of the paragraph rests, in the end, on accuracy at the level of the word.

It is worth remembering, too, that the drift towards the nominal is a relatively recent and largely written phenomenon. Speech remains stubbornly verbal, thronged with people doing things to one another in real time; it is the printed report, the abstract, the statute and the policy paper that have, across the last few centuries, grown steadily more thing-like, cramming ever more meaning into ever fewer and ever heavier noun phrases. To write well at this level is thus partly to resist the gravitational pull of that tradition — to relish the density it affords without capitulating to it, and to keep always in reserve the plain human sentence a speaker would actually utter. The finest academic writers are, in this sense, bilingual within their own language, fluent in the dense idiom of the nominal and yet never deaf to the clarity of the verbal.

Halliday's insight endures because it reframes the choice. To nominalise is neither correct nor incorrect; it is a decision about who is reading and why. The measure of mastery is knowing when the noun should eat the sentence — and when it should be made to give the verb back.

Connectors that hold a dense text together (Linking Words)

Nominalisation packs ideas into nouns; connectors are what link those packed nouns into an argument. In dense academic prose, formal connectors (consequently, moreover, notwithstanding, thereby, whereas) do the work that plain conjunctions do in speech. As you pack, keep the connectors formal to match the density; as you re-verbalise, let them relax too.

Formal connectorIts plain-register twinDense example
consequentlyso / that's whyFunding was cut; consequently, the closure followed.
therebyso as to / and soThe reforms cut waste, thereby raising output.
moreoveralso / andThe rise was steep. Moreover, its effects spread.
notwithstandingdespite / even soNotwithstanding the cost, implementation continued.
whereaswhile / butThe verbal form breathes, whereas the nominal packs.
henceso / this is whyDensity obscures agents; hence its misuse.

See the difference — the same idea, two densities:

Dense / nominalised ✓Re-verbalised for a general reader ✓
The postponement of the launch, occasioned by a failure in supplier coordination, provoked considerable investor anxiety. Its resolution required a renegotiation of delivery schedules. We had to delay the launch because our suppliers were not coordinated, and this worried investors a lot. To fix it, we had to renegotiate when things would be delivered.
Rule
  • In the dense version, make nominalised phrases the subjects ("The postponement... provoked...") and link them with formal connectors.
  • In the re-verbalised version, restore the verbs and agents ("We had to delay...") and use their plain-connector twins.

The two versions carry an identical message, yet their density could not differ more. The first packs each process into a nominalised subject ("the postponement", "the reduction", "a renegotiation") and is largely agentless, which gives it an impersonal, authoritative cohesion suited to a report. The second re-verbalises every noun and restores the agents ("we", "the council"), trading density for clarity so a general reader can unpack it at once. Neither is better; each fits its reader.
