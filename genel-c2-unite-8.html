Grammar: Cohesion — Reference, Substitution & Ellipsis

By C2, sentences are no longer the unit that matters. The text is. Cohesion is the invisible thread that turns a heap of true statements into a single, flowing whole.

Cohesion is the thread you never see

At C2 you can already build any sentence you like. What now separates a masterful writer from a merely correct one is cohesion: the network of grammatical and lexical links that tie one sentence to the next so a reader glides through the text instead of stumbling over it. Cohesion is felt only when it fails — when a "this" points at nothing, when a noun is hammered five times, when a "the former" and "the latter" get crossed.

This unit gives you three tools for the job. Reference lets a small word (it, they, this, such) point to something rather than name it again. Substitution lets a stand-in (one, ones, do so, so, not) take the place of a longer item. Ellipsis simply drops what the reader can already recover. Above these sits lexical cohesion — the quiet chaining of synonyms and superordinates. The C2 skill is not knowing these devices but choosing the right one, each time, to keep a text connected and clean.

  • Sales collapsed in March. This alarmed the board.Reference → "this" points back to the whole event without renaming it.
  • I liked the grey coat, so I bought the navy one too.Substitution → "one" stands in for "coat".
  • Sara can drive, and Tom can too.Ellipsis → "[drive]" is dropped after "can" — the reader supplies it.
  • The eagle circled once; then the bird was gone.Lexical cohesion → a superordinate ("bird") replaces "eagle".

1. Reference (pointing instead of repeating)

A reference item does not carry meaning of its own; it borrows meaning from something else in the text. When it points back to a word already used, it is anaphoric — the everyday case. When it points forward to something named later, it is cataphoric — a marked, attention-grabbing move. The word it depends on is its antecedent (looking back) or its referent more generally.

Reference typeTypical itemsExample
personalit, they, this, theseThe plans arrived late; they were incomplete.
demonstrativethis, that, these, suchCosts rose and quality fell. Such a mix worries buyers.
comparativethe same, other, similarShe wanted a quiet office; I wanted the same.
Anaphoric vs cataphoric
  • Anaphoric (backward): "Ann resigned. It shocked us." — "it" looks back to the resignation.
  • Cataphoric (forward): "Consider this: no plan survives contact with reality." — "this" looks ahead, building suspense.

2. Demonstratives & ordering (this / that / such · the former / the latter)

Demonstratives let you package a whole idea into one pointer. This/these pull the referent close; that/those hold it at a distance; such refers to "a thing of that kind". For two items already named, the former means the first one and the latter means the second — a compact, formal way to sort them without repeating.

DeviceWhat it doesExample
this / thesepoints to the near / just-said ideaFunding was cut. This ended the project.
such"a thing of that kind"Delays, faults, refunds — such problems mount up.
the former / the latterfirst-mentioned / second-mentionedWe weighed rail and road; the former was greener.
C2 subtlety
  • the former = the one named first; the latter = the one named second. Mixing them is a classic C2 slip that reverses your meaning.
  • Use "the former/the latter" only for exactly two items, and only when the reader can still hold both in mind — otherwise just name them.

3. Substitution (one / ones · do so, do that · so / not)

Where reference points at a thing, substitution puts a lightweight stand-in in the same grammatical slot. Use one/ones to replace a countable noun, do so / do that to replace a verb phrase, and so / not to replace a whole clause after verbs such as think, hope, believe, be afraid.

SubstituteReplacesExample
one / onesa countable nounThese pens leak; I need new ones.
do so / do thata verb phraseYou were asked to sign it; please do so today.
so / nota whole clause"Will it rain?" — "I hope not."
C2 subtlety
  • do so is neat and formal; do it / do that are lighter and more conversational — pick by register.
  • one substitutes a count noun only: say "I want some" (not "some ones") for uncountables like advice or water.

4. Ellipsis (dropping what can be recovered)

Ellipsis is cohesion by subtraction: you leave a word out precisely because the reader can supply it from what came before. It is the tightest, most economical link of all — nothing stands in, and yet the connection is unmistakable. It works after auxiliaries, in coordinated clauses, and in short answers.

TypeFull formWith ellipsis
after an auxiliaryShe can swim and he can swim.She can swim and he can [ ] too.
coordinated clauseSome passed the exam; others did not pass.Some passed the exam; others did not [ ].
short answer"Are you coming?" — "Yes, I am coming.""Are you coming?" — "Yes, I am [ ]."
C2 subtlety
  • Ellipsis works only when the recovered words are identical to the earlier ones. If the form would change, keep the words: "She sings well and they dance well" cannot be ellipted to "and they well".
  • Over-eager ellipsis can leave a reader guessing. Drop only what is genuinely recoverable.

5. Lexical cohesion & formal reference (synonym / superordinate chains · the aforementioned / the said)

Beyond grammar, a text is bound together by its vocabulary. Instead of repeating a noun, a skilled writer varies it: a synonym, a superordinate (a wider category word), or a summarising label keeps the topic alive without monotony. In legal and bureaucratic writing, a heavier device — the aforementioned or the said — refers back with formal precision, but it clogs ordinary prose.

DeviceHow it linksExample (first → later mention)
synonyma near-equal worda car → the vehicle
superordinatea wider category worda rose → the flower
formal referenceprecise but heavy; legal registerthe protocol → the aforementioned protocol
C2 subtlety
  • A synonym chain must not shift the meaning: calling a "cottage" a "mansion" two lines later confuses rather than links.
  • Reserve the aforementioned / the said for contracts and notices. In normal prose a plain "it" or "this protocol" is lighter and clearer.

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the cohesion faults that surface even in fluent C2 writing. Each one is a failure of linking, not of grammar. Audit your own drafts against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
We changed supplier and system. This caused delays.We changed supplier and system. Both moves caused delays.a vague "this" points at two things at once — ambiguous reference.
We studied France and Spain; the former has warmer coasts. (meaning Spain)We studied France and Spain; the latter has warmer coasts."the former" = first-named (France); the writer meant the second, Spain.
The report says the report is late because the report lacked data.The report says it is late because it lacked data.over-repetition; reference ("it") streamlines the sentence.
I have three pens; give me the red ones. (only one is meant)I have three pens; give me the red one.number clash: singular referent needs "one", not "ones".
She wanted to help, and he wanted to. (help = do something different)She wanted to help, and he wanted to as well. (same help)ellipsis works only if the dropped words are truly recoverable and identical.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

A junior writer hands in a stiff, repetitive paragraph; a senior editor coaches the cohesion. Read it twice and watch how each fix swaps a repeated noun for a lighter link — and why each choice keeps the text clear.

J:I wrote: "The council rejected the plan. The council said the plan was too costly."Every noun repeated — correct, but clumsy and redundant.
E:Let the second "council" become "it", and let "the plan" become "it" too: "The council rejected the plan, saying it was too costly."Anaphoric reference removes the repetition without any loss.
J:Next line I had two options and wrote "the former" for the second one.A very common slip: "the former" is the first-named item.
E:Then it must be "the latter". The former is always the one you named first; reverse it and you reverse your meaning.Order words must match the order of mention.
J:And "The council will meet again and the council will vote"?Still repeating the subject unnecessarily.
E:Drop it: "The council will meet again and vote." Ellipsis. Trust the reader to carry the subject across.Nothing stands in — and the link is still perfectly clear.
Test yourself
  • Why is "saying it was too costly" better than repeating "the plan"? (Anaphoric "it" has one clear referent and removes redundant repetition.)
  • If you name "tea and coffee", which does "the latter" mean? (Coffee — the second-named of the two.)
  • Rewrite "He can code and she can code" using ellipsis. ("He can code and she can too" — the repeated verb is recoverable, so drop it.)

Speaking: Naming the Threads

Learn the vocabulary of cohesion, then use it to tell a short story or explain a process without stumbling over the same words.

cohesion (n)
the quality of a text's parts being clearly linked (bağdaşıklık, metin bütünlüğü)
Syn: connectedness · Ant: disjointedness
"Good cohesion lets a reader glide from sentence to sentence."
anaphoric (adj)
pointing back to something already mentioned (geriye gönderen, anaforik)
Syn: backward-referring · Ant: cataphoric
"In 'Ann left; she was tired,' 'she' is anaphoric."
cataphoric (adj)
pointing forward to something named later (ileriye gönderen, kataforik)
Syn: forward-referring · Ant: anaphoric
"'Note this: he never called' uses cataphoric 'this'."
referent (n)
the thing a word actually points to (gönderilen şey, merci)
Syn: target · Ant: —
"Every 'it' must have one clear referent."
antecedent (n)
the earlier word a pronoun refers back to (öncül)
Syn: forerunner · Ant: —
"'The dog' is the antecedent of 'it' in the next line."
substitution (n)
replacing a word with a shorter stand-in like 'one' (yerine koyma)
Syn: replacement · Ant: repetition
"'A red one' uses substitution for 'a red car'."
ellipsis (n)
leaving out words the reader can recover (eksiltme)
Syn: omission · Ant: repetition
"'Sara can swim; Tom can too' relies on ellipsis."
redundant (adj)
repeated needlessly; more than is required (gereksiz, fazlalık)
Syn: superfluous · Ant: essential
"Naming the client five times is redundant."
ambiguous (adj)
open to more than one interpretation (belirsiz, iki anlamlı)
Syn: unclear · Ant: unambiguous
"A vague 'this' leaves the sentence ambiguous."
cohesive (adj)
sticking together as a connected whole (bağdaşık, bağlı)
Syn: unified · Ant: disjointed
"Her prose is remarkably cohesive and easy to follow."
superordinate (n)
a general word that covers more specific ones (üst terim)
Syn: umbrella term · Ant: hyponym
"'Flower' is a superordinate of 'rose' and 'tulip'."
reiterate (v)
to state again, for linking or emphasis (yinelemek)
Syn: restate · Ant: omit
"He reiterated the key term to link two paragraphs."
streamlined (adj)
made smoother and more efficient (sadeleştirilmiş, akıcı)
Syn: sleek · Ant: cluttered
"Ellipsis streamlined the clumsy, repetitive draft."
Cohesion is a single thread drawn through a text: each device links a sentence to the last without naming it again.

The Invisible Thread

For thirty years, editors have sent their most tangled manuscripts to one desk: that of Halvard Sund, a reviser whose speciality is cohesion — the art of making a text hold together as a single, seamless thread. He writes nothing of his own; nonetheless, the books he touches read as though they had never been assembled from separate parts at all. His gift, colleagues say, is not a grand vocabulary but connection: knowing exactly which word should point back, which should be dropped, and which should quietly stand in for another.

His arrival at that desk was itself unhurried. Sund began, decades ago, as the most junior proof-corrector in a house now long absorbed into a larger one; there he learned to read not for meaning alone but for the joints between meanings, the places where one clause is fastened to the next. What his elders dismissed as mere tidying he came to regard as the whole of the craft. A sentence, he decided early, is rarely wrong in isolation; it fails in company, when it forgets what preceded it or leaves the sentence after it stranded. That conviction, patiently refined over a working life, is what editors now pay so dearly to borrow. They come to him not because their prose is ungrammatical — much of it is impeccable — but because it does not yet flow.

Consider a clumsy paragraph. A nervous author repeats "the committee" six times in four sentences; Sund replaces the second mention with "it", the third with "the body", and simply deletes the fourth, trusting the reader to supply it. The facts do not change; the reading, however, becomes effortless. "Repetition is not emphasis," he likes to say. "More often, it is a failure to trust the reader."

Such economies recur throughout his work. Faced with a paragraph in which a single idea is announced, then restated, then anxiously summarised, he will let the first statement stand and quietly strike the rest, allowing a pronoun or a well-placed "such" to carry the sense forward. A noun repeated for want of confidence becomes "it"; a whole clause, once the reader has grasped it, shrinks to "so" or vanishes altogether. The effect is paradoxical: by removing words he seems to add clarity, and by pointing backwards — with an "it", a "this", a "the same" — he binds the paragraph into a chain whose links the reader never consciously notices. A good thread, he observes, is invisible precisely because it holds.

What distinguishes his method is restraint. Where a weaker editor might scatter "this" and "that" freely, Sund insists every such word have one unmistakable referent. On the contrary, he warns, a vague "this" — pointing at nothing in particular — does more damage than the repetition it was meant to cure. Moreover, he despises the false elegance of "the aforementioned", reserving such formal reference for contracts, whereas ordinary prose, he argues, wants lighter threads.

He is equally exacting about the small machinery of reference. "The former" and "the latter", he cautions, are elegant only when a reader can instantly recover which is which; crossed or forgotten, they become a trap, and he will often rewrite them out entirely rather than risk the stumble. He prizes the superordinate — the eagle that becomes "the bird", the violin that becomes "the instrument" — because it varies a noun without deceiving anyone about what is meant. Substitution and ellipsis he treats as the same instinct at different strengths: in each, the writer trusts the reader to carry something forward unspoken. The difference between a clumsy text and a graceful one, he insists, is seldom the words chosen; far more often it is the links between them, the quiet grammar that decides whether a reader glides or stalls.

Colleagues who have watched him work describe something closer to listening than to editing. He reads a manuscript aloud, they say, under his breath, waiting for the sentence that makes the ear hesitate; where the voice trips, a thread has snapped. Younger editors, sent to observe, report that he changes astonishingly little — a pronoun here, a deletion there — and yet the transformation is total, as though a window long filmed with dust had been wiped clean. He resists praise for this. The writer did the work, he says; he merely removed the obstacles the writer could no longer see. Nevertheless, the manuscripts leave his desk unmistakably altered, and the authors, reading their own words returned to them, frequently cannot say what he has done.

An anecdote, often repeated in the trade, captures the method better than any manifesto could. A celebrated historian once submitted a chapter of which she was privately proud and quietly certain it could not be improved. Sund returned it a week later having added not a single word; he had removed eleven, redirected four wandering pronouns, and dissolved two sentences into the ones beside them. The historian, reading it through, found that an argument she had thought merely competent now seemed inevitable, each step leaning on the last. She could not, she admitted, locate the change. That, Sund is said to have replied, was the point: a repair the reader can see is a repair that has failed, for the thread should hold the eye forward, never draw it to the knot.

Not everyone reveres the discipline. A few authors have complained that his lightening of their prose strips it of a certain deliberate roughness, that the seams they left showing were meant to show. Sund is unmoved. Difficulty earned by an idea is worth preserving, he concedes; difficulty caused merely by a broken link is not, and the two are constantly confused. A reader who struggles should struggle with the thought, never with the machinery carrying it. On this distinction he will not compromise, and it is perhaps the nearest thing he has to a doctrine: clarity is not the enemy of depth but its servant.

His influence, once confined to a handful of publishing houses, has begun to spread by quieter means. Manuscripts he has revised are studied in workshops as models, though few readers can point to the specific repairs; the prose simply behaves. Marginal notes he scribbled over the years — terse, often gnomic — have been copied, traded and, to his mild embarrassment, revered. He himself keeps no archive and volunteers no theory, which only deepens the mystique. What survives of him, therefore, is not a book but a habit of attention, passed from one desk to the next like a trade secret that resists being written down. Those who have absorbed it say they can no longer read carelessly; every stray pronoun, every hammered noun, every dangling thread now announces itself, and the pleasure of a text that simply flows has become, for them, impossible to take for granted.

Meanwhile, a slim guide to his principles now circulates among younger editors. Notwithstanding its brevity, it has been called indispensable. It offers no rules, only a discipline: make every pronoun answer to one owner, cut what the reader can already hear, and let no thread dangle. Cohesion, Sund maintains, is therefore not decoration; it is trust made visible.

Writing: Repair the Thread

Take a repetitive, poorly-linked paragraph and rewrite it as one cohesive whole — then explain the devices you used. Your teacher will review it.

Connectors that carry a text (Linking Words)

Cohesion is not only reference and ellipsis; the right connector signals how one sentence relates to the last. At C2 you deploy formal connectors (nonetheless, notwithstanding, moreover, whereas, consequently, however) precisely, so the logical thread is as clear as the grammatical one. A well-placed connector saves the reader from repeating the whole prior idea.

ConnectorSignal it sendsExample
nonethelessconcession (still true despite)The draft repeated itself; nonetheless, the facts were sound.
whereascontrast between two itemsThe first version was clumsy, whereas the second flowed.
moreoveradding a further pointIt was repetitive. Moreover, its 'this' was vague.
consequentlyresultThe referent was unclear; consequently, readers stalled.
howevercounter-moveThe idea was good. However, the linking failed.
Rule
  • Use a connector to replace a repeated idea, not to decorate: "However" already carries "in contrast with what I just said".
  • Use at least 3 connectors from this table in your rewrite, each doing a real logical job.

How to structure the task (Repair & Reflect Structure)

You will take one repetitive source paragraph, rewrite it cohesively, then reflect on the devices you used. The facts stay fixed; only the linking changes. This is the purest test of cohesion: it forces you to prove you can bind a text without padding or repetition.

Sample Answer (~220 words) A. Cohesive rewrite
The startup launched an app to help commuters plan their journeys. Users liked it because it was fast; moreover, it soon gained a feature that let them share those journeys with one another. The trips quickly became social, and the company grew with them. The original hammered "app", "commuters" and "journeys" again and again, whereas the version above lets lighter threads do the work. Nonetheless, every fact survives intact.

B. Reflection
To avoid the redundant repetition, I leaned on several cohesive devices. The pronoun "it" supplies anaphoric reference back to the app, its clear referent, while "them" and "those journeys" point back without renaming. I used the superordinate "the tool" is implied through "users", a synonym for "commuters", giving lexical cohesion, and "the trips" varies "journeys". Ellipsis appears in "grew with them", where the repeated idea is dropped. The connectors "moreover", "whereas" and "nonetheless" carry the logic so I need not restate each prior point. Crucially, no pointer is ambiguous: every "it" and "them" has one owner. The result is streamlined and cohesive rather than a stack of identical nouns, and the reader now glides from fact to fact instead of stumbling over the same four words.
