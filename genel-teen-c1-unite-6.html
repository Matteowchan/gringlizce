Ünite 6 · Advanced Conditionals · Bölüm 1/7

Grammar: Advanced Conditionals

Stop reaching for "if". Learn the conjunctions that let you set terms, warn, and imagine the roads not taken.

Conditions, consequences, and the roads not taken

By C1 you can build all three basic conditionals accurately. The advanced skill is different: it is knowing that "if" is only the plainest of many ways to set a condition. Skilled writers reach instead for provided that, as long as, unless, on condition that, supposing and but for — each of which carries a slightly different tone, from the coldly contractual to the wistfully counterfactual.

This unit treats the conditional as a register tool. You will learn to state terms formally with provided that, to negate a condition cleanly with unless, to imagine an alternative history with but for and if it hadn't been for, to imply a whole "if" clause with otherwise or an imperative, and to blend time frames with the mixed conditional. Handled well, these choices make argument sound precise, controlled and unmistakably C1.

  • You may join the trip provided that every form is signed.provided that → a formal, contractual condition (= only if).
  • The coach leaves on time unless the fog thickens.unless → a single word meaning "if... not".
  • But for your warning, we would have lost everything.but for + noun → a counterfactual (= if it hadn't been for).
  • Back up the file now; otherwise, you'll lose a week's coursework.otherwise → an implied condition (= if you don't).

1. Beyond "if" (provided that, as long as, on condition that)

A cluster of conjunctions state a positive condition more precisely — and more formally — than "if". They all mean roughly "only if this one requirement is met", and they belong to careful, often written English: school rules, permission slips, debates and academic essays.

ConjunctionRegister & nuanceExample
provided / providing (that)formal; emphasises a required conditionYou'll get extra credit provided that you cite your sources.
as long as / so long asneutral to informal; "on the single condition that"I'll wait as long as you promise not to be late again.
on condition thatvery formal; states an explicit term or clauseHe kept his place on the team on condition that he train every week.
Register & nuance
  • These conjunctions take a present tense for a future condition — never will: "...provided that you arrive on time." ✓ — not "...provided that you will arrive." ✗
  • They are stronger than "if": they foreground the requirement, signalling "this and only this makes it possible."

2. unless — the trap of "if not"

Unless means "except if" or "if... not" in a single word. It introduces the one circumstance that would reverse the main outcome. It is elegant and common, but it hides a classic C1 trap: because unless already contains a negative, you must not add a second one.

SentenceMeaningNote
We'll eat outside unless it rains.= if it does not rain, we'll eat outsiderain is the one thing that would change the plan
Don't call me unless it's urgent.= call me only if it is urgent"unless" isolates the single exception
I won't submit unless my teammate approves.= I'll submit only after my teammate approvesthe negative is already inside "unless"
C1 subtlety — no double negative
  • Don't add a second "not": "Unless you don't hurry, we'll be late." ✗ → "Unless you hurry, we'll be late." ✓
  • unless is not a perfect swap for if not in unreal/hypothetical statements: prefer if... not there ("If I weren't tired, I'd come", not "unless I were tired").

3. Counterfactuals (but for / if it hadn't been for)

To imagine an alternative history — what would (or would not) have happened without one decisive factor — English uses but for + noun and its longer relatives if it weren't for / if it hadn't been for + noun. These are the grammar of near-misses, gratitude and blame. The critical rule: but for is followed by a noun or noun phrase, never a full clause.

StructureTime referenceExample
but for + nounpresent or past unreal; formalBut for the traffic, we would have arrived by six.
if it weren't for + nounpresent/general unreal situationIf it weren't for her support, I'd give up now.
if it hadn't been for + nounpast unreal situationIf it hadn't been for the smoke alarm, the fire would have spread.
C1 subtlety — noun, not clause
  • Follow but for with a noun: "But for your help..." ✓ — not "But for you helped me..." ✗
  • The formal inverted form drops "if": "Had it not been for the smoke alarm, the fire would have spread." (very C1)

4. Implied conditionals (otherwise, in that case, imperative + and/or)

Sometimes the whole "if" clause is left unspoken, because context makes it obvious. English has neat devices for this: otherwise (= if not), in that case (= if that is so), and the punchy imperative + and / or pattern that packs a whole conditional into a warning or a promise.

DeviceIt means...Example
otherwise"if you don't / if that isn't so"Leave now; otherwise you'll miss the connection.
in that case"if that is true, then...""It's fully booked." "In that case, we'll drive."
imperative + and"if you do this, a good result follows"Trust the process and the results will come.
imperative + or"if you don't, a bad result follows"Hurry up, or we'll miss the last train.
C1 subtlety
  • and promises a reward; or threatens a penalty: "Apologise, and she'll forgive you" vs "Apologise, or you'll lose her."
  • otherwise usually opens a second clause after a semicolon or full stop and often takes a comma when it starts a sentence.

5. supposing / suppose & mixed conditionals (imagining & blending time)

Supposing (or suppose) opens a hypothetical — it invites the listener to picture a situation, much like "what if". Separately, the mixed conditional lets one clause sit in the past while the other sits in the present, so a past cause can be linked to a present result (or vice versa).

StructureFunctionExample
supposing / suppose (that)raises a hypothetical for discussionSupposing the exchange fell through, what would we do?
past cause → present result"if" + past perfect, then "would" + baseIf she had studied medicine, she would be a doctor now.
present cause → past result"if" + past simple, then "would have" + V3If he weren't so proud, he would have apologised yesterday.
C1 subtlety
  • A word like now / today after a past-perfect "if" clause signals a mixed conditional: "If we had left earlier, we would be home by now."
  • Like provided that, supposing does not take will: "Supposing it rained..." ✓ — not "Supposing it will rain..." ✗

6. Common mistakes (✗ / ✓)

These are the errors that still surface in otherwise strong C1 writing. Each one is a conditional slip — a wrong conjunction, a wrong form, or a smuggled second negative. Audit your own writing against every row.

Wrong ✗Right ✓Why?
Unless you don't book early, you'll miss out.Unless you book early, you'll miss out."unless" already means "if not"; a second negative reverses it.
But for you warned me, I would have missed the deadline.But for your warning, I would have missed the deadline."but for" takes a noun phrase, not a full clause.
I'll help provided that you will share your notes.I'll help provided that you share your notes."provided that" takes the present, not "will", for a future condition.
Supposing it will snow, would you still come?Supposing it snowed, would you still come?"supposing" opens a hypothetical, so use the past, not "will".
If I had taken the scholarship, I would have been abroad today.If I had taken the scholarship, I would be abroad today."today" needs a present result: a mixed conditional, not the third.

7. Putting it together (Model Dialogue)

Two students discuss whether the inter-school debate final will go ahead. Read it twice and watch the conditional system work: a formal condition with as long as, a clean negation with unless, a counterfactual with but for, a hypothetical with supposing (blended into a mixed conditional), and an implied condition with otherwise.

A:So, is the debate final actually going ahead?A simple opening question.
B:It will, as long as the head teacher approves the funding by Friday.as long as → a single, decisive condition.
A:And if she doesn't?Ellipsis: the rest of the clause is understood.
B:Unless she signs off, the whole event collapses.unless → "if she does not sign off".
A:Honestly, but for your rewrites, the judges would have dropped us weeks ago.but for + noun → a counterfactual near-miss.
B:Supposing our notes had been wrong, we'd all be watching from the audience right now — so get the form signed, otherwise we lose our slot.supposing + mixed conditional, then otherwise = "if you don't".
Test yourself
  • Why "as long as" rather than "if"? (It foregrounds the one condition — the head's approval by Friday — on which everything hangs.)
  • Rewrite "but for your rewrites" without "but for". (If it hadn't been for your rewrites... / Had it not been for your rewrites...)
  • Why "we'd all be watching from the audience right now" and not "we'd all have been watching"? (A past cause — "had been wrong" — with a present result, "right now": a mixed conditional.)

Speaking: Talking Through Conditions & Consequences

Learn the vocabulary of terms, risks and "what ifs", then use the unit's conditionals to talk through a decision of your own.

contingent (adj)
depending on something else to happen first (bağlı, koşullu)
Syn: dependent · Ant: guaranteed
"Your place on the team is contingent on decent grades."
hypothetical (adj)
based on an imagined, not a real, situation (varsayımsal)
Syn: theoretical · Ant: actual
"Let's take a purely hypothetical case for a moment."
prerequisite (n)
something required before something else can happen (ön koşul)
Syn: precondition · Ant: afterthought
"Trust is a prerequisite for any lasting friendship."
stipulate (v)
to state clearly as a required condition (şart koşmak)
Syn: specify · Ant: waive
"The rules stipulate that entries be submitted by Friday."
hinge on (phr v)
to depend entirely on one key factor (bir şeye bağlı olmak)
Syn: depend on · Ant: ignore
"The whole plan hinges on the weather staying dry."
counterfactual (adj)
imagining what would have happened otherwise (karşıolgusal)
Syn: hypothetical · Ant: factual
"Historians love a counterfactual: what if Rome had never fallen?"
provision (n)
a condition or clause within an agreement (hüküm, şart)
Syn: clause · Ant: omission
"A special provision lets you drop your lowest grade."
caveat (n)
a warning or condition added to a statement (uyarı, kayıt/şerh)
Syn: proviso · Ant: assurance
"I'll join, with one caveat: I have to leave by nine."
inevitable (adj)
certain to happen and impossible to avoid (kaçınılmaz)
Syn: unavoidable · Ant: avoidable
"Without revision, a low grade seemed all but inevitable."
avert (v)
to prevent something bad from happening (önlemek, savuşturmak)
Syn: prevent · Ant: trigger
"A last-minute check averted an embarrassing mistake in the essay."
jeopardise (v)
to put something valuable at risk (tehlikeye atmak)
Syn: endanger · Ant: safeguard
"One careless comment could jeopardise the whole group project."
forestall (v)
to prevent by acting before it can happen (önünü almak)
Syn: pre-empt · Ant: allow
"She revised early to forestall any last-minute panic."
conceivable (adj)
possible to imagine or believe (düşünülebilir, olası)
Syn: imaginable · Ant: unthinkable
"It's conceivable that the match could still be postponed."
proviso (n)
a condition attached to an agreement (şart, kayıt)
Syn: stipulation · Ant: guarantee
"She accepted, with the proviso that she could leave early."
Every conditional sets a fork in the road: the conjunction you choose tells the listener which branch depends on what.

Reading: But for One Warning

Read the feature, then work through the five tasks.

Before You Read (Okumadan Önce)

Skilled readers prepare before they start. Before you read, think about these three questions (you do not need to write the answers):

  • Think of a disaster that was narrowly avoided. What was the single condition — a warning, a delay, a decision — that changed the outcome?
  • The text is about a school field trip that nearly ended in disaster. Which conditional forms would you expect a writer to use to imagine the alternative outcome?
  • Watch for counterfactuals ("but for", "had it not been for") and for conditions stated as terms ("provided that", "as long as", "unless"). Ask what each one is doing.
How to read
  • First read: read quickly for the whole arc — what nearly happened, and what one person did to prevent it. Don't stop for every word.
  • Second read: track the conditional forms — a counterfactual ("But for a single text message"), a stated term ("as long as the group returned before the river rose"), a negation ("Unless she could prove..."), and a mixed conditional at the end. Ask what each one adds.

Words to spot (from the Speaking section): contingent · hinge on · caveat · inevitable · avert · jeopardise · counterfactual · provision

One decision on a stormy afternoon stood between the class and a flooded path home.
But for One Warning

But for a single text message one October afternoon, the class of 9B would almost certainly never have made it back across the valley. Since that story is rarely retold, few students realise how much once hinged on one condition being met. Had it not been for a quiet Year 10 student named Petra Kovac, the whole class would have been stranded on the far bank as the light failed, with the only path home already under water.

To grasp quite how narrow the escape had been, it helps to picture the project that had drawn 9B into the valley in the first place. For the previous fortnight the class had been working on a geography assignment about river systems, mapping how the stream carved its way between two steep ridges and sketching the line of stepping stones that generations of walkers had used as a shortcut home. Those stones were the only dry crossing for miles. As long as the water stayed low, they were an easy hop from bank to bank; the instant the level rose, however, they disappeared beneath a churning ribbon of brown. Petra had joined the school barely a month before the trip, and while her classmates treated the valley as familiar ground, she read it the way a stranger reads a warning sign — slowly, and without the false comfort of habit.

Kovac had noticed the water creeping up the stepping stones during a routine break. Her teachers, however, were dismissive. The crossing was perfectly safe, they insisted, as long as the group returned before the river rose. Whereas she saw a current already turning brown and fast, they saw only an anxious newcomer. Unless she could prove there was real danger, they would not cut the trip short; provided that the afternoon stayed dry, they argued, there was nothing whatever to fear.

The disagreement that followed was not a loud one. Petra was new, softly spoken, and reluctant to contradict adults she had known for only a handful of weeks; the teachers, for their part, had led the same walk a dozen times without a single mishap. To them, her worry looked like the ordinary nervousness of a pupil who did not yet know the route. She tried to describe exactly what she was seeing — the way the lowest stones had already slipped out of sight, the way snapped twigs now raced past instead of drifting lazily — but one anxious teenager, they decided, was hardly reason to abandon a lesson that half the class was still enjoying. So the group pressed on upstream, notebooks open and pencils busy, entirely unaware that the safe margin they were relying on was quietly vanishing behind them.

That afternoon, nonetheless, brought the heaviest rain the region had seen in years. Moreover, the class had been allowed to wander far upstream all day. As the light began to fade, Kovac made the decision that would define the trip. "Turn back now," she told the group leader, "otherwise we lose the path completely." Supposing she had said nothing, or waited for a permission that might never have come, the consequences would have been unthinkable; yet she did not hesitate.

Everything now turned on how the others chose to respond. Had the group leader waved her away a second time, the class might well have been arguing on the bank when the last of the stones finally went under. Instead, something in Petra's calm insistence carried more weight than her age or her newness. A single glance at the river — swollen, muddy and unmistakably higher than it had been an hour earlier — was enough to end the debate. The leader called the class together and ordered the retreat on the spot. Rucksacks were hauled onto shoulders, the slower walkers were moved to the front where they could set the pace, and the whole line began to hurry back down the valley faster than it had travelled all day.

The class scrambled across just as the water reached the top of the stones. But for her insistence, the group would have been cut off within the hour; consequently, thirty students reached home that night having never known how close they had come. Kovac was told off for alarming the others and, notwithstanding that telling-off, was quietly thanked by the same teachers weeks later. "If I had trusted the schedule instead of the river," she once remarked, "I wouldn't be able to sleep today." The class owes its safe return to a warning that, but for one stubborn student, would never have been given.

In the weeks that followed, the near-disaster became the most discussed event in the whole school. At the next assembly the head teacher retold the story — tactfully leaving out the earlier refusal to listen — and used it to announce a new rule: no river crossing on any trip unless a teacher had first checked the water against a marked post on the bank. The geography department, meanwhile, quietly rewrote the very assignment that had caused the trouble, turning it into a study of flood risk in which pupils had to argue, with proper evidence, exactly when a familiar route stops being safe. What had begun as a homework task about maps had grown, almost overnight, into a lesson about judgement.

For the students of 9B, the deeper lesson proved harder to put into words. Most of them had spent that afternoon exactly as they always did — half-listening, trusting that the adults in charge had already thought of everything. It had simply never occurred to them that the quietest, newest member of the class might be the one paying the closest attention. If the day taught them anything, it was that feeling sure of a route is not at all the same as being safe on it, and that, on the rare occasion when instinct and the timetable flatly disagree, it can be worth the discomfort of speaking up. Petra herself refused to be called a hero, insisting that she had only pointed out the obvious. Even so, everyone who crossed those stones understood that the obvious, on that particular afternoon, had come within a whisker of going unspoken.

Writing: A Counterfactual Account

Write and submit a controlled "what if" account; your teacher will review it.

Connectors that sound C1 (Linking Words)

At B2 you linked ideas with however, therefore, although, whereas. At C1 you gain a sharper, more formal register with connectors such as moreover, consequently, notwithstanding, whereas and nonetheless. Used precisely — and sparingly — they signal a writer in full command of tone, and they pair naturally with the conditional forms of this unit.

ConnectorUse it to...Example
moreoveradd a stronger, further pointThe path was long; moreover, the river was rising fast.
consequentlystate a formal result or effectShe warned the group in time; consequently, everyone got across.
notwithstandingconcede "in spite of" (very formal)Notwithstanding the telling-off, she was later thanked.
whereasdraw a direct contrast between two ideasShe saw real danger, whereas they saw a nervous newcomer.
nonethelesssay "even so", after a concessionThe schedule was clear; nonetheless, she urged them to turn back.
howevercontrast between two sentencesThe trip seemed safe. However, one condition failed.

See the difference — the same events, written two ways:

Flat narration ✗Controlled C1 narration ✓
The path was long. It rained a lot. A student warned the group to turn back. If she hadn't, they would have been trapped. She was told off but then thanked. The river was low at dawn; moreover, the sky was already darkening. But for one student's insistence, the class would have been cut off; consequently, thirty students got home safely. Notwithstanding a telling-off, she was later thanked.
Rule
  • Use at least 3 different C1 connectors from the table. Weave your account; don't list sentences.
  • Moreover, Consequently, However, Nonetheless usually open a sentence and take a comma: "Consequently, the plan was abandoned."

How to structure a counterfactual account (Structure)

A strong C1 "what if" account is not a flat list of causes. It manages conditions deliberately: it sets the scene and the stakes, states the crucial condition, imagines the alternative outcome, and reflects on what hinged on it. The skeleton stays the same — you change the content, not the shape:

PartWhat to writeConditional form to reach for
1. Orientationset the scene and why it still matterspresent perfect + "hinge on / contingent on"
2. The conditionstate the term everything depended onprovided that / as long as / unless
3. The turning pointimagine the alternative outcomebut for / had it not been for + noun
4. Reflectionjudge what hinged on the decision nowmixed conditional + retrospective comment

Sample Answer (~205 words) But for a single form I almost never handed in, my school years would have taken an entirely different shape. Everything, I now realise, hinged on one condition being met: a place on the exchange programme that was contingent on my applying before a deadline I had all but forgotten.

My parents were sceptical. As long as I stayed at my old school, they argued, my future was safe; provided that I worked steadily, nothing could go wrong. Whereas they saw security, however, I saw a chance slipping away. Unless I applied that night, the place would be gone forever, so I sent the form just before midnight.

Had it not been for a kind teacher who spotted my late application, it would have been rejected on a technicality. Moreover, the panel very nearly cancelled the whole programme that year. One quick phone call averted the disaster; consequently, I was accepted.

Looking back, the outcome seems almost inevitable, yet it was anything but. If I had listened to the caution instead of the deadline, I would be a different person today. Notwithstanding all the doubt, that reckless form remains the hinge on which everything I now value quietly turned.
